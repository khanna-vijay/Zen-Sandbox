PowerPoint Karaoke

Everything you need to run this with a group. About 4 minutes to read.

What the game is

One person stands up and gives a completely confident presentation over a slide deck they have never seen before. They do not know the next image until it appears. The slides keep advancing. They keep talking.

That is the entire game. The comedy is not in the pictures — it is in watching somebody explain an absurd image with total institutional conviction, as though it obviously belongs in their talk.

The rule that makes it work: never break, never apologise, never say "I don't know what this is". The performer's job is to make the slide make sense.

Why it is worth playing

It is a comedy game, but it trains a specific and unusually transferable set of skills:

  • Spontaneity under observation. You cannot pre-plan. The next slide is genuinely unknown, so the brain has to stop drafting and start responding. This is the single hardest habit to unlearn in public speaking.
  • Justification. Improv's core muscle: take whatever you are given and make it deliberate. Every slide is an unwanted offer you must accept and integrate — "yes, and" against a picture instead of a partner.
  • Recovering out loud. Players learn that a stumble is survivable, because the deck does not wait and the audience does not mind. That confidence carries directly into real meetings and real talks.
  • Structure without a script. The performer is told the deck's shape — problem, insight, plan, ask — so they practise landing a narrative arc while improvising all of the content.
  • Status and commitment. Playing a high-status expert who is completely wrong is a safe, funny way to practise vocal authority.
  • Listening to yourself. Callbacks only happen if you remember what you said three slides ago. It builds working memory for your own material.

Useful for improv classes, presentation-skills training, team offsites, onboarding icebreakers and conference warm-ups.

The rules

  1. You have never seen the deck. Nobody shows it to you, and you do not get to look first. That is not a handicap, it is the game.
  2. Every slide is yours. You made it. You chose it. You put it in this talk on purpose. Explain it that way.
  3. Never break. No apologising, no "I have no idea what this is", no laughing at your own deck, no asking the room what they think it means. Breaking is the only way to actually lose.
  4. Never describe the picture. "This is a photo of a horse" is nothing. "Q3 is the horse" is the game.
  5. Keep the thread. Each slide has to connect to the one before it, however absurd the join. You are giving one talk, not narrating ten unrelated images.
  6. Land the ending. The deck finishes on a Thank You card. Finish on an ask, take questions in character, and stay in character for those too.
  7. Audience rule: laugh, do not heckle, and never tell the performer what a slide "really" is.

The whole game in one line: the slide is never wrong, only your explanation is missing.

How to play — the short version

  1. Press SPIN THE WHEEL. It lands on a talk format and deals a deck behind the scenes.
  2. The card shows two things: the format, and the shape the talk follows. Read the shape out to the performer — it is the only help they get, and it is what stops the talk becoming a list.
  3. Want a premise as well? Press S for a scenario — who they are and what the occasion is — and read it aloud. Optional; plenty of groups prefer to shout one out instead.
  4. Press ▶ Start presenting. Go fullscreen with F.
  5. Advance with →. Either drive it yourself every 20–30 seconds, or let the performer say "next slide" when they run dry. Driving it yourself is harder and funnier.
  6. At the Thank You card, take two questions in character, then Esc and SPIN AGAIN for the next player.

New group? Leave Slide assistant text ticked for the first few rounds — the corner cue tells the performer what each slide is for and stops anyone freezing. Untick it once they have the hang of it.

Spinning again

There is only one button, and it does everything. Every spin re-rolls the format, the premise and all of the slides together. Spin as many times as you like — landing on the same format twice still deals a completely different deck.

The app also remembers what it has already shown you, in two ways. Slides used in the last eight decks are held back from the next draw, so the same image does not turn up two rounds running. And the draw actively favours whichever images have been shown least, so the library gets used evenly instead of the same few pictures doing all the work.

In practice: a twelve-performer class sees about 129 images and, on average, half of one repeat. Every image in the library gets its turn. Both behaviours relax automatically rather than ever failing to deal a deck, and both switch off if you type a seed — a seed has to reproduce its deck exactly.

Nobody previews the deck — not even the facilitator. There is no thumbnail view on this screen on purpose. You cannot spoil a slide you have not seen, and the person running the laptop reacting honestly is half the fun for the room.

Group sizes and timing

  • Solo turn: 8–12 slides, roughly 3–5 minutes.
  • A class of 12: about 60–75 minutes including laughs and notes.
  • Tag-team variant: two performers, swap at every third slide. The incoming player must continue the outgoing player's argument.
  • Expert-and-interpreter variant: one performer talks, a second "translates" for the audience. Doubles the justification pressure.
  • Q&A variant: after the last slide, the audience asks two questions in character. This is where the best material usually is.

What is on the screen

The opening card — page 1 of every deck. It shows only the words Presenter Name, Accomplishments & Credentials, Venue and Topic over blank lines. Nothing is filled in. The performer invents and says all four out loud. It gives them a running start before the first real image lands, and it is the same card in every format.

The corner cue — one or two small words in the bottom-right of each slide. It tells the performer what this slide is for, never what it shows.

Each format speaks its own language. The same slot is Findings in a PhD defence, The Numbers in a board room, Intel in a mission brief, Scripture in a keynote and Specifications in a product launch. A Shark Tank pitch runs The Hook → The Problem → But Wait → Relief → The Offer; the Disney spine runs Once Upon A Time → And Every Day → Until One Day → Because Of That. So the cue is a genuine prompt in the voice of the talk, not a generic label.

It is on by default. Untick Slide assistant text on the spinner screen to hide it — along with the on-screen key reminders — once players are confident enough to work without a safety net. Running with it off is the harder, purer version of the game. Press B while presenting for the facilitator's fuller overlay, which shows the cue, the underlying beat and the line that links it to the slide before.

The closing card — the last page of every deck says Thank You for your Valuable Time over an image the deck never used, so the performer has to sign off in front of a picture they never got to explain. Do not explain it. Take questions in character instead.

Settings (⚙)

  • Format — pick a talk style directly instead of spinning.
  • Deck length — 8 to 12 slides. Shorter is kinder; longer forces a real ending rather than a sprint.
  • Scenario — choose a specific premise, pick Random, or No scenario to have the audience shout one out instead. Choosing one by hand pins it, so later spins stop re-rolling it.
  • Seed — type any number to reproduce the exact same deck later. Use it to run the same deck with three different performers, which is the best teaching exercise in the whole game. A typed seed also switches off the session cooldown, so the deck it names is exactly the deck you get.
  • Session variety — how many slides are currently being held back. Reset session history clears it, which is what you want when a new group takes over the laptop.
  • Apply & rebuild deck — commits your changes.

Sample script

Format: TED Talk. Scenario: a productivity guru who has never had a job. This is what committing looks like:

Opening card “Good evening. My name is Dr. Anand Vale. I hold three certifications, two of which I designed myself. We're at the Riverside Innovation Pavilion, and tonight I want to talk about doing less, harder.”

Prelude “This is the logo of my methodology. Yes, it is a shape. That was deliberate.”

Context “Here is the modern workplace. Look at it. Look at how much of it there is. We have never had more surface area and less depth.”

Personal Story “In 2019 I sat in a car park for four hours. Not waiting for anyone. I want you to understand that those were the most productive four hours of my life.”

Problem “And this — this — is what happens when you ignore that. I don't have to explain this image. You already feel it.”

Insight “Then it hit me. The car park was never empty. I was empty. And an empty vessel, ladies and gentlemen, is the only kind you can fill.”

Call to Action “So I'm not asking you to work differently. I'm asking you to go and sit in a car park. Tomorrow. Tell no one. Thank you.”

Notice what the performer never does: describe the picture as a picture, ask what it is, or laugh at it. Every image is treated as a slide they chose.

Coaching notes

  • Commit before you understand. Start the sentence, then find out where it is going. Hesitating is the only real failure.
  • Name it, then justify it. Say what the thing is with total certainty, then explain why it belongs in your argument.
  • Be specific. "A number went up" is nothing. "Q3 in the Baltic region" is funny. Invent proper nouns freely.
  • Call back. Reusing something from slide 2 on slide 9 gets the biggest laugh available and costs nothing.
  • Land the ask. Finish on a demand of the audience, not a summary. "In conclusion…" kills the ending; "So here is what I need from you" saves it.
  • Facilitators: praise commitment over cleverness. The nervous player who kept going did the exercise correctly.

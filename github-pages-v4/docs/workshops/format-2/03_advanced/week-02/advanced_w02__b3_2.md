# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Discard Twice

![Discard Twice](../img/advanced_w02__b3_2.jpg){ .infographic }

> In pairs, players say the two obvious answers out loud, flick them away, and hand their partner the third one — the specific one.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 02 run-sheet](../week-02.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 2 opens the suggestion. Last week the group learned to accept and build; now they need material worth building on. This five minutes puts the A-to-C move in the mouth before it goes into a scene. Everything after this block — monologue mining, opening forms, first-beat initiations — assumes players can get past the first two associations without stalling. Discard Twice makes that skip physical and fast, so by the time you ask for a scene from 'library' nobody offers a shushing librarian.

## What students actually learn

- That the first two answers arrive whether you want them or not, and that saying them out loud is quicker than suppressing them.
- That specific beats clever — 'the chair with one short leg' plays better than a surreal swerve.
- That their own lived detail is available as material and nobody needs to invent from nothing.
- That a partner can hold their offer accurately, which lowers the cost of being specific.

## Setup

Open floor, people standing in pairs, scattered so voices don't collide. No chairs, no props. Have four or five plain nouns ready in your head to seed anyone who freezes: kitchen, bus, hospital, birthday, garage. Know your demo answer before you start — the demo fails if you visibly hunt for the third.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Pair everyone up. Explain the palm-and-flick: two obvious associations flicked away, then a third that is concrete and small. Demo once with a volunteer on 'wedding'. Name that the third is specific, not surreal. |
| 1 | Round one. A feeds a plain noun, B discards twice and lands the third. Swap roles straight away and keep cycling with new nouns. Push the pace — no deliberating. |
| 1 | Call a partner change; everyone moves. Start round two with the new rule: the third must be something the speaker has actually seen or touched. First-hand only. |
| 1 | Round two continues. Side-coach for sensory detail and for the ones who are inventing rather than remembering. Keep both partners feeding and answering. |
| 1 | Round three, same pairs. The listener repeats the third back word for word before feeding the next noun. Then stop, and tell everyone to hold on to one third they heard. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As round one starts | *"Say the obvious one out loud. Don't hide it."* |
| When pairs slow down deliberating | *"Flick, flick, land. Don't shop for it."* |
| When thirds start getting weird or jokey | *"Smaller and truer. Not stranger."* |
| Entering round two | *"Only what you've actually seen. First-hand."* |
| Mid round two, if answers stay generic | *"Give me the detail nobody else has."* |
| Round three, on the echo | *"Repeat it back exactly. Their words, not yours."* |
| Last fifteen seconds | *"One more each, then hold your favourite."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is loud and quick. You hear the two obvious answers said flatly and fast, a physical flick, then a pause of half a second and something oddly precise — a smell, a piece of furniture, a time of day. Partners react to the thirds with recognition rather than laughter. In round three the echoes come back accurate. Nobody is standing silent trying to be interesting.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Long silences before the third; players staring at the ceiling. | They're auditioning the third for quality instead of reporting a memory. | Call 'first thing you can see, not the best thing'. Drop the pace expectation to one exchange every five seconds and let bad thirds through. |
| Thirds that are surreal — 'a wedding where everyone's a fish'. | They've heard 'unexpected' and read it as 'absurd'. Common in week two. | Re-demo in four seconds with a mundane third. Say plainly: the third is more ordinary than the first two, not less. |
| One person in each pair doing all the answering. | Swaps aren't happening because nobody is timing them. | Call the swap yourself every twenty seconds in round one. Say 'switch' out loud rather than trusting pairs to self-manage. |
| In round three, echoes are paraphrases. | The listener was preparing their next noun instead of listening. | Say 'exact words'. Ask one pair to redo it in front of the others for four seconds so the standard is visible. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Straight pairs, one trio if odd. Demo with a volunteer as written. Round one runs the full minute with you calling swaps every twenty seconds. You can hear most pairs, so side-coach individuals by name. |
| **14–17** | Straight pairs, spread them to the edges of the room so voices don't overlap. Skip the individual side-coaching and call to the whole room only. Keep the partner change but expect ten seconds of shuffle — start round two while stragglers pair up. |
| **18–20** | Pairs only; convert any trio into two pairs by joining a spare to you. Cut the demo to twenty seconds — one noun, two flicks, one third, no explanation. Use a clap rather than voice for swaps and the partner change, or you will not be heard. |

## Variations

**Discard Once** — Only one obvious answer is flicked away before the second one lands. Drop the first-hand rule entirely.  
*Easier — for a nervous group or a first exposure. Removes the pressure of a genuinely third-tier answer while keeping the physical flick.*

**Discard Three** — Flick away three associations. The fourth must be first-hand and must include a texture, a smell or a sound.  
*Harder — forces players well past their stock material and into actual recall. Only use with a group that got fluent in round two.*

**Build From The Third** — After the third lands, the partner adds one true sentence about it, then flicks twice from that and lands a new third.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from generating specifics to chaining them, which is closer to what an opening form actually asks for.*

## Debrief

1. **What happened in your mouth when you had to say the obvious answer out loud?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says it stopped nagging at them, or that they hadn't realised how loudly the obvious one was sitting there until they got rid of it. Move on once one person names the relief.

2. **What third did you keep hold of, and whose was it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Three or four concrete details, spoken quickly, with the owner's name attached. If people are describing the idea rather than repeating the words, ask for the exact phrase and then move on.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact — the flick is off your own palm, not anyone else's. If someone would rather not move on the partner change, let them stay and bring a new partner to them. First-hand memories can land close to home; say before round two that anything private stays private and a boring true answer is a correct answer. Anyone who prefers not to speak can feed nouns for both rounds and take the listener role in round three. Standing is not required; a seated pair works identically.

## Why it works

Framework principle D4.S3: a suggestion's first associations are shared by everyone in the room, so playing them produces scenes the audience has already imagined. The mechanism here is externalisation. Suppressing the obvious answer costs attention and it keeps returning; saying it aloud and physically discarding it clears the channel and marks the move as deliberate rather than as failure. The palm flick gives the discard a body, so under pressure in a scene the player has a motor memory to reach for, not just an instruction. The first-hand rule in round two blocks the escape route of inventing something surreal, which is just a different kind of first association. The round-three echo proves the third was heard, which is the evidence players need to keep risking specificity.

# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Ten Years Later

![Ten Years Later](../img/advanced_w04__b3_2.jpg){ .infographic }

> Pairs make one small gesture and line, then meet it again after time jumps — same beat, later and truer.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 04 run-sheet](../week-04.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 4 is the Harold. They already know a beat can return; they do not yet trust that a return needs no new material. This puts the whole return mechanism in sixty seconds of gesture, so that when the Harold's second beats arrive later in the session, nobody reaches for a fresh idea.

## What students actually learn

- That repeating the same thing later is not a failure of invention.
- That weight comes from what has happened between, not from playing it bigger.
- That watching your partner's return teaches you more than performing your own.

## Setup

Clear floor. Everyone standing in loose pairs, facing each other, roughly an arm's length apart, spread around the room. No chairs, no props. You need a voice that carries over twenty people talking at once — stand where you can be heard from the middle.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Demonstrate one beat yourself: a small gesture, four words or fewer. Pair them up. A offers, B copies exactly, swap. Keep it moving — do not let them workshop the idea. |
| 1 | Tell them the gesture and line are now fixed for the rest of the exercise. On your count, everyone performs their own beat once, simultaneously. Let the noise happen. |
| 1 | Call 'ten minutes later'. A goes, B watches, then B goes, A watches. Same words, same gesture. Repeat the call once more if the room finishes early. |
| 1 | Call 'ten years later'. Same order, same watching. Side-coach against volume and against added business. Nothing new enters — only time. |
| 1 | Call 'last time ever'. Each replays once, then holds still, looking at their partner. Three seconds of silence. Then hands up: two answers on what changed. Close it. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| During pairing, if ideas get elaborate | *"Smaller. Something you'd do without thinking."* |
| On the first simultaneous pass | *"Flat is fine. This is the before."* |
| On 'ten minutes later' | *"Same words. Only the ten minutes is new."* |
| When someone starts acting the years out | *"Let it sit. Don't play old."* |
| When volume creeps up | *"Quieter, not bigger."* |
| Between goes, if they look away | *"Watch your partner. That's half of it."* |
| On 'last time ever' | *"Then stop. Hold. Look at them."* |
| Closing the hands-up | *"Two answers. Don't explain them."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room goes quieter as it goes on, not louder. The same four words land differently the fourth time. Somebody's hand goes up straight away at the end, and their answer is about their partner, not themselves. Some pairs get nothing much from it and still finish on time — that is acceptable.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| People changing their line each round, or adding words. | They think repetition means they have run out of ideas. | Stop the room for five seconds. Say the words are locked. Restate that the only new element is elapsed time, then restart the call. |
| Everyone playing 'ten years later' as decrepit and slow. | They are illustrating age rather than living with a beat. | Call 'not older, just later'. Ask them to keep the same speed and change nothing but where they are looking. |
| Pairs finish each round in four seconds and stand about. | Gestures too small to sustain, or no watching between goes. | Insist on the watch: A goes, B watches, B goes, A watches. Repeat the call a second time within the minute. |
| Giggling that never settles. | Sincerity feels exposed this early in a session. | Do not fight it. Let the giggle run through 'ten minutes later', then drop your own volume on 'ten years later'. The room usually follows. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One round of each call is enough. You will have time to repeat 'ten years later' a second time — use it. With an odd number, take a partner yourself. |
| **14–17** | Run exactly as written. Give the calls from the centre of the room so both halves hear the same beat. No repeats; the timing is tight. |
| **18–20** | Skip the demonstration line and show one gesture wordlessly instead, to save fifteen seconds. Give each call once only. Expect two or three pairs to be off-pace; do not chase them. |

## Variations

**Given beat** — You supply the gesture and line to the whole room — everyone wipes a counter and says 'nearly closing'. Only the time jumps differ.  
*Easier. Removes the invention step entirely, so nervous groups start on the actual skill.*

**Backwards** — Run the calls in reverse: 'last time ever', then 'ten years earlier', then 'ten minutes earlier', then now.  
*Harder. They must build back toward ordinariness, which resists the urge to escalate.*

**Partner's beat** — After the copy phase, each person keeps their partner's beat rather than their own for all three time jumps.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts the work from personal memory to carrying someone else's material faithfully — closer to what a group beat asks of you.*

## Debrief

1. **What was different by the last one?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something specific and small — 'she stopped looking at her hand', 'it got slower without me deciding to'. Not 'it got emotional'.

2. **Did you add anything to make that happen?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'No.' Or an honest 'I tried to and it got worse.' Either lands the point. Move on immediately.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point. Anyone who prefers not to speak can do the gesture silently and their partner mirrors the silence — the exercise still works. Standing is not required; run it seated with a partner if needed. Nobody is asked to draw on personal history; the beat is deliberately mundane, and if someone's choice lands somewhere heavy, let them change it without comment.

## Why it works

Format literacy means recognising structure while inside it. The Harold's second beat asks a player to return to material without freshening it, which feels like failure unless you have felt it work. By fixing the words and the gesture and varying only elapsed time, this removes every other variable — the change the students perceive can only be coming from the return itself. Sixty seconds of watching a partner do it proves the cue faster than an explanation: beat two is the same game, later and truer.

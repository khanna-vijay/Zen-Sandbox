# 😄 Laughter Blast — The Laugh Comes Back

![The Laugh Comes Back](../img/advanced_w05__b2_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> A silly sound is planted, forgotten, then ambushes the room three times until everyone is wheezing.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 05 run-sheet](../week-05.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 5 is about weaving — callbacks, mapping, theme. Before students hunt for callbacks inside a twenty-minute piece, they feel one in their bodies in five minutes. The plant, the dull stretch, the return. It feeds the mapping and reincorporation work that follows, and it gives the room a shared physical memory of why a return gets a bigger laugh than the original.

## What students actually learn

- A callback is not a joke repeated — it is a joke that survived being forgotten.
- The boring stretch between plant and return is doing work, not wasting time.
- Their own body registers the return before their brain does; the laugh arrives ahead of the analysis.

## Setup

Clear floor space so everyone can walk without collisions. No chairs in the middle. No props. Stand where the whole room can see you and know your two sounds cold before you start — rehearse them once alone. Have a raised hand ready as your cut signal and tell them at the top that a raised hand means silence.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Teach the WOOP. You demonstrate first, full size. Room copies three times, bigger each time. Then send them walking and mumbling shopping lists, deliberately dull, ignoring each other. |
| 1 | Let the mumble run flat and boring. With no warning, shout "IT'S BACK!" Three full-volume WOOPs, then immediate return to bored mumbling. Do it once more after a longer gap. |
| 1 | Stop the walk. Teach the bmm-bmm-bmm: knees bent, hands paddling, low. Three loud copies. Send them back to walking and mumbling as if the second sound never happened. |
| 1 | Call "IT'S BACK!" four or five times at random for either sound. Shorten the gaps each time. On the last two, call both — room does both simultaneously. |
| 1 | Call "BOTH, AND DON'T STOP." Fifteen seconds of both sounds at once, then cut dead with a raised hand. Ask one question, take thirty seconds, move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| During the first teach | *"Uglier. Nobody is watching you."* |
| As the walking starts | *"Bored. Properly bored. You have never been more bored."* |
| Mumble is drifting into eye contact or chat | *"Eyes down. You do not know these people."* |
| Immediately after the first return | *"Back to boring. It never happened."* |
| Gaps between calls shortening | *"Faster. Don't wait for it."* |
| On the overlap call | *"Both. At once. Louder."* |
| Final fifteen seconds | *"Don't stop. Don't stop."* |
| The cut | *"Hand up. Silence."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is genuinely dull between calls — real flat mumbling, no performing. Then "IT'S BACK!" lands and the volume jumps instantly, without anyone deciding to. By the third or fourth call people are laughing before they finish the sound. The final overlap is chaotic and loud, and the cut leaves the room breathless and grinning.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The mumbling stretch is fun — people are giggling, making eye contact, playing to each other. | They are treating the dull section as part of the show, so nothing is being forgotten and there is nothing to return to. | Stop them. Say the boring bit is the engine. Ask for a mumble so dull it is embarrassing. Restart the walk and let it run five seconds longer than feels comfortable. |
| The sounds come out small and polite, and the laughs never build. | You demonstrated at half size. The room will not exceed your ceiling. | Redo the teach yourself at full ugly volume. Make them copy three more times, calling for bigger each time, before continuing. |
| By the fourth call people are anticipating and starting the sound before you shout. | Your calls have become rhythmic and predictable. | Break the pattern — one very long gap, then two in quick succession. Surprise is the whole mechanism. |
| Two or three people stand at the edge doing neither sound. | Loud, silly, full-body work in front of strangers; they are waiting to see if it's safe. | Do not single them out. Keep going at full commitment yourself. Most join by the third call. Silent walking is a legitimate way to be in it. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Run exactly as written. One walking space. Five "IT'S BACK!" calls in the third minute. Small rooms feel exposed, so demonstrate twice at the top rather than once. |
| **14–17** | Run as written. Move yourself around the perimeter while they walk so your calls come from different directions — it sharpens the surprise. Six calls in the third minute. |
| **18–20** | Widen the walking space or drop the walk to on-the-spot shuffling to avoid collisions. Six or seven calls, gaps shorter. Cut the final hold to twelve seconds — twenty people at full volume is enough. |

## Variations

**Standing plant** — Skip the walking. Everyone stays in a loose circle, mumbling shopping lists to the floor. Calls land the same way.  
*Easier — removes collision risk and physical self-consciousness, useful with a nervous or mobility-mixed room.*

**Third sound, no teach** — After the second sound, introduce a third by simply doing it once yourself mid-call and letting them copy on the fly. No formal teach.  
*Harder — they have to catch and reproduce a plant without instruction, which is closer to how callbacks actually happen in a piece.*

**They call it** — After the second sound is taught, hand the call to a student. They shout "IT'S BACK!" and choose which sound. Rotate the caller twice.  
*Different emphasis — shifts the skill from responding to a return to judging when a return will land.*

## Debrief

1. **Which call got the biggest laugh, and what happened just before it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a specific call and connects it to the length or dullness of the gap before it — "the one after the long boring bit" — rather than saying the sound itself was funny.

2. **What was the boring mumbling actually doing?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* "Making us forget." "Building the gap." Anything that treats the flat stretch as load-bearing rather than filler. Take one answer, land the cue, move on.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact in this activity. Tell the room at the top that walking is optional — anyone can do it on the spot. The sounds are loud; flag that before you start so anyone with sound sensitivity can position themselves near a door or step out. Knees-bent posture for the second sound is adjustable — hands paddling while standing upright is fine. Nobody has to be funny; the sounds are supplied and identical for everyone. Watch for collisions in the walking phase and call "eyes up" if the room gets tight.

## Why it works

Reincorporation is the punchline of structure. The laugh here is not produced by the sound — the sound is deliberately stupid and gets a thin laugh the first time. It is produced by the gap. The dull mumbling drains the room's attention away from the plant, so the return arrives as surprise rather than repetition, and the audience gets the small pleasure of recognising something they had let go of. Shortening the gaps and then overlapping the two sounds stacks that recognition until the room is laughing at the structure itself. Students feel the mechanism in their bodies in five minutes, which makes the mapping and theme work later in the session land as technique rather than theory.

# 🏟️ Showcase round — One Word, Three Waves

![One Word, Three Waves](../img/advanced_w08__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> A structured long-form set built from a single audience word: three waves of scenes, two group pieces, one shared ending.

`Players 10–20` · `~40 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 8 is the public set. They have built scenes, edits and group work in isolation; this is the first time all of it runs end to end in front of people who did not watch it being made. It follows the shorter form practice earlier in the session and feeds the final set at the end of the course — after this they know the shape holds, and the remaining work is presence, not structure.

## What students actually learn

- They stop treating an audience word as a punchline generator and start treating it as raw stock for a whole piece.
- They feel the difference between a scene that ends and a scene that is stopped — and stop waiting for permission to edit.
- They discover that a set has a shape they can hold in their heads while playing, so they can serve it rather than serve their own bit.
- They learn that being visible in the back line, doing nothing, is part of the performance.

## Setup

Clear a playing area with a defined back line — tape it or use a wall. Chairs for the audience, facing in, close. Invite two to six guests if you can; if not, the non-performing half of the class sits as a genuine audience and behaves like one (no coaching, no notes, no laughing along at process). Nominate a host before you begin — someone who is happy to speak first. You need a visible clock and a way to signal time to yourself only, not to the cast. No props. House lights up is fine; do not fuss with lighting.

## How to run it — 40 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 4 | Place the cast in the back line. Walk them through the shape once, out loud, in under two minutes: opening, three scenes, group, three revisits, group, three collisions, close. Then silence. Let the audience settle. |
| 3 | Host asks for one ordinary word, repeats it twice, steps into the line. Three seconds of stillness. Then the opening — images, tableaux, overheard lines, no scenes — until someone steps out and starts one. |
| 7 | Wave one. Three unrelated two-handers, each drawn from the opening. Roughly two minutes each. Anyone in the line edits by sweeping across or tagging out. Do not intervene unless a scene passes three minutes. |
| 3 | Group piece one. Whole cast, one place or ritual already seen, everyone in within ten seconds. Call the finish yourself with a sweep if it has not resolved by three minutes. |
| 8 | Wave two — the same three scenes, later in time, same characters. Then group piece two off a different thread. Keep it moving; if the cast stalls between scenes, side-coach a name from wave one. |
| 5 | Wave three. Three collisions: characters meet across scenes, or one scene's rule invades another. Faster beats, harder edits. Around ninety seconds each. |
| 3 | Close. Cast finds one final image or line touching the original word. Host calls them forward. Hold the line, bow together, exit. Let the applause finish before anyone speaks. |
| 7 | Notes. Cast sits with the audience. Ask the audience two questions first, then the cast. Keep it to observations of structure, not performance reviews of individuals. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Immediately after the word is given, if anyone twitches | *"Three seconds. Nothing yet."* |
| During the opening, if it starts turning into dialogue | *"Images only. Nobody is a character."* |
| Two minutes into any wave-one scene | *"Sweep it."* |
| When the first player starts a group piece and others hesitate | *"Everyone in. Ten seconds."* |
| At the top of wave two | *"Same people. Later."* |
| Entering wave three | *"Collide them. Shorter."* |
| Approaching the close, if the cast is drifting into a new scene | *"Find the word."* |
| After the final image | *"Hold the line. Bow together."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The back line is alive — people watching the stage, not the floor, and stepping out without a visible decision beforehand. Edits happen from the line, not from the players inside the scene. Wave two lands with a small shock of recognition in the audience because the characters are the same but their situation has moved. The group pieces have a shape you could describe in one sentence afterwards. The close is quiet rather than clever, and the whole thing runs to time without you saying anything after the word is taken.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The opening becomes a two-person scene inside thirty seconds and the rest of the cast sits down. | Nobody is comfortable offering an unattached image, so the first pair grabs the safety of dialogue. | Stop it, reset the line, and run thirty seconds of images only with a hard rule: no second line from the same person. Then continue. |
| Scenes run four, five, six minutes and the set overruns badly. | The line is watching politely rather than editing. Everyone assumes someone else will go. | Between waves, name two people as editors for the next wave. Tell them out loud, in front of the audience. It costs nothing and fixes it immediately. |
| The group piece is twelve people miming vaguely in a clump, all talking at once. | No one established what the place actually is, so everyone hedges. | Side-coach a single call: name the place. Whoever started it says it aloud. Accept the first answer even if it is thin. |
| Wave two is the same scene again with the same information. | Players heard 'revisit' as 'repeat' and are protecting the version that worked. | Before wave two, give one sentence: something has happened to these people since. Do not specify what. Let them choose in the moment. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One cast, everyone on the back line. Host is a cast member who steps back in after the ask. Run the full shape and full timings as written. Bring in outside guests if you can; if not, place three empty chairs at the front and treat them as real. |
| **14–17** | Two casts of seven or eight. Each runs a shortened set: opening two minutes, two scenes, one group piece, two revisits, three collisions, close — thirteen minutes each. The other cast is the audience and does not speak until notes. Setup 4, sets 13 and 13, notes 10. |
| **18–20** | Two casts of nine or ten. Cut group piece two entirely from both sets. Each set runs fourteen minutes: opening, three scenes, one group piece, three revisits, close. Setup 4, sets 14 and 14, notes 8. Non-performing cast sits at the back and stays silent. |

## Variations

**Word on the wall** — Write the audience word on a flipchart and leave it visible throughout. Cast may look at it whenever they lose the thread.  
*Easier — removes the memory load and stops the set drifting away from its source in wave three.*

**No verbal edits** — Every edit must be a physical sweep or a tag. Nobody may say 'scene', clap, or call an end from offstage.  
*Harder — forces the line to commit their whole body to an edit rather than announcing one from safety.*

**Host stays out** — The host does not rejoin the cast. They watch the whole set from the side and call the cast forward at the end, then give the first thirty seconds of notes.  
*Different emphasis — one person carries the shape of the piece from outside it, which sharpens everyone's sense of the set as an object.*

## Debrief

1. **Audience first: what came back? Name one thing from the opening that you saw again later.**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Two or three specific callbacks named without hesitation, including at least one the cast did not plan. If the audience struggles, that is your note: the threads were not planted clearly enough.

2. **Cast: when did you decide to edit, and what told you?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something concrete — 'they'd said the thing twice', 'the laugh had landed', 'I could see it looping'. Vague answers about instinct mean the editing was arbitrary; press once, then move on.

3. **What did you give up in order to serve the piece?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* An actual thing they wanted to do and didn't: a joke, an entrance, a character. If everyone says nothing, the set was probably a series of solos rather than one piece.

4. **Where did the back line stop watching?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* An honest moment — usually just after a big laugh, or during the second group piece. Naming it is enough; do not solve it here.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    Group pieces and tableaux invite physical proximity. State before the first one that nobody has to touch anyone, that a tableau can be built with space between bodies, and that anyone may hold their own shape apart from the group. Ask for a show of hands from anyone who would rather not be touched at all, and tell the cast to honour it without comment. Anyone who does not want to perform in front of guests takes the host role or sits with the audience for the whole set — offer this privately before the block, not in front of the room. Remind the cast that the audience word is a starting point, not a licence: if a thread heads somewhere a castmate clearly does not want to play, edit it out. Nobody has to be funny to be in the set.

## Why it works

Serving the piece requires the piece to be visible to everyone at once, and a single word plus a fixed wave structure makes it visible. Because the material is shared — everything comes from one opening that everyone watched — no player can advance their own idea without also advancing the whole. The three-wave shape then forces attention outward twice: wave two makes you remember someone else's scene accurately, and wave three makes you find the join between two scenes you were not in. That is stage presence in its useful sense — being active while not speaking, tracking the whole room, entering because the piece needs it. Clarity follows from the same constraint: with an audience present and a shape to complete, players name places and people early, because vagueness costs the group time they can all feel running out.

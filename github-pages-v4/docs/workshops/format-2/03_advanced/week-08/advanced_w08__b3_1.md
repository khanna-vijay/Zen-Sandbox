# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Twenty Paces

![Twenty Paces](../img/advanced_w08__b3_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Two lines at opposite walls throw big physical shapes across the room and shout back the one word they read.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 8 is the public set. The room they play tonight is bigger than the room they rehearsed in, and half of what they built in weeks five to seven was built at conversational scale. This resets the calibration before anything else happens: body first, word second, twenty paces of distance doing the arguing. It feeds straight into the set-shaping work later in the block, where a shape held clean reads as an offer everyone can build on.

## What students actually learn

- That a shape which feels enormous to the person making it usually reads as ordinary from the back of the room.
- That an audience reads the whole body — hips, weight, floor contact — long before it reads a face.
- That holding still for five seconds is a legitimate move, not a gap to fill.
- That their partner's one-word read is information, not a verdict.

## Setup

Clear the widest floor you have and push chairs to the ends. Two lines facing across the gap, roughly twenty paces. If the room is narrow, use the diagonal. No props, no music. Odd numbers: put yourself in a line, or pair one person to read two opposite shapes.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Form the two lines and explain. One line throws a full-body shape on your count; the facing line shouts back one word. Demonstrate one small shape and one big one so the difference is obvious. |
| 1 | Line A throws on 'three'. Line B shouts single words, ragged and loud. Repeat twice more, quickly. Cut any discussion between goes. |
| 1 | Swap lines. Line B throws three shapes, Line A reads back. Call for the floor and the ceiling — crouches, reaches, weight dropped low. |
| 1 | Both lines throw simultaneously on your count. Each person reads whoever is directly opposite. Four rounds, no pause between. |
| 1 | Two final rounds: throw, hold dead still five seconds while the far line reads, then drop. Say 'serve the piece' and move straight on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| First round, shapes are timid | *"Twenty paces. Bigger."* |
| Words come out mumbled or hesitant | *"Shout it. First word only."* |
| Shapes are all arms and shoulders | *"Use the floor. Use the ceiling."* |
| People start pausing to think before throwing | *"Don't plan it. Throw it."* |
| Simultaneous rounds get chaotic | *"Your opposite. Only them."* |
| The held round, people wobble | *"Dead still. Let them read it."* |
| Someone laughs off their own shape | *"Commit to it. Hold."* |
| Closing the block | *"Serve the piece."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Loud, fast, slightly messy. Shapes that involve the whole body — someone on the floor, someone stretched up, weight thrown off centre. Words fired back within half a second, overlapping. Nobody checking whether their shape was good. By the held rounds, the room goes genuinely quiet for five seconds and the reads that follow are sharper than the ones before.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Shapes that are basically gestures — a raised arm, a tilted head — and reads that are all vague ('confused', 'thinking'). | People are playing at rehearsal scale because that is what the last three weeks trained. They have no reference for twenty paces. | Stop for ten seconds. Throw a small shape yourself, then the same idea at full commitment. Ask the far line which one they could read. Restart. |
| A pause before each throw while people work out what to do. | They have decided the shape needs to mean something specific and are composing it in advance. | Count faster. Give them 'three' with barely a breath between rounds. The shape only has to be readable, not correct. |
| Reads that are jokes about the person rather than the shape — names, in-references, laughter. | Group is deflecting the exposure of standing in a line being looked at. | Call 'one word, what you see'. Reset with a round where you name the reading line and count them in tightly. Do not lecture. |
| During the simultaneous round, people watch the whole opposite line instead of their partner. | The instruction to pick one person got lost in the noise. | Call 'your opposite, only them' mid-round. If it persists, have each pair make eye contact before you count. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One pair of lines, five to seven a side. Run every round as written; you will have spare seconds, so add a third held round at the end. |
| **14–17** | One pair of lines still works if the room is wide enough. If the lines curve round the walls, split into two facing pairs at either end of the room and run both simultaneously off your single count. |
| **18–20** | Two pairs of lines, five each, running off your one count. Drop the simultaneous section from four rounds to three so the noise stays legible. Stand between the two pairs so both hear you. |

## Variations

**Half distance** — Bring the lines to ten paces. Same rounds, same counts.  
*Easier. Useful for a nervous group or a small room — the read is more forgiving, and you can push the distance out mid-exercise once they warm.*

**Two-beat shapes** — On the count, throw a shape, then change it once into a second shape. The far line reads the change, not either shape.  
*Harder. Forces attention onto transition and intention rather than the static picture.*

**Sound with it** — Each thrown shape carries a non-word sound. The reading line shouts one word for the shape and sound together.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts from purely visual clarity to whole-instrument clarity, which is closer to what the set needs.*

## Debrief

1. **Which of your shapes got read the way you meant it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone naming a specific shape and admitting the read was different from their intention — and not treating that as a failure. If people only say 'that was fun', ask one person directly, then move on.

2. **What changed in the five-second holds?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'I stopped adding things.' 'They had time to actually look.' Anything that names stillness as active rather than empty. Thirty seconds is plenty here.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. Say before the first count that shapes go as low or as high as each body allows, and that a seated or standing-only version reads perfectly well from twenty paces — the far line is reading shape and weight, not athleticism. Shouting is optional; anyone who prefers can say their word at speaking volume and their opposite will lean in. Flag that some shapes involve dropping weight fast, so nobody throws themselves at the floor. Anyone who wants to sit out stands at the end of a line and reads only.

## Why it works

Stage presence and clarity fail at scale, not at intention. Most of the class has spent seven weeks playing to people three feet away, and their bodies have calibrated to that. Twenty paces removes the possibility of subtlety carrying the offer — the only feedback is a stranger shouting a word, immediately, with no chance to explain. That loop is fast enough to change behaviour inside five minutes: throw, hear the read, adjust the next throw. The five-second holds do the second job. They separate the shape from the act of making it, so the performer feels what an audience is actually given. That is the cue — serve the piece — turned into a physical fact rather than a note.

# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Verdict in Three Words

![Verdict in Three Words](../img/advanced_w03__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> A fast circle where each person rules an everyday thing overrated or underrated and defends it in exactly three words.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 03 run-sheet](../week-03.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 3 is about openings — the part of a long-form show that generates material rather than entertains. This runs first, before any opening work, so the room has already felt what it is like to produce specific opinions on demand. It feeds the block that follows, where they build openings that harvest exactly this kind of detail. It also sets the week's cue in the body before it is said out loud.

## What students actually learn

- An opinion arrives faster than they think, and does not need to be interesting to be usable.
- Three words is enough. Length is not the same as substance.
- Everyday objects carry more specificity than invented ones — the well is already full.

## Setup

Nothing needed. Standing circle, everyone able to see everyone. Have six or seven items ready in your head before class: teabags, the office kitchen, Sunday, a doorbell, umbrellas, birthdays, the number eight. Pick things everyone has an experience of, not things that require knowledge.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Give the rule in two sentences. Demo one item yourself with the three words counted on your fingers. Take no questions. |
| 2 | Call item one. Go clockwise, five seconds a head. Then call item two and go the other way, starting to your right. |
| 1 | Final lap. Each person names their own item and its verdict only. No reason. Three seconds each. Keep the rhythm relentless. |
| 1 | Show of hands on the hardest split. Say the numbers. Name the cue: the opening isn't a warm-up, it's the well. Move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Before the first lap starts | *"Three words. Not four. Not a sentence."* |
| First person hesitates | *"First thought. Wrong is fine."* |
| Pace starts to sag mid-lap | *"Faster. Don't earn it."* |
| Someone begins explaining or qualifying | *"Stop. That's the whole turn."* |
| Someone tries to reply to a previous verdict | *"No debate. Next."* |
| Starting the final lap | *"Your item. Your verdict. Go."* |
| Closing out | *"That's the well. Everything came from a teabag."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The circle sounds like a metronome. Verdicts land without preamble, some of them flat and boring, and nobody apologises for the boring ones. You hear at least two answers that make the room laugh by accident. When you ask for the split, hands go up fast and the room notices it disagreed about something ordinary. Total elapsed time is five minutes, not seven.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| People counting words on their fingers before speaking, and the lap taking twice as long. | The three-word rule is being treated as a puzzle to solve rather than a length limit. | Say "three-ish, keep going" and accept two or four. The constraint exists to stop speeches, not to be policed. |
| Answers getting funnier and longer as the lap goes on, with people clearly writing ahead. | The room has decided this is a joke competition. Later players are drafting instead of listening. | Cut in with "Faster. Don't earn it." Reverse direction unexpectedly so nobody knows when their turn lands. |
| One or two people go blank and the circle stalls on them. | Being watched while producing an opinion under time pressure. Common in week three with quieter students. | Say their verdict for them out loud and move on immediately. Do not wait. Come back to them in the final lap when the stakes are lower. |
| The whole room agrees on everything and the show of hands is 18-0. | Your items were too safe, or the first speaker set a verdict everyone copied. | Pick items with genuine division next time — Sunday, the seaside, birthdays. Note the unanimity out loud as a finding and move on. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Run three items plus the final lap — the extra item fits comfortably inside two minutes at this size. |
| **14–17** | One circle, two items plus the final lap as written. Hold the five-second cap firmly or you will overrun. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten. You call the item to both rooms at once, they run in parallel. Drop the second item — one item plus the final lap only. Bring them back together for the show of hands. |

## Variations

**One word** — Reason must be a single word instead of three. Verdict, one word, next.  
*Easier — removes the counting problem entirely and doubles the speed. Use it if the group is nervous or if you are short on time.*

**No repeats** — No word already used by anyone else in that lap may be used again. If you repeat, you say "repeat" and pass.  
*Harder — forces listening across the whole circle instead of drafting ahead. Adds roughly thirty seconds per lap.*

**Overrated by whom** — Instead of a bare verdict, each person names who overrates it, in three words. "Overrated by landlords."  
*Different emphasis — shifts from opinion to point of view, which feeds character-driven openings rather than object-driven ones.*

## Debrief

1. **Where did your answer come from?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says they had no opinion until they opened their mouth, or that they took the opposite of the person before them. Anything that admits the opinion was manufactured on the spot. Move on as soon as one person says this plainly.

2. **Which answers do you still remember?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They name specific ones — the odd, flat, or overly personal answers, not the polished jokes. If they name a joke, ask what else they remember. Stop when someone says "the weird ones stuck."

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. Standing circle — offer chairs and run it seated if anyone prefers; the exercise is unaffected. Anyone may pass by saying "pass", and you move on without comment. Keep items neutral: no food-and-body items, no politics, no religion, nothing that turns a verdict into a disclosure. If a verdict lands as a personal dig at someone in the room, do not address it in the moment — keep the lap running and have a word afterwards.

## Why it works

Format literacy starts with knowing what an opening is for. An opening is not a warm-up; it is a source of raw material a group draws on for the next half hour. This exercise puts the group through that mechanism in miniature: a neutral prompt goes in, and fifteen unrehearsed specifics come out, none of them planned, most of them useless, two or three of them alive. The three-word cap prevents the reflex that ruins real openings — explaining rather than offering. By the time they build an actual opening in the next block, they have already felt that material arrives from ordinary things under pressure, not from having something to say.

# 🏟️ Showcase round — One Word, Three Waves

![One Word, Three Waves](../img/advanced_w06__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> A structured long-form set built from a single audience word: three waves of scenes, two group pieces, one shared ending.

`Players 10–20` · `~40 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 06 run-sheet](../week-06.md)

## Why this activity, here

Weeks four and five gave them the edit and the group piece as separate tools. This is the first time they hold a whole set in one shape and feel where it sags. It feeds the final showcase: after tonight they know what twenty-six minutes of self-managed pacing actually costs.

## What students actually learn

- They stop treating an edit as a rescue and start treating it as a rhythm choice.
- They learn that the opening is a bank — three minutes of material spent across the next twenty.
- They find out who in the cast they habitually talk over, and hear it named while it is still fixable.
- They get the physical sense of a set having a middle that has to be shorter and hotter than its start.

## Setup

Clear a playing space with a marked back line. Chairs in a shallow arc facing it — anyone not casting sits there as audience. No props, no chairs on stage. Put a visible clock where the back line can see it, and nominate yourself or one sitting student as timekeeper with silent hand signals for one minute and thirty seconds remaining. Write the shape on the board before they arrive: OPENING / WAVE 1 / GROUP / WAVE 2 / GROUP / WAVE 3 / CLOSE. Leave it up during the set.

## How to run it — 40 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 4 | Walk the board. Name each section and its length. Pick the host. Say the two rules that matter: the opening contains no scenes, and every player has permission to edit. Take the contact consent check now. |
| 3 | Cast to the back line. Host asks for the word, repeats it twice, steps back. Three seconds of silence. Run the opening — images, tableaux, lines. It ends when someone starts a scene. |
| 7 | Wave one. Three unrelated two-handers, each seeded from the opening. Roughly two minutes each. Coach the sweep and the tag edit out loud if nobody moves by ninety seconds. |
| 3 | Group piece one. Somebody names a place, idea or ritual already seen. Whole cast in within ten seconds. Keep it moving — no static line of statues. |
| 7 | Wave two. Same three scenes, later in time. Same characters, situation changed. Do not let them replay the earlier scene. Call the jump if they stall. |
| 3 | Group piece two. Same rules, different thread from the first one. Do not let them repeat the same place. |
| 6 | Wave three — three collision scenes, faster and shorter. Then the close: one final image or line off the word. Host calls the cast forward. Hold, bow, exit. |
| 7 | Cast sits with the audience. Run the debrief questions. Keep it about shape and timing, not about who was funny. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Opening, first thirty seconds, if two people start dialoguing | *"Images only. No scenes yet."* |
| Opening, at 2:45 | *"Someone step out. Start it."* |
| A wave-one scene has hit its best moment and is still going | *"Cut on the rise."* |
| A quieter player is being spoken over or narrated at | *"Let the quiet one breathe."* |
| Group piece has fewer than half the cast in it after ten seconds | *"Everybody in. Now."* |
| Wave two opens and the players resume the old scene | *"Later. Same people, new trouble."* |
| Wave three, scenes running as long as wave one | *"Shorter. Collide them."* |
| Last ninety seconds | *"Find the word. One image. Done."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The back line stays alive — people watching their own show, leaning in, ready. Edits arrive from players who are not in the scene. Wave three feels faster than wave one without anyone rushing their speech. At least two threads from the opening come back without being explained. The close lands on an image rather than a joke, and the bow is together. Expect it to be rough. A first run where six of nine scenes work and the shape holds is a success.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The opening collapses into a two-person conversation inside the first minute. | Silence feels unbearable, and dialogue is the reflex escape. | Call "Images only" and let it continue. Afterwards, tell them the opening is the bank they spend for the next twenty minutes — no bank, nothing to withdraw. |
| Scenes run four minutes. Nobody sweeps. | Editing a friend feels like an insult, so everyone waits for someone else. | For wave one only, clap once at ninety seconds and require an edit within five seconds. Hand the timing back to them from group piece one onwards. |
| Wave two plays the identical scene again with the same information. | "Later in time" heard as "carry on". | Side-coach "Later — what changed since?" If it happens twice, stop for fifteen seconds and have each pair say one thing that has changed before restarting. |
| Three confident players take almost every two-hander. | Speed of initiation, not intent. | Before wave two, cap it: nobody plays more than two two-handers across the set. Name the players who have not been in a scene and put them in wave three. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One cast, everybody in, timings exactly as written. Two or three students who want to watch can sit as audience for waves one and two, then join the group pieces and the close. |
| **14–17** | Still one cast. Trim every two-hander to ninety seconds so nine scenes fit inside the same fourteen minutes of wave time. Cap each player at two two-handers. Track who has not spoken and place them in wave three. |
| **18–20** | Split into two casts of nine or ten. Each runs a half set: two-minute opening, wave one of two scenes, one group piece, wave three of two collision scenes, close — twelve minutes each. Cut the frame to three minutes, allow two for changeover, debrief both casts together in eleven. |

## Variations

**Waves with a caller** — You stand at the side and announce each section aloud as it begins, and you call every edit yourself.  
*Easier — removes the cost of self-managed structure so they can concentrate on the scenes.*

**No opening** — Take the word and go straight into wave one. The cast has to mine the word directly, with no shared bank of images.  
*Harder — forces invention under pressure and exposes who was relying on other people's offers.*

**Silent group pieces** — Both group pieces run with no speech at all. Movement, sound and rhythm only.  
*Different emphasis — moves the focus from content to tempo and shared breath.*

## Debrief

1. **Where did the set drag?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They name a specific place — "the second scene of wave two, we already knew everything" — rather than "the middle bit". Someone identifies a moment where an edit was available and nobody took it.

2. **Which edit was the best one, and who made it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone credits a player who cut while the scene was still climbing, and can say what the cut protected. Not "we should edit sooner" in general.

3. **Who did not get enough room, and when?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* A named player, a named section, said without apology or defensiveness. The person named agrees or amends. If it turns into reassurance, redirect once and move on.

4. **What did the close do with the word?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They can describe the image and connect it to something from the opening. If nobody remembers the opening, that is the answer — say so and move on.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    Group pieces and tableaux bring people close. Before the set, say plainly: touch is optional, ask or signal first, and a near-touch shape reads the same from the audience. Anyone may pass. Confirm with a show of hands that everyone has heard this. Nobody has to enter a scene — staying on the back line for a whole wave is a legitimate choice and you will not call them out for it. If someone is having a bad night, seat them in the audience with a job: watch the edits and report in the debrief. Keep the debrief on structure. Do not let it become an assessment of who was funny.

## Why it works

Pacing is invisible when scenes are practised one at a time. This block makes it visible by fixing a shape and a clock, then handing both to the cast. Because wave three must be faster than wave one and everyone can see the board, the cast has to make tempo decisions in public — and the cost of a late edit is felt immediately in the section that follows. The cue does the rest: cutting on the rise trains them to leave energy in the room rather than spend it, and letting the quiet one breathe stops the fast players from setting the tempo for everybody.

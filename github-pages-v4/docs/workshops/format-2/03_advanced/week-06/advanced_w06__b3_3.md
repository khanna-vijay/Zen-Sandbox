# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Count the Gaps

![Count the Gaps](../img/advanced_w06__b3_3.jpg){ .infographic }

> A standing circle counts upward with no leader, and no two silences between numbers may be the same length.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 06 run-sheet](../week-06.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 6 is about pacing, edits and the shape of a set. Before they cut scenes, they need to feel time as a shared material. This warm-up puts the whole room on one clock with no leader, so the rest of the session — where edits land, when to hold a beat — has a bodily reference. It feeds directly into the cue: cut on the rise, let the quiet one breathe.

## What students actually learn

- They stop treating silence as a mistake to be filled and start hearing it as something they are choosing.
- They begin tracking the whole room's impulse instead of only their own urge to speak.
- They notice that varying rhythm is a decision, not an accident, and that a long pause can be the strongest move.

## Setup

Clear floor space for one standing circle. No chairs, no props, no paper. Stand in the circle yourself for the first attempts, then step out so the group carries it. Have 'from one' ready as your only restart call — say it fast and flat, never as a reprimand.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Give the rules: count from one, anyone speaks next, no leader, no order. Two voices together or a wrong number means back to one. Start immediately. |
| 1 | Run plain attempts to eight. Call 'from one' the moment it breaks and restart within a second. Cut off any spoken strategy with 'no plans'. |
| 1 | Add the gap rule. Each silence must differ in length from the one before. Target eight again. Let them fail several times without comment. |
| 1 | Raise the target to twelve, gap rule still live. Side-coach the long pause: tell them a very long gap is legal and usually the strongest choice. |
| 1 | Take one last attempt. Stop it wherever it is. Hold five seconds of silence together, then release the circle and move on without discussion. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| First plain attempt, as soon as two voices collide | *"From one. Straight back in."* |
| Someone starts explaining a system | *"No plans. Just listen."* |
| The count is rattling out fast and even | *"Nobody rush. The gap is part of it."* |
| Immediately after adding the gap rule | *"Change the length every time. Never twice the same."* |
| A long silence opens and someone lunges to fill it | *"Let it sit. The long one is legal."* |
| The group reaches seven or eight and tightens | *"Breathe. Don't grab it."* |
| Final attempt, wherever it stops | *"Stop there. Five seconds of quiet together."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The circle is quiet between numbers and nobody looks anxious about it. Gaps are visibly uneven — a two-second pause, then a snap, then a five-second hold. Restarts happen without groans or apology. By the end, at least one attempt runs past eight, and the room notices the long pause landing rather than panicking through it.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers coming out fast and evenly, one every second, with no silence at all | The group is treating this as a speed task and filling every gap to avoid collision risk. | Stop the attempt. Say: 'Slowest possible version. Nobody speaks until the silence feels too long.' Restart from one. |
| The same two or three confident people saying nearly every number | Quieter members are waiting for a gap that never opens, and the loud ones are covering the risk for everyone. | Say: 'If you've said three numbers already, sit the next attempt out vocally — still stand in the circle.' Do not name individuals. |
| Someone whispering a rotation plan or nodding to signal turns | The group is solving the puzzle rather than listening, which is the normal first instinct. | Call 'no plans' and add: 'Eyes down or soft focus.' Removing visual signalling forces them onto sound. |
| Visible frustration and sighing after repeated restarts | You are treating restarts as failures in your tone, or the target is too high for the room today. | Flatten your restart call to two words. Drop the target back to six. Say out loud that restarts cost nothing. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Plain target eight, gap-rule target eight, then twelve. Timings as written; you will likely get one clean run past ten. |
| **14–17** | One circle still — the difficulty is the point. Keep the plain target at eight but set the final target at ten rather than twelve, or the last minute is all restarts. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles at opposite ends of the room, each running independently. Give both the same targets: eight, then ten. Run the same clock and call restarts for whichever circle you are nearest; they self-manage the other. Bring both circles into one for the closing five seconds of silence. |

## Variations

**Count to five** — Drop the target to five and keep only the plain rule for the whole block. No gap rule at all.  
*Easier — for a nervous or very new room, or when the previous block ran long and you have four minutes.*

**Backwards from twelve** — Keep the gap rule and count down from twelve to one instead of up.  
*Harder — the descending sequence removes the automatic momentum of counting up, so they must track both number and rhythm.*

**Words, not numbers** — Replace numbers with a single agreed word repeated — 'yes', or the group's name for the show. Same collision rule, same gap rule, target eight repetitions.  
*Different emphasis — with no sequence to track, all attention goes to rhythm and shared timing.*

## Debrief

1. **What made you decide to speak when you did?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something about sensing the room rather than deciding in advance — 'I felt the pause getting long', 'I could tell nobody else was going to'. If answers are all about counting turns or plans, run one more attempt instead of talking.

2. **Which gap was the best one?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They name a long silence, not a fast exchange, and can say why it felt good — it built pressure, it let the number land. That is the pacing note you carry into the scene work.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact and no touch in this activity. Standing is the default; anyone who needs to sit may sit at the edge of the circle and count from there with no loss of standing. Eyes-down or closed-eyes versions are optional — offer them, never require them, since closing eyes in a group is uncomfortable for some. Nobody is named or singled out for causing a restart; say 'from one' to the room, never to a person. Anyone who prefers not to speak can stay in the circle and listen for the first attempts.

## Why it works

Pacing is usually taught as an individual choice, which is why it does not transfer to ensemble work. This puts the clock in the middle of the circle where nobody owns it. The no-leader rule removes the option of deciding in advance, so the only way to avoid collision is to track everyone else's readiness — the same attention an edit requires. The gap rule then makes silence a variable they must actively change rather than a void they rush past. Once a group has held a five-second gap on purpose and survived it, 'let the quiet one breathe' stops being an instruction and becomes something they have already done.

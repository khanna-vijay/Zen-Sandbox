# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Call Me For

![Call Me For](../img/advanced_w07__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Each person names one thing they can genuinely be relied on for, and the room steps in if they need it this week.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 07 run-sheet](../week-07.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 7 is where the group stops treating the audience as a judge and starts treating it as a source of live data. Before they can read a room's energy without obeying it, they need practice reading each other's — cheaply, without discussion. This ice-breaker puts real need in the room and makes the response silent and physical, so the group registers signal without negotiating about it. It feeds the session's main work on reading laughter, silence and restlessness as information.

## What students actually learn

- That admitting a need in public costs less than they assumed, and passes in a second.
- That a room gives you accurate information physically, before anyone speaks.
- That noticing a signal does not oblige you to act on it in the moment.
- That an offer stated plainly is more usable than an offer dressed up.

## Setup

Standing circle, no props, no chairs in the way. Everyone visible to everyone. You need roughly one metre of floor per person so a step forward is possible. Timer visible to you only. Have two of your own examples ready — one practical, one trivial — in case the first lap stalls.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up standing. Explain both laps in one go. Give your own "Call me for ___" example. Start the lap immediately clockwise from you. |
| 2 | Run the first lap. One sentence each. After each claim, hold one beat for step-ins, then move to the next person. Keep the rhythm tight. |
| 1 | Second lap, same direction, faster: "Don't call me for ___", two or three words. No step-ins. Cut stories on the spot and move on. |
| 1 | Take two hands: who'll use a claim they heard? Ten seconds each, name person and thing. Name what the room now knows. Move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Before the first lap starts | *"Small and true. Not impressive."* |
| Someone starts explaining their claim | *"Sentence only. Next."* |
| Nobody steps in for three claims running | *"If you need it, step. It costs nothing."* |
| A step-in gets a laugh or a comment | *"No commentary. Keep the circle moving."* |
| Starting the second lap | *"Two words. Faster. Go."* |
| Someone tells a story in the second lap | *"Cut. Next."* |
| Taking the two hands at the end | *"Name the person. Name the thing. Ten seconds."* |
| Closing | *"That's information, not homework. Warm-up now."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The first lap runs at roughly six seconds a person and step-ins happen without hesitation or apology. Nobody justifies a claim. The second lap is noticeably quicker and slightly loud. At the end, two people name a specific person and a specific thing without hedging. The group is standing closer than when it started, and you move into the warm-up on time.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Claims getting longer and funnier as the lap goes round — people building on the last one. | Someone landed a laugh early and the group read the game as a bit. | Cut in with "Sentence only. Next." Skip your reaction entirely. Deadpan the next three claims yourself and the rhythm resets. |
| No one steps forward at all for the whole first lap. | Stepping reads as asking for a favour in front of strangers, and nobody wants to go first. | Step in yourself for a claim you genuinely could use. Say nothing about it. One visible instance usually opens the rest. |
| People stuck, saying "I can't think of anything I'm good at". | They've heard the prompt as a claim of expertise rather than availability. | Offer trivia out loud: lifts to the station, spare phone chargers, knowing which pub is quiet. Say "reliable, not skilled" and move on. |
| The block runs to seven minutes. | You let the second lap be a second first lap. | Two or three words, no beat between speakers. Point at people in turn rather than waiting for them to start. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Both laps as written, with a full beat after each claim for step-ins. You'll finish with slack — take three hands at the end instead of two. |
| **14–17** | One circle. Tighten the step-in beat to a single count and cut your own example to five words. Take exactly two hands at the end. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten, each with a nominated starter. Run both laps simultaneously; the noise is fine. Skip the two-hands round and close with a single sentence from you. |

## Variations

**Written first** — Hand out slips before circling. Everyone writes their "Call me for" claim, then reads it aloud when their turn comes.  
*Easier — removes on-the-spot generation for people who freeze in circles, and the whole lap runs faster.*

**Second-hand claims** — After the first lap, go round again and each person names something the person to their left could be called on for, guessing from what they know.  
*Harder — requires attention and risks being wrong out loud, which is exactly the discomfort of misreading a room.*

**Read the step** — Run the first lap with claimants closing their eyes. After each claim they guess how many people stepped in, then open and check.  
*Different emphasis — moves the work from disclosure to reading response accurately without seeing it, which is closer to reading a dark house.*

## Debrief

1. **What did you notice in your body when people stepped forward for your claim?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something concrete and slightly surprised — relief, a jolt, wanting to explain themselves. Not a general comment about how nice the exercise was. Move on once two people have said something specific.

2. **Did anyone change their claim after hearing the room?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone admits they'd planned something bigger and downgraded it, or upgraded after nobody stepped in for the person before. That's the room's energy acting as instruction — name it and connect to the cue.

3. **How is a step-in different from applause?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* A step-in tells you something specific and true; applause tells you the room is warm. Good answers land on "one is information, one is temperature". Then stop.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. Stepping forward is the only physical element and it is entirely voluntary — say explicitly that not stepping means nothing. Anyone who prefers not to step may nod instead; state that option before the first lap. People may pass on either lap with a raised hand and no explanation; take the pass and move immediately, without warmth or comment, so passing costs nothing. Watch for claims that edge into disclosure about health, money or family; if one lands heavily, acknowledge it with a single neutral nod and keep the circle moving rather than pausing on it. Anyone unable to stand for five minutes takes a chair in the circle and raises a hand rather than stepping.

## Why it works

Audience-energy management fails when performers treat a response as a command. This exercise separates the two mechanically. A step-in is unambiguous, costless and silent — it delivers information with no request attached, and the claimant cannot act on it during the lap because the lap keeps moving. That gap between receiving a signal and being able to obey it is the whole skill. The second lap adds the counterweight: naming what you're not for, fast enough that nobody can perform humility about it, so the group learns that limits stated plainly don't cool a room. By the time they're onstage reading laughter, they've already practised the split between noticing and complying.

# 😄 Laughter Blast — Stadium Lungs

![Stadium Lungs](../img/advanced_w07__b2_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> The whole room becomes a football crowd — gasps, groans and a rolling roar of laughter with nothing on the pitch.

`Players 8–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 07 run-sheet](../week-07.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 7 is about the contract with the audience, and the session skill is reading their energy without obeying it. This is the physical warm-up that puts crowd energy in their own bodies first — they make the roar, they make the groan, they feel how fast it builds and how fast it dies. It comes before any scene work today, and it feeds the longer audience-facing sets later in the session, where players have to notice a room going quiet and keep playing anyway.

## What students actually learn

- They stop treating a big laugh as a verdict, because they have just manufactured one from nothing.
- They learn that a room's energy has a shape — build, peak, drop — and that the shape is readable in real time.
- They find their loud voice in a group before they need it alone.
- They feel silence after noise and register it as data rather than failure.

## Setup

Standing circle, all chairs pushed back. No props. You need enough floor for everyone to bounce on the spot without touching. Tell anyone with a voice injury, migraine, hearing sensitivity or late-stage pregnancy that they can mouth the sounds, clap instead, or stand a step outside the circle — say this before you start, not after. No contact in this activity, so no consent step needed, but check spacing so bouncing arms miss faces. Warn a neighbouring room if the walls are thin.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Frame the stadium in two sentences. Demonstrate the near-miss 'ooooOOOH' at full volume yourself. Then start the crowd sounds: near-miss and disappointed groan, twice each, everyone together. |
| 1 | Finish the crowd sounds: sharp collective gasp twice, then the slow rising 'aaaaay' twice. Push for volume on the second go of each. Do not let them get precious about it. |
| 1 | Laughter wave. Everyone laughs continuously. Point round the circle; your hand raises that section to full volume, then they drop to a chuckle. One full lap slowly. |
| 1 | Second lap of the wave, faster. Then go straight into the full terrace: everyone roaring, bouncing, arms up. Build the volume for roughly forty seconds. |
| 1 | Throw both hands down. Cut to total silence and hold it thirty seconds. Then ask one question, take one or two answers, and move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Right after your demo, when they look sceptical | *"Ugly is correct. Pretty is wrong."* |
| First crowd sound comes out thin | *"That's a village hall. Give me a stadium."* |
| During the groan | *"From the belly, not the throat."* |
| Starting the laughter wave | *"Don't wait to find it funny. Just make the sound."* |
| Your hand passes a section | *"You're the loudest people in the ground."* |
| Building the terrace | *"Feet off the floor. Arms up. More."* |
| Two seconds before the cut | *"Hands watching me."* |
| In the silence, if someone giggles | *"Stay in it. Notice what just left the room."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    By the second crowd sound the volume is genuinely uncomfortable. Nobody is glancing sideways to check whether they are doing it right. The laughter wave sustains between the loud sections instead of dropping to nothing. During the terrace, feet are actually leaving the floor. When your hands come down, the cut is inside half a second and the silence holds — people are grinning, slightly out of breath, and nobody talks. That gap is the whole point of the block.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Polite, half-volume sounds and a lot of eye contact around the circle | You demonstrated at seventy per cent, or you explained the game instead of doing it. They are matching your commitment level exactly. | Stop. Do one more solo demo at genuine full volume, ugly on purpose. Then go again immediately. Do not discuss it. |
| The laughter wave collapses into silence between the pointed sections | They think the chuckle is a rest. It is the bed the wave rides on. | Call 'nobody goes to zero' mid-lap and keep pointing. Reduce to one lap if the energy will not hold. |
| Two or three people standing with arms folded, mouthing along | Volume in a group is exposing, and for some this is the loudest they have been in public in years. | Leave them. Do not single them out or make eye contact. Most join by the terrace. Some never do and the block still works. |
| The silence after the cut breaks instantly with chatter and laughter | You cut without a clear physical signal, or you spoke too soon after. | Make the hands-down enormous and freeze yourself. Your stillness holds theirs. Count thirty in your head before speaking. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Two full laps of the laughter wave as written — a small circle laps quickly, so point deliberately and let each section have three or four seconds. The terrace will feel thin; get closer to them and roar into the middle yourself. |
| **14–17** | One circle, exactly as written. Point in blocks of three or four people rather than individuals so the wave has weight. Two laps fits comfortably in the ninety seconds. |
| **18–20** | One circle, but point in blocks of five. One and a half laps is enough — a full second lap will overrun. Step into the centre for the terrace so everyone can see your hands for the cut. |

## Variations

**Sit-down terrace** — Run the whole thing seated in the circle. Keep the sounds and the wave; replace the bouncing with hands and stamping feet.  
*Easier. Use it when the group is stiff, older, first thing in the morning, or when several people cannot stand for five minutes.*

**Two ends** — Split the circle into two halves. Give each half a competing chant or roar and let them try to out-volume each other. Cut both at once.  
*Harder. Sustaining volume against another group is more demanding than riding a shared wave, and the cut is far harder to land clean.*

**The pitch is real** — Narrate an invisible match in single phrases — 'he's through', 'off the post', 'penalty' — and let the crowd respond without you calling the sound.  
*Different emphasis. Moves them from copying a called sound to reading a stimulus and choosing a response, which is closer to what the session skill needs.*

## Debrief

1. **What happened in your body in the thirty seconds of silence?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Concrete physical reporting — heart still going, ears ringing, wanting to laugh, feeling suddenly exposed. Not analysis of the exercise. One or two answers and move on.

2. **You just built a huge laugh with nothing funny in the room. What does that tell you about a laugh onstage?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something like: the laugh is the room's state, not a score on my scene. If someone says 'so laughs don't mean anything', accept it and move — the nuance comes later in the session.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    Say the opt-outs aloud before the first sound, not after: mouth it, clap it, or step a metre out of the circle and stay. No judgement attached. Flag it specifically for anyone with a voice strain, hearing sensitivity, migraine, recent chest or abdominal surgery, or late pregnancy. There is no physical contact in this block, so no consent step is required, but check the circle is wide enough that raised arms and bouncing do not connect. Watch for anyone getting genuinely light-headed from sustained loud laughing — it happens occasionally — and let them sit out the terrace. Keep the total under five minutes; the ugliness is fine, the exhaustion is not. If the room is shared, warn neighbours beforehand.

## Why it works

The cue is 'their energy is information — not instruction.' Most players only ever meet crowd energy from the receiving end, where it arrives as a verdict on what they just did. This block puts them on the supply side. They generate the roar, the groan and the gasp on command, with an empty pitch in front of them, which severs the link between the sound and any performance that caused it. Then the hard cut to silence gives them the drop without the shame that normally accompanies it — a room going quiet, and nothing to blame. Having produced both states deliberately, they are less likely to read either as a scoreline later in the session, and more likely to read them as a shape they can track and play against.

# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Last Photo

![Last Photo](../img/advanced_w01__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Everyone describes the last photo on their phone, first in pairs, then in five words to the room.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 01 run-sheet](../week-01.md)

## Why this activity, here

First block of the first week of the long-form term. The room has been apart; some faces are new. This puts every voice in the air inside five minutes with nothing to invent, then hands the room a shared rhythm to hold. It feeds directly into the peripheral-awareness work that follows, where students must track the whole circle rather than one partner.

## What students actually learn

- That they can speak to the whole room without preparing anything, because the material already exists in their pocket.
- That the circle has a pulse, and keeping it is a shared job rather than the facilitator's.
- That listening without replying is a real action, not a gap in the conversation.

## Setup

Standing circle, chairs pushed out. Phones stay in pockets — nobody shows a screen. No props, no music. Know your own last photo so you can model the five-word version. Have a watch or timer you can glance at without breaking the circle.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Get everyone standing in a circle. State the subject and the two rules — no screens, no justifying. Pair the room off; make one three if the numbers are odd. Send them to talk. |
| 1 | Thirty seconds for A, then call the swap and give thirty seconds for B. Keep the pairs to describing what is in the frame. Cut it cleanly on time. |
| 2 | Reform the circle. Point at one person to start and give a direction. Each person gives their photo in five words or fewer, around six seconds. No commentary between speakers. |
| 1 | Show of hands: taken today, has a person in it, a photo of a screen or a receipt. Note what you see out loud in one sentence. Move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As pairs begin describing | *"What's in the frame. Not why you took it."* |
| Thirty seconds in | *"Swap. B now."* |
| First two or three people in the circle round | *"Five words. Then stop."* |
| When someone laughs and the round stalls | *"Keep it moving. Next."* |
| When someone starts adding context | *"That's the photo. Leave it there."* |
| Halfway round the circle | *"Feel the pulse. Come in on it."* |
| Last three people | *"Same tempo. Don't slow down for the end."* |
| Straight after the show of hands | *"Hands down. Stay standing."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The circle round takes about two minutes and never stalls. People come in without being pointed at, because they can feel whose turn is next. Descriptions are flat and concrete — "my dog on the sofa", "the boiler pressure gauge" — and nobody apologises for their photo being boring. Laughs are short and the round keeps going through them.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The circle round runs long and you cross four minutes with people still to go. | People are adding a sentence of context after their five words. | Cut in with "five words, then stop" and physically turn to the next person. Model it once yourself, brutally short. |
| Someone says they have no photos, or nothing they want to say. | Privacy, or an empty camera roll, both entirely reasonable. | Offer the last photo they were sent, or the last screenshot. If still nothing, they say "pass" in rhythm and the round continues. |
| Pairs are showing each other their screens. | Describing is harder than pointing, so people default to the easier thing. | Call "phones away" across the room once. Don't single anyone out. Restate that the description is the work. |
| One or two people give a genuinely personal photo and the room goes quiet and careful. | The prompt occasionally lands on grief, illness or a family thing. | Receive it at the same tempo as everything else and move to the next person. Do not comment on it or make it a moment. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Run the pair stage as written and give the circle round the full two minutes — you will have slack, so let the pulse settle rather than rushing. |
| **14–17** | One circle. Tighten the circle round to five seconds a head and be firmer on cutting commentary. Start pointing at the next person if the round drags. |
| **18–20** | One circle still, but split the circle round into two halves running simultaneously from opposite points — you call the start for both, and both work inward. Cut the pair stage to twenty-five seconds each side. |

## Variations

**Second-to-last photo** — Use the photo before the last one instead. Same structure, same timings.  
*Easier — removes the pressure of whatever happened to be most recent, which people sometimes feel exposed by.*

**Partner's photo** — In the circle round, each person gives their partner's photo in five words instead of their own.  
*Harder — the pair stage becomes real listening, because you have to carry something out of it.*

**One word each** — Circle round is a single word per person, three times round the ring, no repeats.  
*Different emphasis — pushes the group onto shared tempo and peripheral tracking rather than content.*

## Debrief

1. **When did you know it was nearly your turn?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a signal they were reading — two people to the left, a shift in body weight, the pace of the round. That is the peripheral awareness naming itself. Move on.

2. **What was harder — the thirty seconds or the five words?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Split answers with reasons. Someone says the thirty seconds felt long because they ran out of frame to describe; someone else says five words forced a choice. Both are useful. Don't resolve it.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact, no touching, standing circle. Anyone who wants to sit can sit and still take their turn. Phones stay private throughout — nobody is asked to show a screen, and "pass" is always available in rhythm without explanation. If someone's photo touches something heavy, take it at the same tempo as the rest and keep going; do not make it a moment in front of the room. Anyone who prefers not to disclose can describe the last photo they were sent instead.

## Why it works

Peripheral awareness is trained by removing the cue that lets you stop looking. There is no facilitator calling names in the circle round, so knowing when to speak depends on tracking the whole ring — where the round is, how fast it is travelling, who is drawing breath. Because the content requires zero invention, all available attention goes to the timing. The pair stage first gets every voice warm and drops the volume of self-monitoring, so by the circle round nobody is composing while they should be tracking.

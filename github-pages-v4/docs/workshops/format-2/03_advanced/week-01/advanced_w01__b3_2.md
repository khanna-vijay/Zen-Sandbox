# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Third Point

![Third Point](../img/advanced_w01__b3_2.jpg){ .infographic }

> Each person silently keeps an equal distance from two others, so the whole room becomes one slow-moving system nobody is steering.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 01 run-sheet](../week-01.md)

## Why this activity, here

First block of Week 1, straight after the re-contract. The term ahead is long-form, where scenes are built by a group that has to feel each other without discussing anything. This puts that feeling in the body before anyone speaks a line. It feeds every group-mind exercise that follows, and it gives you a shared reference — 'find your third point' — to call back to all term when a scene starts being steered by one person.

## What students actually learn

- They stop needing to look directly at someone to know where that person is.
- They feel what it is like to be adjusted by other people's choices without being told.
- They notice that a room can organise itself with no leader, and that trying to lead it breaks the pattern.
- They arrive in the class body-first, without having had to be clever or funny to be included.

## Setup

Empty floor, roughly two square metres per person. Push chairs and bags to the walls. No props, no music, no talking. Check for anyone with mobility or vision needs before you start — anchors are a real role, not a consolation. You stand outside the mass so you can see the whole shape and call freeze cleanly.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Spread them out. Give the rules: pick two people silently, keep equal distance from both, move slowly, no talking. Confirm nobody says who they picked. Have them pick now. |
| 1 | Call go. Stay out of the mass. Let it churn without comment. The moment it stops moving, call freeze. |
| 1 | Frozen, everyone points one hand at each of their two. Give them five seconds to read the web. Drop arms. New pick, silently. |
| 1 | Call go. This round nobody stops moving. Side-coach soft eyes and peripheral tracking. No staring, no swivelling heads. |
| 1 | Still running, name three anchors who root to the spot. Let the system fail to settle. Call freeze, hold ten seconds, ask one question, move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As they pick, before you call go | *"Keep it secret. Nobody knows who has them."* |
| First ten seconds of round one | *"Slower. Half that speed."* |
| When someone is snapping their head around | *"Soft eyes. Widen out. Take the whole room in at once."* |
| When people bunch in the middle | *"Use the corners. The floor is bigger than you think."* |
| Round two, when someone freezes to think | *"Keep moving. Solve it while you travel."* |
| Just after you name the anchors | *"Anchors, roots down. Everyone else, keep solving."* |
| When the unsettleable round gets frantic | *"Nothing to fix. Just keep answering."* |
| On the final freeze | *"Hold. Feel where everyone is without checking."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    After twenty seconds of chaos the room slows into a wide, even scatter and stops almost together — no one person deciding when. Heads stay fairly still while bodies drift. On the pointing beat there is a quiet laugh of recognition when people see who had them. In the anchored round the drift keeps going, unhurried, with nobody standing around waiting for instructions.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone squashed into a tight knot in the centre of the room. | People default to picking whoever is nearest, and the equal-distance solution collapses inward. | Call 'use the corners' while it runs. On the reset, tell them to pick one person far away and one close. |
| Heads whipping around, people walking backwards into each other. | They are solving the problem with direct vision instead of peripheral vision. | Call 'soft eyes, widen out'. Say it twice. If it persists, have them slow to half speed for ten seconds. |
| Round one never settles inside the minute. | Normal — it often does not settle, especially in a bigger or newer group. | Do not wait it out. Call freeze anyway and go to the pointing beat. Say nothing about it having failed. |
| Talking, laughing, apologising when people bump. | Nerves in week one; silence feels exposing. | Re-state the silence rule once, flatly, without scolding. Do not stop the exercise to do it. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One system, run as written, but use two anchors instead of three — three will freeze the room solid at this size. Round one usually settles inside forty seconds, so you may have a few spare seconds; spend them on a slower reset. |
| **14–17** | One system, three anchors, exactly as written. This is the size the timing is built for. Expect round one to settle right around the freeze call. |
| **18–20** | One system still — do not split it. Push the walls out and claim every metre of floor. Use four anchors. Shorten your instructions in step one to about twenty seconds, because the churn takes longer to develop and you want the drift time. |

## Variations

**One point** — Each person picks a single other and keeps a fixed arm's-length-times-three distance from them. Run one round only.  
*Easier. Halves the tracking load; good if the group is very new or the floor is tight.*

**Hidden roles** — Before you start, whisper to two people that they are to keep one person between them and one specific other person at all times.  
*Harder. Two hidden agendas distort the whole system and the group has to absorb the distortion without knowing why.*

**Sound anchors** — Run the moving round with eyes half-closed. Anchors hum a low continuous note. Everyone else navigates by sound and edge-of-vision only.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts from visual periphery to whole-body orientation; useful before listening-heavy work.*

## Debrief

1. **When did you stop looking and start knowing?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a moment — 'about halfway through the second round I could feel her behind me' — rather than describing the rules again. One or two answers is enough. Move on.

2. **What changed when the anchors went in?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They describe the system as a whole, not their own struggle: it stopped resolving, it kept generating work, they gave up on finishing. If someone says 'it felt like failing', say that is the point of a long-form and move.

3. **Who was steering that?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Nobody. A pause, then a few people say it at once. If someone claims they were, ask whether the room agreed — usually it did not.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact is required or expected. Tell them at the top: if you end up close to someone, adjust rather than touch. Bumps happen — say sorry with a nod, keep going. Anyone with reduced mobility, low vision, or who does not want to move in a crowd takes an anchor role for the whole exercise; offer this before you start, quietly and as a normal option, not as an exemption. Anyone who wants out sits at the wall and watches; they can point during the freeze if they picked two people. Watch the floor for bags and chair legs before you call go.

## Why it works

D4.S1 is peripheral awareness, and the cue is knowing where everyone is without looking. Direct vision can only hold one person at a time, so the moment a task requires continuous knowledge of two moving people, direct vision fails and the body recruits the periphery instead. Nobody has to be told to do it; the task removes the alternative. The secrecy matters — because you cannot know who is tracking you, you cannot solve it by negotiation, only by constant small adjustment. And the anchored round removes the possibility of a settled answer, which is exactly the state a long-form runs in: a system that keeps generating adjustments and never resolves. The group learns the feeling before it has to name it.

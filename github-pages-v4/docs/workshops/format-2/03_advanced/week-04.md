# Week 04 — The Harold — Beats, Groups, Returns

> *A structure is not a cage; it is a promise that what you started will come back changed.*

| Course | Week | Domain | Focus | Stage | Length | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Piece — Long-Form & Audience | 4/8 | D4 | `D4.S6` — Format Literacy | Competent → Proficient | 180 min | 10–20 |

!!! note "Builds on"
    Week 3 taught them to fill the well; this week gives that material a shape with returns built into it.

## 🎬 The arc of this session

They arrive believing in openings — last week's montage held together and they know a well can be filled. This week the material gets a spine: the same three scenes come back twice, later and under more pressure, with nobody calling the shots from the side. They leave having run three beats without a map, and able to say out loud what changed between beat one and beat three.

!!! tip "Watch for"
    Watch what happens in the second after a scene ends. If eyes come to you, the format is still your property and not theirs. Say nothing, hold the pause, let it get slightly uncomfortable — then note who moves first, because that person is doing the work you want spread across the room by block 9.

## 🎒 Before you start

- Clear the widest possible playing space. You need a back line where eight to ten people can stand without crowding, and a clean edge they can step out from. Chairs stacked or pushed to one wall — no chair-based scenes today.
- Chalk or tape a faint back line on the floor if the room has no natural one. Groups drift forward during Harolds and the drift eats the space.
- Write the shape on the whiteboard before anyone arrives: OPENING / 1a 2a 3a / GROUP / 1b 2b 3b / GROUP / 1c 2c 3c. Leave it up all session. Do not annotate it or add arrows — the bare shape is the teaching.
- Re-read your notes from Week 3 on who filled the well well and who went quiet in openings. You will need those names when you build showcase teams in block 9.
- Print or write out four genre prompts for the Scene Lab and keep them face down.
- Have a timer you can see without looking down. Block 9 runs uninterrupted and you will be tempted to talk.

## 1. 🧊 Ice-breaker (5 min)

*Loose circle, standing. Nothing to solve here, just say what is still open.*

**Why here:** Gets them naming the unfinished things they are carrying, which is exactly the mental habit a Harold asks for — holding several threads open at once.

### Open Tabs

> Everyone reads out the browser tabs they actually have open right now, one lap round the circle, no explaining.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand everyone in a circle, phones in hand. Tell them to look at the tabs actually open right now — real ones. Anything private gets skipped, no questions asked.
2. Have each person turn to the neighbour on their left and, both talking at once, list three tabs. Everyone speaks. Nobody listens carefully. That is fine.
3. Run one lap of the circle. Each person names one tab, title only, roughly seven seconds. No context, no apology, no story. Keep the lap moving.
4. Run a second lap, same order, same tab. Each person says only 'keeping it' or 'closing it'. Two words. Cut anyone who explains.
5. Close by naming two tabs from the room you would like opened. Move straight into the warm-up without discussion.

## 2. 😄 Laughter Blast (5 min)

*Same circle, tighter. This one is loud and it is meant to be — nobody is being clever.*

**Why here:** Five minutes of shared noise before a demanding session; it drops the self-monitoring that will otherwise make second beats cautious.

### Rolling Boil

> The whole room crouches down as kettles, hums up through the pitches and boils over into laughing.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand everyone in one circle, arms free. Tell them this is noise, not comedy — nobody has to be funny, and anyone can hum quietly or sit out and clap the pulse.
2. Demonstrate alone at full volume: crouch low, hum your lowest note, rise slowly as the hum climbs, break into a kettle whistle at the top, then laugh it out.
3. Run round one all together. Crouch, lowest hum, count the climb up over eight seconds, whistle at the top, shake out. Tell them cheerfully that round one is always thin.
4. Run round two slower and louder. Hold the whistle until breath runs out, then drop the whole room into ten seconds of laughing. Laugh loudest and longest yourself.
5. Run round three identically, but at the top nobody stops. Keep a belly laugh going for twenty seconds while bouncing on the spot. Count the seconds out loud.
6. Call the simmer. Everyone stays standing and bubbles — small random 'pff' and 'ha' sounds, no pattern, all at once — until the room is one soft rolling boil.
7. Cut the simmer with a single clap. Shake hands out, ask one question, take answers shouted over each other, and move straight into the next block.

**Side-coach:** *“Let it build. Don't cap it early.”*

## 3. 🔥 Warm-up cluster (15 min)

*Three short things back to back. Each one is a muscle you will need in about ninety minutes.*

**Why here:** Three warm-ups that each rehearse one piece of the return: escalation, time-jump, and holding three separate ideas alive at once.

### Come Back Bigger

> Three standing groups relay one move through three beats, each return larger and later than the last.

`Players 8–24` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Split the room into three groups. Stand them in a wide triangle, facing the centre, so every group can see the other two clearly.
2. Start everyone jogging or marching on the spot. Tell them the engine stays running for the whole five minutes, including between beats.
3. Demonstrate a two-second move with a sound — a shove and a grunt. Say the rule: three groups, three beats, one move, later and bigger each time.
4. Clap. Group A invents a move and does it in unison. Clap again: Group B repeats A's move, heightened. Clap: Group C heightens it further.
5. Run the same three-beat relay with Group B originating, then Group C. Clap the beats roughly four seconds apart. Nobody invents on beats two or three.
6. Speed up. Clap every two seconds through another full cycle. Same rule: return the move you were given, do not replace it.
7. Finish with Group A's first move. All three groups perform it together at full size, then stop dead. Hold three seconds of silence before you speak.

### Ten Years Later

> Pairs make one small gesture and line, then meet it again after time jumps — same beat, later and truer.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Pair everyone up. Odd number: make one three, where the third person partners with both in turn.
2. Tell A to make one small ordinary gesture with one short line — wiping a counter, saying 'nearly closing'. B copies it back exactly. Swap roles.
3. Tell them each gesture-and-line is now their own beat, kept for the whole exercise. Everyone says their beat once, all at the same time.
4. Call 'ten minutes later'. Each partner replays their own beat, same gesture, same words, changed only by ten minutes. They watch each other between goes.
5. Call 'ten years later'. Same gesture, same words again. Tell them to let the time do the work, not the volume.
6. Call 'last time ever'. One final replay each. Then they hold still and look at their partner.
7. Ask for hands: whose partner's beat meant something different by the end? Take two answers. No discussion. Move on.

### Three Seeds

> Plant three images in a silent circle, then bring each one back twice — later each time — with only one voice allowed at a time.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand the group in one circle, shoulders loose, no chairs. Tell them three images get planted, then each returns twice, later in time each return.
2. Set the traffic rule: one voice at a time. If two people speak together, both step back, say nothing, and that seed waits for the next call.
3. Ask three volunteers for one seed each — a single concrete image in one breath. 'A rusted kettle.' No story, no joke, no punchline. Just the image.
4. Call for stillness. Eyes soft, gaze to the middle. Tell them to hold all three images and plan no sentence.
5. Call 'Seed one', then two, then three. For each, one person steps forward a pace, says one sentence placing that image later in time, steps back.
6. Call the same three seeds in the same order again. Second returns land later still and must acknowledge what the first return changed. A different speaker each time.
7. Close in silence. Ask each person to name inside their head the one seed they would carry into a Harold today. Break the circle straight into the work.

## 4. 🧠 Theory — Format Literacy (20 min)

![Format Literacy](../../../assets/infographics/D4.S6.jpg){ .infographic }

- **The big idea:** A structure is a promise that what you started will come back changed. Three scenes go out, three scenes come back, three scenes come back again — and each return carries the same game further, not the same game repeated.
- **Where you are on the path:** They are competent scene players who can fill a well and can play a montage. What they cannot yet do is hold a scene in memory while playing two others, then re-enter it without a plan. Expect competence in the first beats and visible panic at the first return.
- **The one cue to coach:** *“Beat two is the same game — later, and truer.”*

**The three beats**

1. **Say it.** Put it in three sentences and stop. "A Harold is nine scenes in three waves, with a group scene between the waves. Every scene you start in wave one, you will come back to in wave two and again in wave three. Beat two is the same game — later, and truer." Then point at the board and say nothing for five seconds. Do not explain deconstruction, do not explain the history, do not explain what happens if a beat fails.
2. **Show it.** Take two volunteers. Give them a game in one line: a man apologises for something he clearly enjoyed doing. Run beat one for forty seconds, cut it. Then say "beat two" and run the same two players for forty seconds — but before they start, tell the room the only rule: nothing resets. Whatever the apology cost him in beat one has now landed. The room will see the difference between a rerun and an advance without you naming it. Then name it in one sentence.
3. **Try it.** Everyone into pairs, all at once, no watching. Sixty seconds: play a scene where one person wants something small from the other. Call cut. Thirty seconds standing still: each pair agrees on one sentence — what is the game. Then sixty seconds: play beat two, same two people, same game, later. Call cut and ask three pairs only what changed. Keep the answers to one sentence each.

!!! warning "The misunderstanding that always comes up"
    They hear "same game" and play the same scene — same location, same opening line, same beats, slightly louder. Say it plainly: the game is the pattern between the people, not the situation around them. Beat two can be a different room, a different year, different stakes, as long as the same pattern is now costing more.

## 5. 🎲 Drill A — isolation (20 min)

*Back line, everyone up. We are only making material — nobody has to be in a scene.*

**Why here:** The opening is the well; before they can run returns they need enough distinct material on the wall to return to.

### The Open Canvas

> Navigate a seamless, self-directed longform set using only organic edits and collective intuition.

`Players 2–99` · `~20 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Line the ensemble across the back of the playing space. Take one word from the room. Hold three seconds of silence before anyone moves.
2. Agree the tag before you start: a hand on the shoulder or upper back. Anyone who would rather not be touched steps into eyeline and says "tag" instead.
3. Send one or two players out to build a platform — who they are, where they are, what is happening — coloured by the word.
4. Tell the backline to watch rather than plan. They are listening for a repeated idea, an emotional shape, or the point where the scene has said its thing.
5. Let any backline player edit at will: sweep across the front to clear the stage, tag a player out, or start a fresh scene on the opposite side.
6. Free the next scene from the story. Parallel lives, echoes, analogies and flat abstractions of what just happened are all legal.
7. Ask for returns. A character, a place or a dynamic from earlier comes back in a new context — same game, further down the line.
8. Have the group vary the temperature. After a loud scene, someone takes a slow one. Nobody announces this; they just do it.
9. End on a return that lands. One player sweeps, or the whole group steps back into the line together, and the set is over.

**Side-coach:** *“Take something already said and turn it over. Don't start a new subject.”*

## ☕ Break — 10 minutes

Water, notes, informal talk. Non-negotiable at three hours — do not let it collapse into a working discussion.

## 6. 🎲 Drill B — under load (20 min)

*Same idea as before, but now the threads interrupt each other and you have to pick one back up mid-air.*

**Why here:** Three threads under load, cut against each other — the first time today they must hold a scene in memory while playing a different one.

### Braided Narratives

> Interlace multiple distinct storylines to discover unexpected thematic connections and build a cohesive ensemble piece.

`Players 3–99` · `~20 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Split the room into pairs and trios. Give each group a letter — A, B, C — and a fixed running order. Every group is one storyline for the whole drill.
2. Send group A out first. Tell them to establish two characters, one location and one clear activity inside sixty seconds. No plot twists, no exposition dumps.
3. At your call, a player from group B walks into the space. A's players clear immediately, mid-sentence if needed. B starts their own scene from nothing.
4. Run through every group in order until each thread has one scene on the floor. That completes cycle one.
5. Send A back on. Tell them to resume the same relationship — either exactly where they stopped, or years later. Same characters, same game, new position on it.
6. Cycle again in the identical order, A then B then C, every time. Never reorder. Players edit each other by stepping in; you call the beat if nobody moves.
7. From cycle two, tell players to listen while offstage and carry one thing across — a phrase, an object, an emotional temperature — into their own thread without commenting on it.
8. Stop after three cycles. Give each thread a final short beat and let the room end on the last group's last line.

**Side-coach:** *“You are re-entering, not restarting. Come in after the gap, not before it.”*

## 7. 🎯 Scene Lab (35 min)

*Groups of five. You get a genre and you get one wave, then you get the same wave again with everything gone further.*

**Why here:** Genre gives them a container with built-in expectations, so the return has something to press against and they can feel the shape without the full nine-scene load.

### The Genre Blueprint

> Deconstruct a specific film genre through audience-sourced tropes, then weave them into a cohesive feature.

`Players 4–99` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Ask the room for a narrow film genre or a director's signature style. Reject broad answers like 'action' or 'comedy'. Push until you get something with a visible look and sound.
2. Harvest twenty to thirty specifics from the room: tropes, character types, props, locations, stock situations, lines. Write every one on the whiteboard in large letters.
3. Run Part One as a collage. Each short scene must lean hard on at least one item from the board. Call the cut yourself; keep them coming fast.
4. Tell players Part One scenes do not connect. No shared characters, no plot. Each vignette exists to show off the genre's vocabulary and land one clear moment.
5. Stop the collage. Ask the watchers to vote on their favourite scene. That scene becomes the Anchor.
6. Replay the Anchor exactly. Invite watchers to call out lines, blocking or details the players missed. Accuracy matters more than improvement here.
7. Run Part Two as one continuous story in the genre, with a beginning and an end. Place the Anchor somewhere inside it — inciting incident, midpoint or climax, not the opening.
8. Keep the board live. Coach players to pull unused items into the story as it runs. Cross each one off when it lands.

**Side-coach:** *“The genre is the wallpaper. The game is still two people and a pattern.”*

## 8. 🏟️ Showcase round (40 min)

*Teams of eight. One word from the room, then I say nothing until it is over. Nobody is going to save you and that is the point.*

**Why here:** A full Harold, one word, no interruptions — so they feel the actual size of the form before they decide anything about it.

### One Word, Three Waves

> A structured long-form set built from a single audience word: three waves of scenes, two group pieces, one shared ending.

`Players 10–20` · `~40 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Line the cast across the back wall, faces up, visible throughout. Send one player forward as host to greet the audience and ask for one ordinary word.
2. Have the host repeat the word twice, clearly, then step back into the line. Nobody moves for three full seconds of silence.
3. Run the opening: three minutes off the word. Spoken images, short physical tableaux, overheard lines. No scenes, no characters yet. It ends when someone steps out and starts a scene.
4. Wave one: three two-handed scenes, unrelated to each other, each pulled from something the opening threw up. End each scene by sweeping across the stage or tagging a player out.
5. Group piece one: the whole cast builds a three-minute moving picture of one place, idea or ritual already seen. Anyone may start it. Everyone joins within ten seconds.
6. Wave two: revisit the same three scenes later in time. Same characters, changed situation. Then run group piece two the same way, built from a different thread.
7. Wave three: three scenes that collide the earlier threads. Characters from different scenes meet, or one scene's rule invades another. Keep the beats shorter and faster.
8. Close: the cast finds one final image or line that references the opening word. Host calls the cast forward. Hold the line, bow together, exit.

**Side-coach:** *“Say nothing during the run. Between teams, only ask: what changed between beat one and beat three.”*

## 9. 💭 Reflection & homework (10 min)

**Deepen your improv**
1. Name a beat two you played today that was a rerun. What would have advanced it instead?
2. When you came back to a scene, what did you actually remember — the location, the lines, or the pattern between the people? What does that tell you about what to hold on to next time?

**Beyond the stage**
3. Think of a conversation you have had more than once with the same person. Is it a rerun, or has it advanced? What would advancing it require of you?

??? note "🎓 Facilitator notes"

    **Timing risks**
    - Theory runs long every time. The try-it pairs will want to keep going and you will want to explain more. Cut both at the stated time — the drills teach it better than you will.
    - Block 9 is forty minutes for two or three full Harolds plus notes. At 18-20 that is three teams and you must hold each run to roughly ten minutes and each note to two. Set the timer where you can see it.
    - The Scene Lab overruns because groups negotiate genre for too long. Hand out the prompts face down and give them thirty seconds to look and go.

    **Failure modes this week**
    - Second beats as verbatim reruns. This is the standard failure this week, not a sign of a weak group. Name it in the room the first time it happens, take one minute, then run it again immediately with the same players.
    - Players waiting on the back line for permission. If nobody initiates for more than five seconds, do not call a name — let the pause run once, then afterwards ask the group what stopped them.
    - Group scenes becoming chaos with nine people shouting. Give one instruction only: find the pattern in the room and join it, don't add a new one.
    - Someone treats the whiteboard shape as a checklist and starts narrating position — "this is 2b". Stop that once, gently. The shape is a promise, not a scoreboard.

    **What good looks like by the end:** By the end, at least two teams have completed three waves without you speaking during the run. Second beats are recognisably later — more cost, more truth, not more volume. When you ask what changed between beat one and beat three, the answers are specific and about people, not about plot.

    **Carry into next week:** Note which groups treated returns as escalation and which treated them as deepening — next week's work on endings depends on knowing which instinct each team defaults to. Also note the two or three players who initiated returns without prompting; they are your engines for the final piece.

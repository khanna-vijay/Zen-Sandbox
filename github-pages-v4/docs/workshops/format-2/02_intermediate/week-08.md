# Week 08 — The Public Showcase — Match or Genre Night

> *A show is something you do for the people in the room, and every choice on stage either serves that or serves you.*

| Course | Week | Domain | Focus | Stage | Length | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Engine — Scene & Ensemble | 8/8 | D5 | `D5.S2` — Audience-Energy Management | Advanced Beginner → Competent | 180 min | 10–20 |

!!! note "Builds on"
    Last week's dress run showed them exactly where the format breaks; this week they run it for strangers, with you in the back row and off the floor.

## 🎬 The arc of this session

They arrive over-caffeinated and slightly sick, carrying last week's dress run in which the format broke twice in the same place. Across the morning the teaching shrinks and the logistics grow, until by the second half they are setting the room, warming themselves and covering their own gaps while you sit in the back row. They leave having run a public show for strangers without you on the floor, able to name which choices served the room and which served themselves.

!!! tip "Watch for"
    Eyes flicking to you before every decision. From Block 7 onwards, stop answering — look back blankly, or look at the host. The single measurable thing today is whether the show starts on time without your say-so.

## 🎒 Before you start

- Set the house by 08:45: playing space marked with tape, audience chairs in two shallow arcs, two backline rows of chairs facing in, a small table at the door for the sign-in list.
- Props out: a timer with an audible bell, a whiteboard with the running order and job names blank, suggestion slips and a bowl, scorecards or paddles for The Scoreline, water for both backlines, spare marker, a chair for you in the back row with your name on it.
- Put last week's two format breaks on the board in plain words before anyone arrives — the handover between rounds and the suggestion-taking — so they read them on the way in.
- Print the jobs list and pin it by the door: host, timekeeper, referee/scorekeeper, door, suggestions, music, one backline captain per team. Names go on it before the ice-breaker, chosen by them.
- Sizes: 10-13 — two teams of 5-6, four rounds, host doubles as timekeeper. 14-17 — two teams of 7-8, five rounds, separate host and timekeeper. 18-20 — two teams of 9-10, five rounds with the backline rotating on at the halfway bell, plus a dedicated door and suggestions person; nobody sits out a whole show.
- Consent: if any game in the running order involves contact, call it and take opt-outs before doors open. Name the tap-out to the room and to the audience once, briefly, in the host's opening.

## 1. 🧊 Ice-breaker (5 min)

*Up, no chairs, we start in ten seconds — take a position and stand by it.*

**Why here:** Puts a decision in every mouth in the first minute, before nerves harden into politeness.

### Stand By It

> People place themselves on an invisible line between two real habits, then tell the neighbour beside them why they landed there.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Point at one wall, then the far wall. Name each end as an extreme. Tell them anywhere between counts, but the exact middle is banned — pick a side, however slightly.
2. Call prompt one: cancelling plans and feeling fine at one wall, never having cancelled anything at the other. They walk and stand.
3. Give them twenty seconds each with whoever is nearest. One true sentence on why they landed there. Then stop them.
4. Call prompt two: singing in the car at full volume against total silence in the car. Walk, place, find a new neighbour, one sentence each.
5. Call prompt three: wanting people you know in tonight's audience against preferring complete strangers. Walk, place, new neighbour, one sentence each.
6. Hold the line where it is. Starting at one end, each person calls five words or fewer on that last prompt. Go straight along. No commentary, no reactions.
7. Tell them to look at the shape of the line. Then break to the circle. No discussion.

## 2. 😄 Laughter Blast (5 min)

*Shake it out. Sound before sense — nobody has to be funny for this.*

**Why here:** Show-day adrenaline needs somewhere to go that isn't chatter or speed.

### Catching Hiccups

> The whole room fakes contagious whole-body hiccups until the breathing goes ragged and the faces go loose.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand where everyone can see you. Give one huge fake hiccup — knees buckle, elbows fly, the sound comes out of your chest, not your throat. Repeat it three times, bigger each time.
2. Say: on your spot, now. Everyone hiccups roughly every two seconds, whole body each time. No unison, no rhythm. Keep hiccupping yourself throughout.
3. Open the floor. Tell them hiccups are catching: when one lands near you, hiccup back bigger within a second. Keep moving. Stay an arm's length from everyone.
4. Call the pitch ladder. Every ten seconds hiccups climb higher and squeakier. Lead the climb yourself until the room sounds like mice.
5. Call the cure. Mouths shut, bodies freeze, everyone holds it in. Count down from five aloud.
6. On zero, the whole room releases one giant hiccup and lets it fall apart into laughing.
7. Shake arms out. Ask one question, take thirty seconds of answers, move straight on.

**Side-coach:** *“Let the laugh come from the group, not from a joke you made.”*

## 3. 🔥 Warm-up cluster (15 min)

*Three in a row, fifteen minutes, no notes from me between them. Whoever's hosting tonight, you call the changeovers.*

**Why here:** The three warm-ups walk the shape of a show — raise the energy, give it away, land it down — so they feel the arc in their bodies before they have to build it for strangers.

### Lift the Room

> Two facing lines trade places as crowd and stage, scaling noise and body size to a called dial and cutting it dead on command.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Split the room into two lines facing each other, about two metres apart. Name them out loud: Line A is the crowd, Line B is the stage. No guessing.
2. Teach the dial. Call a number from one to five; the crowd claps and stomps at that size. Run it up to five and back down once.
3. Have each person in Line B choose one big repeating action — digging, waving, punching the air. Tell them the action is fixed for the round.
4. Call numbers at random. Line B scales its action to the crowd's number, not to its own preference. Louder crowd, bigger body. Quieter crowd, smaller body.
5. Add the landing. On your call of 'Land it', the crowd cuts to silence in one beat and Line B freezes clean. Practise it twice before moving on.
6. Swap sides. Line A takes the stage, Line B becomes the crowd. Same dial, same landing, calls come faster.
7. Run a last round with no calls from you. The stage side pushes or pulls the energy on its own; the crowd answers honestly. Call 'Land it' once inside twenty seconds and stop.

### Not Mine Anymore

> In pairs facing the same wall, one player makes a tiny offer and the other sends it out to an imaginary crowd, so the credit lands on the room rather than the maker.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Pick one wall as the crowd wall. Everyone faces it. Pair up quickly — a trio is fine if numbers are odd.
2. Demo with one volunteer: make a small sound and gesture. They copy the shape and play it big to the back wall.
3. Name the rule out loud: the giver stays small, the receiver sends it out. Credit lands on the room, not the maker.
4. Round one: A offers something tiny every few seconds. B takes each one and throws it to the crowd wall. A never grows.
5. Swap. B gives small, A sends it wide. Same pace. No discussing it between rounds — just go.
6. Round three: drop fixed roles. Whoever gives goes small, whoever receives goes wide. Aim for a handover every three seconds.
7. Call freeze. Everyone faces the crowd wall. One shared breath out towards it. Move straight on.

### Ride It Down

> The room supplies a manufactured laugh wave and the player holds still until it hits the floor, then speaks.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand the group in a loose arc facing one open spot. Tell them they are the audience and the laugh is a machine you switch on, not a reaction.
2. Show the signal: your hand rising means the whole arc laughs warmly, your hand lowering fades it to full silence. Run one wave together, up and down.
3. Take the spot yourself. Say one flat sentence — 'I've brought the wrong keys.' Conduct a wave. Stand completely still through it. Speak your second sentence on the exact bottom.
4. Round one: players take the spot one at a time, two plain sentences each, your wave between. Nobody attempts to be funny. Rotate fast, no comment between turns.
5. Round two: same rotation, but the player raises their own hand to start the wave and decides when to cut it and speak. Stay silent yourself.
6. Last pass: vary the wave without warning. Some get a huge one, some a tiny one, some nothing. They hold and continue either way.
7. Let anyone pass the spot and stay in the arc. Passing costs nothing and needs no reason.

## 4. 🧠 Theory — Audience-Energy Management (20 min)

![Audience-Energy Management](../../../assets/infographics/D5.S2.jpg){ .infographic }

- **The big idea:** Audience energy is information, not a verdict. You read what the room is giving you and spend it on the show; you do not collect it for yourself.
- **Where you are on the path:** They can build a scene and hold a format. What they cannot yet do is notice the room mid-scene and adjust without panicking or performing at it. Expect competence with a nervous edge.
- **The one cue to coach:** *“Serve the show, not your bit.”*

**The three beats**

1. **Say it.** Say it in eight minutes, no more. "Tonight there are people in the room who did not come to watch you learn. Every choice you make on stage either serves the show or serves your bit. Your bit is the thing you know will land — the voice, the callback, the character you've been saving. Serving the show is doing the thing this room needs in the next three seconds, even when it's not yours." Then add the honest bit: something will go wrong. The audience forgives a bad scene, a dropped game, a wrong buzzer. They do not forgive stalling. Then spend the remaining twelve minutes on the running order, jobs and the two breaks on the board.
2. **Show it.** Take two volunteers. Give them a suggestion and let them play eight seconds. Freeze. Replay the same eight seconds twice: first, one of them chases the laugh they just got by repeating it; second, they take the laugh and move to the next thing. Ask the room which one they would rather watch for twenty minutes. Do not explain further.
3. **Try it.** Threes. One is the host, two are the room. The host has thirty seconds to cover a gap between rounds — no game, just talk to the room and hand off cleanly. Rotate twice so everyone hosts once. The only rule: no dead air and no apologising. Five minutes, then stop.

!!! warning "The misunderstanding that always comes up"
    They hear "serve the show" as "be small, feed lines, don't take space." Correct it out loud: sometimes the show needs the biggest swing in the room, and refusing to take it is also hoarding. Serving is about direction of the choice, not size.

## 5. 🎲 Drill A — isolation (25 min)

*Same skill, stripped back — one person steering the room's attention, everyone else supplying it.*

**Why here:** Isolates the one skill of the week: noticing what the room is giving you and steering it deliberately, with nothing else to manage.

### The Fourth Wall Conductor

> Direct and redirect the fourth wall in real-time to master audience connection and energy.

`Players 3–99` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Take a one-word suggestion from the seated group to start each scene.
2. Choose one Conductor. Stand them at the side of the playing area, facing the audience, not the players. Their job is watching the room.
3. Teach two signals. Flat vertical palm, held up toward the players, is Wall Up. Open hand sweeping outward toward the seats is Wall Down.
4. Start every scene Wall Up. Players build a self-contained scene, attention fully on each other, playing as though nobody is watching.
5. Tell the Conductor to scan for laughter, leaning in, seat-shifting, drifting eyes. Drop the wall on a dip, a comic peak, or when the story has gone murky.
6. On Wall Down, players pivot instantly to direct address, still in character. Open the body out, voice to the back row, ride any laugh before speaking again.
7. On Wall Up, players cut the address dead and return to the scene's reality. Treat what they just told the audience as private, unspoken thought.
8. Run each scene five to seven minutes with several switches. Call 'Scene!' to end it, then rotate players and Conductor.

## 6. 🎲 Drill B — under load (25 min)

*I'm in the back row from now. Host, it's yours — call it when you're ready.*

**Why here:** Same skill with the format's demands loaded on top, and the first block you sit out — this is where you move to the back row and stay there.

### The Fourth Wall Weave

> Master the boundary between stage and spectator by dynamically toggling direct address based on live audience energy.

`Players 3–99` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Take one open suggestion from the room — a place or an activity. Refuse anything clever or loaded. Two players start a scene from it.
2. Hold up the green card to open. Green means a sealed fourth wall: play only to each other, but stay open-angled, audible and upstage-free.
3. Swap to the red card at unpredictable intervals, between fifteen and forty-five seconds. No warning, no pattern.
4. On red, at least one player steps forward within two seconds and speaks directly to the audience — one short, pointed line or question.
5. Tell players the direct address must read the room. Laughter: ride it. Silence: name the tension. Confusion: give one clarifying fact about your character.
6. Return the green card. Players re-seal the wall immediately and continue the scene, absorbing whatever the audience just gave them.
7. Ring the bell whenever you need to freeze the action. Give one note on calibration, projection or transition speed, then restart from the frozen position.

## 7. 🎯 Scene Lab (35 min)

*Thirty-five minutes. Run the two rounds that broke last week, twice each, and fix the handover between them.*

**Why here:** Last chance to break the format in private; push them to fluctuate the wall deliberately rather than accidentally, and to fix the two handovers on the board.

### Fourth Wall Fluctuations

> Deliver a monologue while dynamically adjusting your performance to real-time, visual audience feedback cards.

`Players 2–99` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Give each group three cards in big capitals — PROJECT, CONNECT, CLARIFY — plus a bell, chime or keys to shake. Name one person Audience Proxy for each turn.
2. Send one performer up. They begin a monologue with the fourth wall closed: talking to themselves or to an imagined listener, not to the room.
3. Every twenty to forty-five seconds the Proxy chimes and holds up one card. The performer takes it mid-sentence — no stopping, no apologising, no dropping the material.
4. On PROJECT: open the stance, put breath behind the voice, aim every word at the furthest person in the room.
5. On CONNECT: break the wall. Find individual faces, hold eye contact, say the private thought out loud to those people.
6. On CLARIFY: slow down, still the fidgeting, make gestures large and readable, and restate the last idea in plainer words.
7. Tell the Proxy to move — back row, side wall, corner — so the performer must re-aim voice and body while still speaking.
8. Run four to six cards per turn. Call time. Have the performer finish on a CONNECT beat, delivered straight to the room.

## 8. 🏟️ Showcase round (30 min)

*Doors in five. I'm sitting down now — I won't be answering anything after this sentence.*

**Why here:** The public show: hosted, timed, refereed and reset entirely by them, with you seated and silent.

### The Scoreline

> Two teams play the same timed improv game one after the other while a referee whistles fouls and the audience applauds a winner.

`Players 10–20` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Open the show with three house rules: applaud both teams, shout suggestions only when asked, the referee's word is final. Referee introduces both benches and blows the whistle once so everyone knows the sound.
2. Referee names the four fouls, one whistle each, one point to the other team: blocking a partner's offer, waffling instead of choosing, talking over a partner, laughing at someone's expense.
3. Hold a bank of six games: Open Scene, Story One Word, Emotion Dial, One Prop, Tag Scene, Expert. Run five inside the block and drop from the end if you overrun.
4. Run every round the same way. Referee names the game, reads its rule aloud, takes one audience suggestion, then both teams play that same suggestion in turn. Alternate which bench starts.
5. Open Scene: two players, ninety seconds, audience supplies a place. Story One Word: the whole team lines up and tells a story with the given title, one word each.
6. Emotion Dial: two players, ninety seconds, referee calls a number one to ten and they play at that intensity. One Prop: players take turns turning a chair into other objects.
7. Tag Scene: a two-hander runs, teammates tap a player out and open a fresh scene from the last line spoken. Expert: one player answers interview questions on an audience topic.
8. Score after each round. Referee raises a hand over each bench, audience applauds, the louder side takes two points and the other takes one. Scorekeeper flips the numbers. Final round scores double.
9. Close by reading the total. Both teams bow together. Then name the referee, scorekeeper, tech and front of house in turn and let each of them bow.

## 9. 💭 Reflection & homework (10 min)

**Deepen your improv**
1. Name one moment tonight where you took something the room gave you and spent it on the show, and one where you kept it. What was different about the second one?
2. Something went wrong. Who covered it, how fast, and what did the rest of you do in that three seconds?

**Beyond the stage**
3. Where else do you do your bit — the reliable story, the joke you know works — when the room in front of you needs something else? What would spending that attention outwards look like on Monday?

??? note "🎓 Facilitator notes"

    **Timing risks**
    - Theory will swallow the logistics. Hard-stop the talking at eight minutes; the running order and job names must be finished on the board before the break.
    - Audience arrival is the real variable. If doors slip, cut Scene Lab by five minutes rather than the showcase — never start the show late to protect a drill.
    - Scene Lab overruns because they want one more pass. Call time at thirty minutes and give the last five to resetting the house and the backline chairs.

    **Failure modes this week**
    - They stall between rounds and look at you. Do not rescue. The silence is the lesson and it costs about six seconds.
    - One or two strong players start collecting laughs and stop passing. Note the moment, name it in reflection, not during the show.
    - The host over-explains the format to the audience and burns two minutes. Fix it in the pre-doors huddle: one sentence per game, maximum.
    - Nerves come out as speed — everyone talks over the suggestion and nobody hears it. Have the suggestions person repeat every suggestion back to the room, every time.

    **What good looks like by the end:** The bell goes on the advertised minute without you saying anything. Gaps between rounds are under five seconds. When something breaks — and it will — somebody covers it inside three seconds and the audience never learns it was a mistake. Afterwards they can tell you, specifically and without prompting, which of their own choices served the room and which served themselves.

    **Carry into next week:** Write down the two moments where the format sagged and who covered them. Next week those names lead the repair, and the people who hoarded get the host and timekeeper jobs.

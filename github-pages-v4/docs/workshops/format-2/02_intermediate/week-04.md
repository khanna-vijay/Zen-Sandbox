# Week 04 — Platform, Tilt & the Story Spine

> *A story is a pattern set up, broken on purpose, and then made to make sense.*

| Course | Week | Domain | Focus | Stage | Length | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Engine — Scene & Ensemble | 4/8 | D3 | `D3.S3` — Narrative Architecture | Advanced Beginner → Competent | 180 min | 10–20 |

!!! note "Builds on"
    Wants gave them a reason to keep talking; this week gives the talking a shape over time — routine, break, consequence.

## 🎬 The arc of this session

They arrive able to hold a scene alive for two minutes on want alone, and they think that is the whole job. Today they learn that a scene has a shape over time — a pattern built on purpose, broken on purpose, and then paid for — and the building is the part they will want to skip. They leave able to run a routine three times, tilt it once, and let the world absorb the tilt instead of explaining it away, and with the first taste of a timed competitive format under their belt.

!!! tip "Watch for"
    The confident ones breaking the routine on line two because breaking is more fun than building. Make them count the pattern out loud in warm-ups so they cannot pretend they did not notice. The quieter half has the opposite problem — they will build the routine happily for five minutes and never tilt it; give them a hard signal, not encouragement.

## 🎒 Before you start

- Clear a playing space with a clear front. Chairs in a shallow arc, not a full circle — from Drill A onward they need an audience side.
- Whiteboard or flipchart. Write the cue at the top before anyone arrives: "Break the routine — then make it make sense." Under it leave room for three columns: ROUTINE / BREAK / CONSEQUENCE. You will fill these live in theory.
- Timer with an audible end (phone alarm is fine) and something to call time with — a bell, a clap, a whistle. You need it for Block 9 and for the 90-second resets in Drill B.
- Print or write the three Scoreline penalties on the board before Block 9: WAFFLE, BLOCK, DULL. Do not explain them until you are in the block.
- Review last week's homework on wants. Take two minutes at the top of theory to link it: the want is why they keep talking, the routine is what the talking looks like when nothing has gone wrong yet.
- Group splits by size, decided now, not in the room: 10-13 runs as one circle in Drill A and one playing area throughout; 14-17 splits into two circles for Drill A and two Scoreline teams with players tagging in; 18-20 runs three circles in Drill A, two simultaneous halves in Scene Lab with you rotating, and two Scoreline teams of nine or ten with a fixed four on the floor per round.

## 1. 🧊 Ice-breaker (5 min)

*On your feet, loose circle, no thinking ahead. We are swapping things out for other things and we are going to be wrong a lot.*

**Why here:** Gets them substituting one thing for another in a low-stakes way, which is the mechanical shape of a tilt before it has a name.

### Instead of This

> Each person names where they actually were an hour ago and the one thing they could plausibly be doing right now instead.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand everyone in one circle. Say the two things each person will give: where they actually were an hour ago, and one plausible thing they could be doing instead. About ten words total.
2. Give an example from your own hour so the shape is clear. Keep it flat and ordinary — no punchlines, no elaborate fantasy.
3. Have everyone turn to the person on their left and both say their two things at the same time, over each other. That is the only rehearsal.
4. Run one lap round the circle, one voice at a time, eight to ten seconds each. Take no questions, no follow-ups, no commentary between turns.
5. Keep the lap rolling. If someone stalls, say their name, count them down from three, take whatever they give.
6. On your count of three, everyone points at the person whose alternate they would swap into now. Say that pointing at the floor is fine.
7. Count the fingers out loud, name the most-pointed-at answer, thank the room, and move straight into the next block.

## 2. 😄 Laughter Blast (5 min)

*Stay standing, stay in the circle. This one has no content. It just needs your voice.*

**Why here:** Five minutes of shared physical silliness so the room is warm before a session that asks for patience and discipline.

### Down We Go

> The room shuffles in a circle chanting nonsense, then drops to a crouch together and laughs on the floor.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Get everyone in one circle, shoulders loose, arms free. No hand-holding, no contact.
2. Demonstrate first, loudly. Shuffle sideways chanting "wibble wobble" on a loop. Do it badly and commit — that gives them permission.
3. Tell them the only rule: when you call "AND DOWN", everyone drops to a crouch and laughs out loud.
4. Start the circle shuffling left, chanting together. Call "AND DOWN" after about ten seconds. Crouch, laugh five seconds, stand, reverse direction. Repeat.
5. Run round two in slow motion: low droning chant, drop over a count of five, land and laugh slow and deep. Two drops.
6. Run round three fast: double-speed chant, high voices, a drop every five seconds. Four or five drops. No pause between landing, laughing and standing.
7. Run the last round in a whisper — whispered chant, whispered laugh in the crouch. On your final call, everyone drops and laughs at full volume for ten seconds.
8. Stand the room up. Ask one question. Take shouted answers from where people are standing, then move straight into the next block.

## 3. 🔥 Warm-up cluster (15 min)

*Four things, quick, no notes. Every one of them is the same shape: build a pattern, feel the pattern, then change one thing.*

**Why here:** Four short drills that install the three-then-break count in the body before theory names it, so theory confirms something they have already felt.

### Beat, Beat, Break

> The room drills a three-move body rhythm in unison, then absorbs a deliberate break into the pattern and keeps going.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand the room in a loose circle, everyone facing in. Tell them nobody has to be clever here — they only have to copy.
2. Teach three beats: stomp the right foot, one clap, breathe out a hard 'HUP'. Run it eight times together, slow, until the room locks in.
3. Call the count and drive the tempo up over four faster rounds. Keep it loud. Keep it together.
4. Call 'TILT' and point at one person. Next cycle, they add one move of their own on beat four. Everyone copies it immediately, no discussion.
5. Run the four-beat pattern six times at speed, then tilt a new person onto beat five. Copy fast.
6. Keep tilting every six cycles until the pattern runs seven or eight beats long. Expect wobble. Drive through it — never restart.
7. Finish with the whole grown pattern twice at full volume, then cut the room dead. Hold the silence three seconds before you speak.

### Every Tuesday, Until

> Pairs build a dull shared routine line by line, then one line breaks it and the other makes it make sense.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Pair everyone off, standing face to face. Odd number: make one three, with the third speaking in turn.
2. Tell them they alternate single lines, each starting 'Every Tuesday we…', building one shared ordinary routine. Dull and concrete. No jokes, no twists yet.
3. Demo two lines with a volunteer so they hear the flatness you want. 'Every Tuesday we buy the same bread.' 'Every Tuesday we complain the bread is stale.'
4. Start Round 1. Let them build for about forty seconds, then call 'Until' loudly across the room.
5. Tell them: whoever speaks next breaks the routine in exactly one line. Their partner answers with exactly one line that makes the break make sense. Then stop.
6. Run Round 2 with a new routine and the other partner opening. Call 'Until' at forty seconds again.
7. Run Round 3 with new partners — hands up if unpaired — then build, break, justify on your call.
8. Run a fast round with that same partner: three lines of routine only, then call 'Until' straight away. Break, justify, stop.
9. Call stop. Take two pairs shouting their break and its justification across the room. Move straight on.

### Three Photographs

> Silent groups build a frozen picture of an ordinary moment, let one person break it, and adjust until the broken picture makes sense again.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Split the room into groups of four or five. Spread them out so each group has floor space and a clear sightline to you.
2. Set the rule: three frozen photographs, no words, no sound. Photo one is ordinary. Photo two breaks it. Photo three makes the break normal.
3. Call a place — 'a bus queue'. Clap once. Groups have five seconds to freeze into an ordinary picture of that place.
4. Hold the freeze ten seconds. While they hold, walk the room and point silently at one person in each group. No speaking.
5. Clap again. The person you pointed at changes one thing: position, level or direction. Everyone else makes exactly one silent adjustment so the picture still reads.
6. Clap a third time. Groups freeze the settled version. Hold ten seconds, then release.
7. Run round two with a new place — 'a waiting room'. Same three claps, but point within two seconds so the break lands early.
8. Hold the final photograph ten seconds. Stop there. Do not let groups discuss or replay.

## 4. 🧠 Theory — Narrative Architecture (20 min)

![Narrative Architecture](../../../assets/infographics/D3.S3.jpg){ .infographic }

- **The big idea:** A story is a pattern set up, broken on purpose, and then made to make sense. Platform is the pattern. Tilt is the break. Spine is the line of consequence that runs from the break to the end of the scene.
- **Where you are on the path:** Competent means they can break a routine and justify the break. In the room that looks like: three clean repetitions before anything changes, a break that the other player notices, and at least two lines afterwards that treat the break as real. It does not yet look like elegant structure or a satisfying ending. Do not ask for endings today.
- **The one cue to coach:** *“Break the routine — then make it make sense.”*

**The three beats**

1. **Say it.** Say: a scene starts with a routine — the thing these people do, that they have done before, that they would do again if we left. Play it three times so we can see the shape of it. Then one thing changes. That change is not random and it is not a joke; it is the first unusual thing. After that, the scene is about the change. Everything that happens next is a consequence of it. Write ROUTINE / BREAK / CONSEQUENCE on the board and say: today you only have to do these three. Not four. Not an ending.
2. **Show it.** Take one volunteer. Set a routine: you two lay out the market stall every morning. Play the beat three times out loud — crates, apples, the same complaint about the weather — and say "one" "two" "three" as you go so they hear the count. Then break it yourself: today there are no apples in the crate. Then show the two wrong responses fast — panic-explaining it ("there must have been a mix-up at the depot, this never happens") and ignoring it. Then show the right one: change your behaviour. Start counting the other crates. Say nothing about what it means. Sixty seconds, stop, sit down.
3. **Try it.** Everyone into pairs, standing, all at once, no audience. Pick a routine both of you have actually done — washing up, queueing, closing the shop. Play it three times, out loud, counting under your breath. On the third, one of you breaks it with one concrete change. Both of you then behave differently for two lines. Stop. Swap who breaks. Run it twice through. Five minutes total, then pull them in and ask only: who broke early, and did it feel better or worse?

!!! warning "The misunderstanding that always comes up"
    They think justification means explanation — a character supplying a reason. It is the opposite. Justification is the world absorbing the break: behaviour changes, stakes change, someone treats the new thing as normal and acts on it. If a character apologises for the break or accounts for it, the scene resets to the platform and the tilt is wasted. Say this out loud, twice.

## 5. 🎲 Drill A — isolation (25 min)

*One circle if we're under fourteen, two circles if we're over, three if we're eighteen or more — sort yourselves now and stand shoulder to shoulder. Nobody plays a character in this one.*

**Why here:** Isolates the spine with no scene pressure: they practise letting one change drive the next thing, and the next, without acting or characterising.

### The Narrative Current

> Steer scenes to deeper waters by pausing to voice and integrate hidden narrative currents.

`Players 3–99` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Get a location or a relationship from the room. Give it to two players. Tell them to build a plain, unhurried scene — who they are, where they are, what they are doing.
2. Let the platform run thirty to sixty seconds. When it settles or stalls, call "Freeze!", name one player, and name a Story Current prompt for them.
3. Have the named player take one step out of the scene, drop eye contact with their partner, and say the answer straight out — one clear sentence, no performance.
4. Send them straight back in. Tell them not to repeat the sentence as dialogue. It has to show up in posture, tempo, tone and what they choose to do next.
5. Tell the partner to accept it on the spot and change their own behaviour to make it true. No arguing, no asking for proof.
6. Work through the prompts in order of pressure: My Want, The Stakes, Relationship Shift, Environmental Truth, Hidden Truth, Complication. Give each player at least one.
7. After the last prompt lands, stop calling. Let the scene run a further minute so the players push the raised stakes to a finish.

## 6. 🎲 Drill B — under load (25 min)

*Pairs — if we're odd, one three, and the third person watches for ten seconds then joins. Ninety seconds per run, I call the resets.*

**Why here:** Puts the same spine under load: they must hold the routine while something in the ground keeps moving, which is where careless breaking shows up first.

### Shifting Foundations

> Adapt to sudden, fundamental changes in your scene's reality by retroactively justifying everything before.

`Players 3–99` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Ask two or three players into the space. Take a plain, everyday suggestion from the room — a place, an object, a chore. Nothing wacky.
2. Tell them to build the platform fast: who they are to each other, where they are, what they want. Thirty to forty-five seconds, no plot.
3. Call 'Reality shift' and state one new truth about the scene out loud. Keep it single and disruptive: one fact, not a paragraph.
4. Tell players to accept it instantly. No pause, no double-take, no stepping out of character to comment on it.
5. Have them reach back and justify what they already said and did so it now makes sense under the new truth. One line each is enough.
6. Push them to follow consequences: if this is true, what else is true? Play the next thing that follows, don't discuss it.
7. Add one or two more shifts as the scene runs, each landing on top of the last. Stack, don't reset.
8. Close the scene once the final shift is absorbed and something has resolved. Cut it yourself if it drifts past ninety seconds.

## 7. 🎯 Scene Lab (35 min)

*Full scenes now, two or three players, five minutes each. At eighteen or more we split into two halves with our own audiences and I move between you. Your only job is routine, one break, and then live with it.*

**Why here:** First five-minute scene of the course. This is where they find out that holding a shape over time is a different job from being alive for two minutes.

### The Ripple Effect

> Transform subtle, unspoken physical and verbal offers into deep, deliberate narrative choices.

`Players 2–99` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Set two players in a scene with a plain relationship and a shared task — two colleagues boxing up an office, two siblings clearing a garage. No conflict brief.
2. Seat everyone else as Spotters. Their job is to watch for tiny offers: a hesitation, a turned shoulder, an odd word, a hand that stops mid-reach.
3. Tell Spotters that anyone may call "Ripple!" at any moment. On that call the scene freezes instantly — bodies hold, no talking from the players.
4. Have the caller name the detail only, in one sentence: "I noticed you put that photo frame face down." No interpretation yet, no praise, no joke.
5. Have the caller then interpret it: "This implies that…" One reading of the subtext or hidden feeling. Short. The players do not argue with it.
6. Ask the player who made the detail's partner to declare aloud: "To create a ripple, I will respond by…" — a concrete line or action, not a feeling.
7. Call "Play." The scene restarts and that declared response happens immediately. Do not let them ease back in with small talk first.
8. Run three or four Ripples per scene, then let the players find an ending. Cut it yourself if it drifts past the time.

## 8. 🏟️ Showcase round (30 min)

*Two teams. I am the referee and I am not neutral about waffle. Three categories, timed, and I will stop you mid-sentence and tell the room why.*

**Why here:** First contact with the competitive format: a clock, a referee and public penalties, so they know what they are voting on later and can find out now whether they like it.

### The Scoreline

> Two teams play the same timed improv game one after the other while a referee whistles fouls and the audience applauds a winner.

`Players 10–20` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Split the room into two benches of equal size, facing a clear playing area. Take the whistle yourself as referee. Appoint a scorekeeper from whichever bench is larger.
2. Open as host in one minute. Three house rules: applaud both teams, call suggestions only when asked, the referee's word is final. Name both benches. Blow the whistle once so everyone hears it.
3. Name the four fouls and whistle after each: blocking a partner's offer, waffling instead of choosing, talking over a partner, laughing at someone's expense. Each whistle gives one point to the other bench.
4. Pick four games from the bank of six. Open Scene: two players, ninety seconds, audience gives a place. Story One Word: the bench lines up and tells a titled story one word each.
5. Emotion Dial: two players, ninety seconds, you call a number from one to ten and they play that intensity. One Prop: players take turns using a chair as something other than a chair.
6. Tag Scene: a two-hander runs, teammates tap a player out and start a fresh scene from the last line spoken. Expert: one player answers interview questions on an audience topic.
7. Run each round the same way. Name the game, read its rule aloud, take one suggestion, then both benches play it in turn to that same suggestion. Alternate who goes first.
8. Score each round in one minute. Raise a hand over one bench, then the other. Louder applause takes two points, the quieter side takes one. Scorekeeper flips the numbers.
9. Double the points on the final round. Read the totals, have both benches bow together, then name the scorekeeper, tech and front of house and let them bow.

## 9. 💭 Reflection & homework (10 min)

**Deepen your improv**
1. Think of the scene today where the break landed best. What did the other player do in the two lines straight after it — and what did they not do?
2. Where did you break early? Name the pull. Was it boredom, was it fear the scene was dying, or was it wanting the laugh?

**Beyond the stage**
3. Think of a routine you are part of outside this room — a meeting, a family Sunday, a commute. When something breaks it, does the group absorb the break or explain it away? What does absorbing it look like from the inside?

??? note "🎓 Facilitator notes"

    **Timing risks**
    - The warm-up cluster is four activities in fifteen minutes. That is under four minutes each including the set-up sentence. Cut Three Photographs first if you are behind — its work is repeated in Drill A.
    - Scene Lab overruns every time. Five minutes means five minutes; call time mid-line and do not let a scene run to a natural end. At 18-20 you are running two halves at once and cannot give notes to both — give one note per scene, to the whole half, and move on.
    - The Scoreline eats thirty minutes fast because the explanation is the teaching. Budget four minutes for the format and penalties up front and keep the three categories short rather than dropping the third.

    **Failure modes this week**
    - Breaking on line two. Most common failure today. Stop the scene, send them back to the top, make them play the routine three times before they are allowed to touch it. Do this once publicly and the room self-corrects.
    - Justification-as-apology. A character explains the break, the other player accepts the explanation, and the platform reassembles. Name it from the side the moment it happens: "you just put it back."
    - Tilts that are weird rather than concrete — a dragon, a ghost, the ceiling falls in. Nothing to absorb. Redirect to a change in the routine itself: the thing that is always there isn't, the person who always comes doesn't, the answer is different this time.
    - One or two people bristling at the Scoreline — the clock, the penalties, being stopped. That is useful data, not a problem to fix. Let them say so in reflection. Do not defend the format and do not sell it.

    **What good looks like by the end:** By the end, most pairs can build a recognisable routine, break it once with something concrete, and keep playing for two or three minutes with the break still true. Scenes will be shapeless at the end and that is fine — you did not ask for endings. The test is whether the room can now spot an early break in someone else's scene without you saying anything.

    **Carry into next week:** They can break and justify. Next week is what the break costs — consequence with weight, and letting a scene change someone. Bring back two names from today: the person who breaks too early and the person who never breaks, and pair them deliberately. Also note who visibly enjoyed the Scoreline and who did not; you will need both before the format vote.

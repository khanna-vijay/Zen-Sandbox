# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Pull Them In

![Pull Them In](../img/intermediate_w07__b3_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> One player at a time works a live crowd that answers only with its feet — step in if it lands, step back if it doesn't.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 07 run-sheet](../week-07.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 7 is match play: short-form under a clock and in front of a crowd that reacts in real time. This is the first thing they do all morning. It puts the audience's response into the body before any scene starts, so 'reading the room' stops being an idea and becomes a thing they can see moving towards or away from them. It feeds directly into the format games later in the session, where players either adjust to the room or keep pushing something that died.

## What students actually learn

- They feel the difference between a reaction they caused and a reaction they asked for.
- They stop needing words to hold attention — stillness, focus and one clear change become tools.
- They learn to notice the room mid-moment and change course inside ten seconds, rather than after the scene.
- They accept a step back as information, not as failure.

## Setup

Clear floor. Chairs pushed right back. Arc of players along one wall with a visible start line — use a floor mark, a tape line, or just 'where your toes are now'. Two metres of empty space in front. Timer in your hand, or count ten aloud. No props, no music. Have the order of the arc clear so nobody has to be picked.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Set the arc and the start line. Explain the rules in about forty seconds, then demo for thirty: stand in the middle, do something flat, let them step back, change one thing, pull them in. Name the change. |
| 2 | First pass. Ten seconds per player, straight down the arc, no gaps. Call 'next' hard on the count. Crowd resets to the start line between every player. Do not comment on individual turns. |
| 1 | Second pass. Crowd starts one step back with arms folded. Take six to eight volunteers, ten seconds each. Players must earn the first step in from a cold room. |
| 1 | Call the huddle — tightest comfortable, opt-out to the edge is fine. One shared breath out. One sentence from you: earn it, never beg for it. Move straight on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As the first player steps in | *"Body only. No jokes."* |
| When a player rushes into shapes | *"Slower. Let them find you."* |
| When the crowd steps back and the player keeps going | *"They moved. Change something."* |
| When a player mugs or looks for approval | *"Stop asking. Just do it."* |
| When the crowd hesitates instead of moving | *"Feet, not faces. Commit to a step."* |
| Start of the second pass | *"Cold room. Earn the first step."* |
| On every ten-count | *"Next."* |
| Calling the huddle | *"In tight. One breath out."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The arc is genuinely moving — steps in and steps back, not a polite frozen line. Players are mostly still and specific rather than busy. At least a few people visibly change tack mid-turn when the crowd retreats, and you can see the crowd come back in. The second pass is harder and everyone knows it. No one is talking. The whole thing has forward motion and ends before it gets precious.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The crowd stands still for every player, nobody steps either way. | They are being kind. Stepping back feels like a judgement on a person they will do scenes with all day. | Stop the round. Say the step back is data, not a verdict, and that a still crowd gives the player nothing. Insist everyone moves on every turn — in or back, no neutral. |
| Players go big and gurning immediately, and the crowd laughs but does not step in. | They are begging. Mugging reads as a request for a reaction rather than something worth watching. | Side-coach 'stop asking'. Give one player a redo with the instruction to do one small thing with total attention. Point out when the crowd steps in for the quiet version. |
| Turns overrun and you lose the arc before everyone has gone. | You are letting good turns run because they are working. | Call 'next' on the count every time, including on the best turn. The cut is the exercise. Cap the arc at thirteen players and put the rest in the second pass. |
| One or two people never step at all and hang at the back. | Self-consciousness about being watched while reacting. | Give the crowd a job: 'you are the only instrument the player has'. Reframe stepping as helping rather than judging. Do not single anyone out. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One arc. Everyone takes a turn in the first pass — thirteen players at ten seconds fits the two minutes with the resets. Second pass takes six volunteers. |
| **14–17** | One arc. First pass covers the first thirteen in the line, called fast. The remaining four to six go in the second pass as automatic volunteers, so everyone has been in the middle once. |
| **18–20** | Split into two arcs of nine or ten, side by side facing away from each other. Each arc watches and steps for its own player. You count ten aloud for both at once and call 'next' for both. First pass gets everyone through. Second pass: four volunteers per arc. |

## Variations

**Pairs in the middle** — Send two players in together for each ten seconds. They can play off each other but still cannot speak.  
*Easier — halves the exposure and gives nervous players something to react to instead of an empty floor.*

**Held back twice** — Crowd starts two steps back. A player must earn two steps in within their ten seconds or they sit the pass out.  
*Harder — raises the cost of the flat opening and forces an immediate change of tack.*

**One word only** — Each player may say a single word at any point in their ten seconds. Everything else is body.  
*Different emphasis — shifts attention to timing and placement of a line rather than physical hold.*

## Debrief

1. **Who felt the crowd move and changed something because of it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone describes a specific moment: 'they went back so I stopped and just looked at one person.' Not 'I think I did badly.' One or two answers is enough — move on.

2. **What pulled you in as a crowd member?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Concrete and behavioural: stillness, a clear intention, someone committing to something small. If the answers are all 'the funny ones', push once for what the funny ones were actually doing, then stop.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact is required at any point except the closing huddle, and that is opt-in — say so out loud before you call it, and tell people the edge of the huddle is a full place to stand. Anyone who prefers not to take a turn in the middle can stay in the crowd; the crowd role is real work, not sitting out. Stepping is the only demand, and a small shift of weight counts for anyone who does not want to move much or who uses a mobility aid. Nobody has to be funny to pass this.

## Why it works

Room reading fails when the feedback signal is invisible. In a normal scene the audience response is diffuse — a laugh, a silence, a shuffle — and beginners either miss it or over-read it. Here the response is a physical, unambiguous, collective movement, delivered inside a ten-second window. That compresses the loop between action and consequence to the point where a player cannot avoid noticing. Stripping speech removes the escape route of talking through a dead moment, so the only available fix is to change what you are doing. And because a step back costs nothing socially, players stop protecting themselves and start experimenting. That is the cue in physical form: the crowd comes to you when you give them something, and it retreats the instant you ask.

# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Instead of This

![Instead of This](../img/intermediate_w04__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Each person names where they actually were an hour ago and the one thing they could plausibly be doing right now instead.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 04 run-sheet](../week-04.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 4 is platform and tilt. This is the first thing they say all day, and it makes them say a platform and a tilt in one breath before anyone calls it that. The lap gives you twenty mundane worlds and twenty small, plausible departures from them — the raw material you will point back at all session. It feeds directly into the platform-building work that follows, where the note will be the same: ordinary first, deviation second, deviation must still make sense.

## What students actually learn

- That a departure only reads as interesting if the thing it departs from was established first.
- That plausible beats clever — the alternates the room wants to swap into are usually the boring, believable ones.
- That they can speak in the circle without preparing something good.
- That their own ordinary Saturday is usable material.

## Setup

No props. No chairs. Clear floor space for one standing circle of up to twenty. Know your own answer before you start — you go first as the example and it must be dull. A visible clock or phone timer where you can see it; you are running four short beats inside five minutes and you cannot let the lap sag.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Give the two-part shape and your own flat example. Then everyone turns left and both say theirs simultaneously, over each other. Cut it after fifteen seconds of noise. |
| 2 | Run the lap. One at a time, eight to ten seconds each. You keep the pace: no questions, no follow-ups, no commentary. Nod and move to the next person immediately. |
| 1 | Point round. Count to three. Everyone points at the alternate they would swap into. Hold the point for three seconds so people can look around at where the fingers landed. |
| 1 | Count the biggest cluster out loud, name that answer, thank the room. Say the cue once — break the routine, then make it make sense — and move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Before the simultaneous rehearsal | *"All at once, over each other. Nobody's listening."* |
| Opening the lap | *"Where you were. What you'd be doing instead. Two things."* |
| Someone starts explaining or justifying | *"Just the two. Next."* |
| An answer gets big or fantastical | *"Plausible. Something you could actually be doing."* |
| The lap starts to drag or people laugh between turns | *"Keep it rolling. Straight on."* |
| Someone freezes | *"Anything true. Three, two, one — go."* |
| Setting up the point round | *"Point at the life you'd swap into. Floor is a fine answer."* |
| Closing | *"Ordinary first. Then the swerve. That's the whole session."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The lap takes under two minutes and never stalls for more than a beat. Answers are short and unremarkable — was at the launderette, could be asleep. Nobody is performing. When the fingers go up they cluster on two or three answers, and those answers are almost always the mundane ones, not the exotic ones. The room notices that and someone says so out loud. You are moving into the next block while the energy is still up.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Answers getting longer and funnier as the lap goes on, each one topping the last. | Early speakers set a comedy bar and you let it stand. | Cut in after the third inflated answer. Say the word count out loud — ten words — and take the next person immediately. Do not laugh at the big ones. |
| The lap eats three and a half minutes and you lose the point round. | You allowed follow-up questions or reacted to individual answers. | Stop responding. Nod and say the next person's name before the current one finishes their breath. If you are past two minutes, skip the point round and just close. |
| Two or three people give the alternate but skip where they actually were. | The platform half is less interesting to them, so they drop it. | Ask them for the first half only, in three words, then move on. Say to the room: both halves, the boring one first. |
| Nobody points, or everyone points at the floor. | The instruction landed as a judgement on other people rather than a preference. | Reframe fast — this is which afternoon you'd steal, not who did best. Recount on three. If it stays flat, drop it and close on time. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Give each person a full ten seconds; the lap runs comfortably inside two minutes. You can afford one repeat of an answer you liked before the point round. |
| **14–17** | One circle. Tighten to eight seconds each and say so before you start. Cut your own example to one sentence. Do not repeat anyone's answer back. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten for the lap, each run by a nominated timekeeper who just says next. Reconvene as one circle for the point round; people may point across circles. |

## Variations

**Written first** — Hand out slips. Everyone writes their two things down in silence for thirty seconds, then reads from the slip on their turn.  
*Easier — removes on-the-spot generation for nervous groups, and shortens every turn.*

**And therefore** — Each person adds a third beat: one consequence of the swap. Was at the shop, could be on a train, so I'd miss the delivery.  
*Harder — introduces cause and effect, and previews the tilt-and-consequence work later in the session.*

**Steal the platform** — Each person repeats the previous speaker's actual location as their own, then gives a new alternate from inside it.  
*Different emphasis — shifts the work from self-disclosure to listening and building off what was just established.*

## Debrief

1. **Which alternates did the room point at, and what did they have in common?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone notices the winners were small and believable — a nap, a pub garden — not the dramatic ones. They connect wanting it to being able to picture it.

2. **What happened when you heard only the alternate and not where the person actually was?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* It landed flat, or they had to guess the context. Someone says the swap only means something once you know what's being swapped out.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. Pointing is at a distance and pointing at the floor is stated as a legitimate choice — say it before you count. Personal information is optional: anyone can invent both halves and nobody needs to know. If a real answer is private — a hospital, a funeral — they can say somewhere else and give an alternate. Anyone who prefers not to speak may pass with a hand and stay in the circle; take them at the end of the lap or not at all. Standing is not required — run it seated if anyone needs to sit, and seat the whole circle rather than one person.

## Why it works

Narrative architecture starts with a platform: a shared, unremarkable picture of where things are. This activity forces the platform out first — an hour ago, a real place — and only then permits the tilt. Because the tilt has to be plausible, the deviation stays tethered to the world it left, which is exactly the constraint that makes a story spine hold. The simultaneous rehearsal removes the fear of the first solo sentence by making everyone's first sentence inaudible. The point round supplies the evidence: the room reliably chooses the ordinary alternates, which demonstrates without a lecture that interest comes from the relation between platform and swerve, not from the size of the swerve.

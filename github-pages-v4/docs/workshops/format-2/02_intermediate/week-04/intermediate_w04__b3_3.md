# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Three Photographs

![Three Photographs](../img/intermediate_w04__b3_3.jpg){ .infographic }

> Silent groups build a frozen picture of an ordinary moment, let one person break it, and adjust until the broken picture makes sense again.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 04 run-sheet](../week-04.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 4 is about platform and tilt. This is the first thing they do, before any talking. It gives them a physical, five-minute version of the whole session shape: establish the ordinary, break it, absorb the break. Everything later in the session — verbal platforms, tilts inside scenes, the story spine — is this same move with words attached.

## What students actually learn

- A tilt is something that happens to a picture, not a punchline someone thinks of.
- They can absorb a change someone else makes without stopping to negotiate it.
- The ordinary has to be built properly first, or there is nothing to break.

## Setup

Clear floor, no chairs in the playing space. You need room for three or four clusters of people to stand and freeze without touching. No props. Know your two locations before you start: a bus queue, a waiting room. Have a loud clap or a hand drum — the claps carry the whole exercise, so they must be unmistakable.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Form groups of four or five and spread them out. Give the rule in one pass: three photographs, no words, no sound, ordinary — broken — normal again. Do not demonstrate. Start. |
| 2 | Round one, 'a bus queue'. Clap, five seconds to freeze, hold ten while you point at one person per group. Clap, they change one thing and everyone adjusts once. Clap, freeze, hold ten, relax. |
| 1 | Round two, 'a waiting room'. Same three claps, but point within two seconds of the first freeze so the break arrives before the picture settles comfortably. |
| 1 | Hold the last photograph ten seconds. Release. Ask one question, take two answers, move on. Do not let it become a discussion. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As they form the first photograph | *"Ordinary. Boring is correct."* |
| During the first ten-second hold | *"Stone still. Let me see the picture."* |
| Immediately after the second clap | *"One change only. Then stop."* |
| While the group adjusts around the break | *"Everyone shifts once. Make it make sense."* |
| If people start mouthing or gesturing to each other | *"No words. Read the bodies."* |
| Round two, as you point early | *"Break it now — before it's comfortable."* |
| On the final freeze | *"Hold. This is normal now."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Three distinct held pictures per group, with silence between the claps. The second picture visibly disturbs the first — someone is on the floor, or facing the wrong way, or has climbed on something. The third picture has everyone else angled toward that change, so a stranger walking in would read a coherent situation rather than one person misbehaving. Nobody speaks. Nobody laughs their way out of a freeze.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Groups whispering and pointing before the first freeze. | They are trying to agree a plan. Four seconds is not enough time to plan, so they use words. | Cut it on the spot: 'No words — just stand in it.' Say before round two that the picture is made of whatever they happen to do in five seconds. |
| The break is a big comic gesture — a mime punch, a collapse played for laughs — and the third photograph is just everyone laughing at them. | They read 'break it' as 'be funny'. | Call 'One change only' louder next round. Point at a quieter person. Praise a small break in the debrief: 'She turned around. That was enough.' |
| The third photograph is identical to the second — nobody adjusts. | They do not know what 'makes it normal' means physically. | Say it as a body instruction: 'Everyone move one part of you toward the change.' A single head turn from each person is enough to make the new picture read. |
| Groups relax and chat during the ten-second holds. | The hold feels long and pointless to them. | Narrate the hold: count it, walk between the groups, look at them. Being observed keeps freezes frozen. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Three groups. Run both rounds as written. You can point at all three groups inside two seconds in round two. |
| **14–17** | Three or four groups of four or five. Point at the largest group first so its adjustment has time to ripple. Keep the timing exactly as written. |
| **18–20** | Four groups. Trim the first hold to seven seconds so you can still reach every group with a point inside the time. If you run late, drop round two's hold to seven seconds rather than cutting the round. |

## Variations

**Named break** — Instead of pointing silently, call the change out loud: 'Third person from the left — sit down.'  
*easier — removes the guesswork about who was chosen and what counts as a change, so the group can spend all its attention on adjusting*

**Rolling break** — After the third clap, clap once more. A different person breaks the settled picture, and the group normalises again.  
*harder — chains two tilts, so they have to absorb a change into a platform that was itself built from a change*

**Same picture, new place** — Groups hold their final photograph. Call a completely different location and let them adjust once so the picture reads as that place instead.  
*different emphasis — moves the work from tilt to justification, which is what the story spine needs later in the session*

## Debrief

1. **When you were the one who broke it, what did you feel the group do?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something concrete about being met: 'They all turned towards me' or 'Nobody fixed me back into place.' If they say 'I felt bad for ruining it', name that the break was the job.

2. **What made the third photograph work when it worked?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They describe reacting rather than correcting — everyone leaned in, or looked the same way, or lowered to match. The useful answer treats the break as new information, not as a mistake to repair.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact is needed and none is required. Say so before the first clap: 'Photographs, not contact — leave a gap between bodies.' If someone puts a hand on someone, let it pass once and restate the rule to the room. Floor levels are optional; anyone who does not want to kneel, lie down or climb can make their change standing — turning, looking away and changing height by a few inches all read fine from across the room. Anyone who prefers not to be pointed at can tell you, and you point elsewhere in their group. People can watch a round and join the second one.

## Why it works

Narrative architecture is usually taught as a sequence of ideas, which invites people to think ahead and plan the tilt. This exercise removes language and gives them four seconds, so no plan is possible. The platform gets built by bodies arriving in a space, the tilt arrives from outside as your point, and justification becomes a physical act — everyone moves once, toward the change. Because the group can see the third photograph either read or fail to read, the feedback on whether they justified the tilt is immediate and visual. They arrive at the scene work later in the session already knowing, in their bodies, that a break is absorbed rather than corrected.

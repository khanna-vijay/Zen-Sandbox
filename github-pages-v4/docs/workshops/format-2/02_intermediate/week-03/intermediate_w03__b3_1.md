# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Ten Seconds Left

![Ten Seconds Left](../img/intermediate_w03__b3_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> The whole room mimes a mundane task against a shouted countdown, then names what it just lost.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 03 run-sheet](../week-03.md)

## Why this activity, here

Opens Week 3. Weeks 1 and 2 built agreement and listening; scenes were pleasant and consequence-free. This is the first thing in the room that attaches a price to a moment. It puts stakes in the body before anyone has to talk about them, so when the session cue lands — what do they stand to lose? — the answer already has a felt reference. Feeds directly into the scene work later in the block, where the want has to be visible in how someone moves, not only in what they say.

## What students actually learn

- Urgency lives in the body before it reaches the dialogue — they feel the difference between hurrying and needing.
- Naming a loss out loud is easy and unembarrassing, which lowers the bar for doing it in a scene later.
- A deadline is a cheap, reliable way to make a boring action watchable.

## Setup

Clear floor, everyone standing with arm's length of space around them. No chairs, no props, no partners. You need a loud voice and a list of tasks — five or six is plenty. Nothing to print, nothing to hand out. Stand where the whole room can see you and count with them.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Spread everyone out. Explain the shape in two sentences: you name a task and a deadline, the room mimes it at full speed while counting down from ten together, freeze on zero. Demonstrate the freeze. |
| 1 | Two plain rounds. "The lift doors are closing and your bag is inside" — count ten to zero with them, freeze. Then "the pan is boiling over" — count, freeze. Do not comment between rounds. |
| 1 | Add the shout. Three rounds, same countdown, but on zero everyone calls out what they lost in one or two words. All at once. Take whatever tasks you like — burnt toast, missed bus, spilled paint. |
| 1 | Drop to a count of five. Three rounds, higher cost: the interview call is ringing, the cement is setting, the baby is waking. Faster count, louder shout on zero. |
| 1 | Stop the room. Shake arms and hands out, ten seconds. Deliver the closing line: the count is not the point, what the count costs you is. Move straight to the next block. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| First round, bodies are polite and small | *"Full speed. You are actually late."* |
| Counting has gone ragged or quiet | *"Louder on the count. All together."* |
| On zero, first freeze rounds | *"Freeze. Hold it. Don't tidy up."* |
| Introducing the shout | *"One thing you lost. Two words maximum."* |
| Shouts come out tentative or mumbled | *"Say it like it mattered."* |
| Switching to the count of five | *"Half the time. Same job."* |
| Someone is inventing elaborate business instead of hurrying | *"Simpler task, harder push."* |
| Final round, before the shake-out | *"Last one. Everything you've got."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is loud and slightly untidy. Bodies are committed — reaching, lunging, grabbing — rather than sketching neat mime. The countdown is unison and gets louder as it drops. On zero the freeze is genuine, and the shouted losses overlap into a mess of specific nouns. Nobody is watching anyone else. Energy is up when you stop it, and you have to raise your voice to move on.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Polite, small, decorative mime — people demonstrating a task rather than racing it | They are performing for you instead of chasing the deadline. Common in week three when the room is still self-conscious. | Call "full speed, you are actually late" and count faster than they expect. Drop to five earlier than planned. |
| The shout on zero is a mumble, or half the room says nothing | Nobody wants to be the first voice. They are waiting to hear what others say. | Shout your own loss on zero with them for one round. Model volume, not cleverness. Then step back out. |
| People glancing round and copying their neighbour's task | The instruction to work alone did not land, or the task you named was too vague to act on. | Name tasks with a concrete physical object in them — a pan, a bag, a ringing phone. Say "eyes on your own job". |
| Someone stops after two rounds, out of breath | Full-speed miming for two minutes is genuine cardio and the group varies in fitness. | Say once, to the room, that scaling the size of the movement down is fine — the urgency counts, not the range. Keep going. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Run exactly as written. The room may be quiet enough that the unison count sounds thin — count loudly with them throughout, all rounds. You can add a fourth round at the count of five if you finish early. |
| **14–17** | As written. Drop your own counting voice after the second round; the group carries it. Watch the edges of the room for people who have drifted into spectating and call them by name into the next round. |
| **18–20** | Space is the constraint. Push furniture back before you start and tell everyone to keep both arms free without touching a neighbour. Cut one round from step three to protect the timing — two loss rounds instead of three. |

## Variations

**Count only** — Run all rounds without the shouted loss. Task, countdown, freeze, next.  
*Easier — good for a very new or very shy group. Gets bodies moving without asking anyone to make a sound of their own.*

**Count of three** — After the round of five, add two rounds counting three to zero, with the most expensive tasks you can name.  
*Harder — almost no time to plan, so the body chooses. Exposes who is still thinking their way through the mime.*

**Name the loss first** — Before each round, you announce what is at stake — "you lose the flat" — then name the task and count.  
*Different emphasis — shifts attention from panic to consequence, and rehearses holding a known cost through an action rather than discovering it at zero.*

## Debrief

1. **Which rounds felt different — the tens or the fives?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says the shorter count stopped them planning and they just moved. Someone else notices the fives felt worse, not just faster. Move on as soon as the word cost or panic comes up.

2. **What made a task worth hurrying for?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They point at the price, not the task. "Boiling pan is nothing. The interview call is my rent." Once someone names a consequence rather than an action, you have what you need.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact in this activity. Keep an arm's length gap in all directions and say so out loud before the first round — full-speed mime in a crowded room causes collisions. Tell the group that the size of the movement is theirs to choose: a small, urgent gesture reads the same as a lunge, and anyone can sit or stay still and count only. Shouting is optional; counting under the breath is fine. Flag before you start that this involves fast repeated movement and loud voices, so anyone with a joint, back or hearing concern can scale down without announcing why.

## Why it works

Stakes are usually taught as a note — "raise the stakes" — which asks the student to think their way into urgency mid-scene, too late to help. This inverts the order. The countdown imposes an external deadline the body responds to automatically; nobody has to decide to be urgent. Then the shout on zero attaches a noun to that physical state, so the student links the sensation to a nameable loss. By the time you ask what do they stand to lose, they have said fifteen answers out loud without being assessed on any of them. That kills the main obstacle, which is not ignorance of stakes but the feeling that naming a want is presumptuous. Doing it in unison, at volume, six times, removes the exposure.

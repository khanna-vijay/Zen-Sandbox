# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Last Photo, Five Words

![Last Photo, Five Words](../img/intermediate_w03__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Everyone describes the most recent photo on their phone in five words or fewer, no scrolling for a better one.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 03 run-sheet](../week-03.md)

## Why this activity, here

Opens Week 3, where scenes get built on wants and stakes. Before anyone plays a character who needs something, they say why a real thing exists — every photo was taken for a reason. That reason is a want. Feeds the block on objectives, and the session cue, 'What do they stand to lose?'

## What students actually learn

- That everything they do already has a reason behind it — they don't need to invent motivation, they need to notice it.
- That a want stated in five words lands harder than a want explained in fifty.
- That speaking without editing is survivable, which lowers the cost of the first offer in a scene.

## Setup

Standing circle, everyone able to see across. Phones in hand — say this in the room-entry patter so people aren't digging in bags. No chairs, no props, nothing on the floor. You stand in the circle, not outside it, and you go first so the five-word limit is demonstrated rather than described.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Give the rule: last photo on the phone, the real one, no scrolling. Private photo means use the last one you'd say out loud. Model your own five-word version now. |
| 1 | Everyone turns to a neighbour. All talk at once — the photo and why it exists. Tell them nobody is listening, that's the point. Call time while the noise is still high. |
| 2 | Round the circle, one at a time, five words maximum. Count on your fingers if you need to. Cut at six. No follow-ups, no laughs chased, straight to the next person. |
| 1 | Second lap, same order, one word: the feeling when they took it. No hesitation allowed — take whatever comes out. Land on the last person and go straight into the warm-up. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As phones come out and thumbs start moving | *"Stop scrolling. The one you've got is the one."* |
| During the simultaneous-talking round, if it goes quiet | *"Keep talking. Nobody's listening. That's the deal."* |
| First time someone runs long in the five-word lap | *"Six. Next."* |
| When someone starts explaining the context of their photo | *"Five words. The story's not needed."* |
| When the circle slows and people start prepping | *"Faster. Don't draft it."* |
| During the one-word feelings lap, if someone stalls | *"First word. Any word."* |
| On the last person, going into the warm-up | *"Hold that. Everyone had a reason. Keep it."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The five-word lap moves at roughly one person every four seconds and sounds slightly rushed. People are laughing at the compression, not at each other's photos. A few descriptions are flatly mundane — a receipt, a parking bay, a dog — and nobody apologises for them. The one-word lap comes out fast and unguarded. The room is warm and talking as you move on.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Three or four people still scrolling when the circle starts. | They're auditioning their camera roll for something worth saying. | Say 'phones down, whatever's on screen' and start the lap immediately. Don't wait for them. They'll describe what they have. |
| Descriptions balloon to fifteen words with a story attached. | You let the first over-runner finish, so the limit reads as decorative. | Cut the very first person who goes long, including yourself if it's you. One visible cut sets the rule for the whole circle. |
| The simultaneous-talking round dies within ten seconds. | People feel rude speaking over each other and default to taking turns. | Call over the top: 'Both of you, at the same time, keep going.' Raise your own volume. Noise gives permission. |
| Someone visibly uncomfortable, phone face down. | Last photo is genuinely private — medical, a document, someone else's business. | Say the opt-out again to the whole room, not to them: 'Anything you'd rather not, skip to the last one you're happy with, or pass entirely.' |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Both laps fit comfortably. You can allow a beat after each description without losing the pace. |
| **14–17** | One circle, but drive the five-word lap harder — no pause between speakers. Drop your own turn if you're running tight. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten, each with a nominated starter. Run both simultaneously; you float and call the cuts. Keep the one-word lap in the split circles too. |

## Variations

**Show, don't say** — Instead of describing the photo, hold the phone up to the circle for two seconds and say nothing. Then one word for why it exists.  
*Easier — removes the verbal compression for a group that's shy about speaking.*

**Three words** — Drop the limit from five words to three. Enforce it hard. The one-word feelings lap stays.  
*Harder — forces choice about which detail carries the reason, not just the picture.*

**The want behind it** — Keep five words for the photo, then a second lap of five words on what they wanted when they took it: 'to remember', 'to prove it happened'.  
*Different emphasis — moves the frame straight onto objectives, useful if the session's main block needs a running start.*

## Debrief

1. **Whose photo existed for a reason you didn't expect?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a specific mundane one — a fridge shelf, a bus timetable — and identifies the want behind it: proof, reminder, a message to someone. Move on as soon as one person does this.

2. **What happened to the descriptions when you only had five words?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'I had to pick the thing that mattered.' Or: 'It came out more honest because I couldn't dress it up.' You want them noticing that compression forced a choice, not that it was hard.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No physical contact in this activity. Camera rolls are private territory — state the opt-out twice, once at setup and once before the lap starts, and make passing entirely acceptable with no comment from you. Nobody shows a screen unless they choose to. Anyone without a phone, or with a dead one, describes the last thing they photographed from memory. Standing circle is default; anyone who prefers to sit can, and the lap order simply includes their chair.

## Why it works

D3.S4 asks players to find what a character wants and what losing it costs. The barrier isn't skill, it's the belief that motivation must be invented. Every photo on a phone was taken because someone wanted something for a second — to keep, to prove, to send, to laugh at later. Naming that reason out loud, in public, in five words, shows the class that wants are already everywhere and already legible. The five-word cap does the second job: it strips justification, so what's left is the bare want. That's the same compression a scene needs when a player states a need instead of explaining one.

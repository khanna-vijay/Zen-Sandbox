# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — If I Don't

![If I Don't](../img/intermediate_w03__b3_2.jpg){ .infographic }

> In pairs, one person hands over a small everyday want and the other answers, eye to eye, with what they lose if they fail.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 03 run-sheet](../week-03.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 3 opens the question of what a character stands to lose. Students arrive able to state a want; most state it flat, as an errand. This warm-up puts a cost under an errand within ninety seconds of walking in, so the scene work later in the session has somewhere to reach. It feeds directly into the longer objective scenes and gives you a shared phrase — "if I don't, I lose" — to side-coach with all morning.

## What students actually learn

- That the size of a want has nothing to do with the size of the stake underneath it.
- That an answer given at speed, eye to eye, is usually truer than one they've had time to design.
- That naming a loss out loud raises the temperature of a scene without anyone raising their voice.

## Setup

Clear floor, people standing. No chairs, no props, no paper. Pairs stand facing each other about a metre apart, spread around the room so nobody is shouting over a neighbour. Have one volunteer in mind for the demo — pick someone who arrived early and looks awake. Know your own three example wants before you start.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Pair everyone up. Give the rule: A offers a small everyday want in one sentence, B answers straight back with "If I don't, I lose...", eye contact held. Demo once with a volunteer, then start. |
| 1 | Round one. A feeds, B answers. Keep the pace up from the side — a new want every few seconds. Six or seven exchanges. Cut off any pair that starts discussing. |
| 1 | Swap roles. B feeds, A answers. Call for smaller wants than the first round: post a letter, unload the dishwasher, ring your sister back. Same pace, same eye contact. |
| 1 | New partner. Alternate every line — you give a want, they answer, they give a want, you answer. Faster than before. Push the tempo twice from the side. |
| 1 | Pairs pick the one loss that landed hardest. Go round the room, one pair at a time, one sentence out loud. No responses, no laughs invited. Stop on the last pair. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As the first round starts | *"Don't think. Answer."* |
| When pairs start slowing between exchanges | *"New want. Now."* |
| When answers get grand — world peace, my soul | *"Smaller. Something from Tuesday."* |
| When eye contact drops | *"Eyes up. Say it to them."* |
| When a pair laughs off an answer | *"Let it sit. Next want."* |
| Starting the swap or the new-partner round | *"Shrink the want. Keep the loss."* |
| Just before the round-the-room share | *"One sentence. No preamble."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is loud, overlapping and fast. Wants are dull — turn the heating down, reply to that email — and the answers are not. You hear a few losses that make the pair go quiet for a second before the next want lands. Nobody is workshopping. In the final share, at least three sentences land in the room and nobody feels the need to fill the pause after them.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pairs stop after each exchange to talk about the answer. | They're treating it as a conversation, not a drill. | Call "new want, now" over the room. Tell them the only two things allowed out of their mouths are a want and a loss. |
| Every loss is enormous — my marriage, my dignity, my life. | They think bigger equals better stakes, and grandeur is easier than specificity. | Side-coach "smaller" repeatedly. Model one yourself: "If I don't post it, I lose the only week she'd have believed me." |
| Answers arrive as jokes and both partners laugh. | Nerves, plus the standing assumption that improv means being funny. | Say the joke costs them the round. Remind them nobody is being assessed on wit. Drop the pace slightly so they aren't panicking into gags. |
| One partner dries up completely and stares at the floor. | Feeder is offering abstract wants, or the answerer has frozen on the eye contact. | Feed that pair a want yourself. Tell the answerer they can look at the partner's forehead. Give them a stock opener: "I lose the chance to..." |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Straight pairs, five or six of them. Run all four rounds as written. The final share fits easily — every pair speaks with time spare, so let one or two say a second sentence. |
| **14–17** | Seven or eight pairs. Same four rounds. Trim the demo to a single exchange so the share stays inside its minute; go round the room briskly, one sentence, no repeats. |
| **18–20** | Nine or ten pairs. Keep all four rounds, but cut the demo to one exchange and skip your own commentary. For the share, take only the six or seven pairs nearest you and say so plainly — everyone still spoke in the drill. |

## Variations

**Given wants** — You feed the wants to the whole room from the front. Everyone answers their partner simultaneously on each one. Six wants, no swapping roles.  
*Easier. Removes the pressure of inventing wants, so nervous students only have to answer.*

**Second loss** — After each answer, the feeder says "and?" once. The answerer names a second, different loss from the same want before moving on.  
*Harder. Forces past the first available answer into the one underneath it.*

**If I do** — Same structure, but the answer is "If I do, I lose..." — the cost of getting the thing rather than missing it.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts attention to conflicting wants, which sets up scenes where the character isn't simply trying to succeed.*

## Debrief

1. **Which was harder — thinking of wants, or answering them?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says the answering got easier as the wants got smaller, or that they stopped searching once they gave up trying to find a good loss. Move on as soon as two people have spoken.

2. **What happened to the boring wants once you put a loss under them?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a specific pair — dishwasher, letter, phone call — and says it suddenly mattered. That's the whole point of the block. Don't chase further examples.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. Sustained eye contact is the only physical demand and it isn't for everyone — say up front that looking at a forehead, a shoulder, or away entirely is fine and needs no explanation. Nobody is asked for anything true about their own life; the wants are invented and the losses can be too. If someone lands somewhere personal, let it pass without comment and keep the pace up. Anyone who wants to sit out can feed wants to a pair from the side. In the final share, passing is allowed — just move to the next pair.

## Why it works

Stakes are hard to teach as a note because "raise the stakes" gives a student nothing to do. This drill makes the stake a grammatical requirement: the sentence cannot finish without a named loss. Pairing that with speed removes the option of designing a clever answer, so what comes out is the first genuine consequence the student can see. Shrinking the want each round breaks the assumption that stakes scale with subject matter — a dishwasher can carry a marriage. By the end the class has felt, rather than been told, that the cue "what do they stand to lose?" is answerable in under a second, which is the only speed at which it's useful mid-scene.

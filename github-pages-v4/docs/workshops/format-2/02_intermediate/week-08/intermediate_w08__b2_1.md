# 😄 Laughter Blast — Catching Hiccups

![Catching Hiccups](../img/intermediate_w08__b2_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> The whole room fakes contagious whole-body hiccups until the breathing goes ragged and the faces go loose.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

Showcase week. The room walks in nervous and holding its breath. This is the first thing after the door shuts — before the format briefing, before any scene work. It breaks the held breath physically, gets voices out of the throat and into the body, and puts everyone in the same silly water at the same time. It feeds directly into audience-energy work: they need to have felt a room's energy rise and drop under a caller's hand before they try to manage one from the stage.

## What students actually learn

- That they can raise and drop the energy of a room deliberately, not just hope it happens.
- That their voice and breath loosen faster through absurdity than through warm-up drills.
- That being contagious is a choice — you take what lands near you and give it back bigger.
- That collective silliness costs nothing and buys the ensemble ten minutes of nerve.

## Setup

Clear floor, enough for everyone to walk without weaving. Push chairs to the walls. No props, no music. Know where your quieter students are standing so you can pass near them. Have your countdown ready — say the numbers loudly, do not mumble them.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Demonstrate. Four enormous fake hiccups, escalating, whole body. No explanation first — do it, then say 'everyone, on your spot, now.' Room hiccups in place, one every two seconds, no unison. Keep going yourself. |
| 1 | Hold the spot work. Push size, not speed. Call for knees, elbows, chest. If anyone is doing it politely from the neck up, hiccup at them directly until they match you. |
| 1 | Release them to walk. Hiccups are catching: one lands near you, you return it bigger within a second. Arm's length apart at all times. Keep circulating yourself and keep infecting the edges of the room. |
| 1 | Call the pitch ladder. Every ten seconds, higher and squeakier. Lead the climb with your own voice. Take it to a ridiculous top end, then hold it there for the last ten seconds. |
| 1 | Cure: mouths shut, everyone freezes, holds it in. Count down from five aloud. On zero, one giant collective hiccup, let it collapse into laughing. Shake arms out. Ask your one question, thirty seconds, move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| First fifteen seconds of the on-the-spot round | *"Whole body. Not your face."* |
| When hiccups start syncing up | *"Break the rhythm. Off the beat."* |
| As they start walking | *"Catch what lands near you. Give it back bigger."* |
| Mid-walking, if the room is drifting apart | *"Arm's length. Stay in traffic."* |
| Starting the pitch ladder | *"Up a floor. And again."* |
| Top of the ladder | *"Squeakier. Silly is the point."* |
| During the freeze | *"Hold it in. Do not let it out."* |
| On zero | *"Let it go."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Ragged breathing, red faces, people laughing at each other rather than at a joke. Hiccups are landing at odd intervals, not in unison. Voices are out of the throat. When you call the freeze, the effort of holding it in is visible and someone breaks early. After the release, the room is louder than when it walked in, and nobody is checking their phone.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Polite little hiccups from the neck up, arms still at sides. | They copied your volume but not your body, or they are self-conscious in week eight and reverting. | Stop calling instructions. Walk up to individuals and hiccup enormously at them, one at a time. Physical modelling beats verbal correction here. |
| The room locks into a unison beat, like a metronome. | Group instinct to synchronise. It kills the contagion premise and makes the whole thing feel like a drill. | Call 'break the rhythm' and deliberately hiccup on the off-beat, loudly, twice. It scatters within five seconds. |
| Two or three people stand at the edge doing almost nothing while the rest go for it. | They think this is optional performance rather than a room-wide warm-up, or they arrived tense. | Do not single them out verbally. Walk your own hiccups over to that edge and stay there for fifteen seconds. Proximity usually does it. |
| The countdown lands flat and the release is a mumble. | You counted too quietly or too fast, so nobody felt the pressure build during the freeze. | Count slowly and loudly, with a gap between numbers. The silence between the counts is what makes the release land. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Run exactly as written. One group, whole floor. During the walking round you can reach every person, so make a point of passing each one at least once. |
| **14–17** | Same structure, but shorten the on-the-spot round by about ten seconds and give that time to the walking round — more bodies means contagion needs longer to cross the room. Keep everyone in one group. |
| **18–20** | Split the floor in two halves for the walking round only, with a clear invisible line down the middle, so nobody has to weave. Keep the pitch ladder and the cure as one whole room. Take the top of the ladder five seconds shorter; twenty voices at squeak pitch is plenty. |

## Variations

**Spot only** — Cut the walking round. Everyone stays on their own mark for the whole thing, hiccupping and catching from whoever is nearest.  
*Easier — removes navigation and proximity worry, useful with a nervous or very large group, or a cramped room.*

**Named handover** — During the walking round, each hiccup must be aimed at one specific person by eye contact. That person must return it within a second before passing it on.  
*Harder — adds precision and a one-second offer-and-return clock on top of the physical release.*

**Ladder both ways** — After the pitch ladder tops out, call it back down to a subterranean growl over thirty seconds, then run the cure from there.  
*Different emphasis — puts the focus on tracking a caller's energy up and down, which is the showcase skill you are actually building.*

## Debrief

1. **What happened to the room's energy when I started calling the ladder?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names that it rose because one voice led it, not because everyone decided independently — that they were following a caller. That is the point; move on.

2. **Who was easier to catch a hiccup from — the loudest person or the nearest?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* The nearest, almost always. If someone says that, you have the handle for tonight: energy travels by proximity and attention, not volume. Stop there.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. Arm's length is a hard rule during the walking round — say it, then watch for it. Anyone with a voice injury, breathing condition, pregnancy, hernia, recent abdominal or chest surgery, or back trouble does the whole thing at half size or arms-only, with no volume; say this before you demonstrate, not after. Anyone can sit out and clap the rhythm from a chair instead. The pitch ladder is optional for anyone protecting their voice for the showcase — tell them to stay at their comfortable pitch and go bigger in the body instead. Loud and sudden; flag it if you have anyone noise-sensitive.

## Why it works

Audience-energy management is a following skill before it is a leading skill. You cannot steer a room's temperature until you have felt one being steered. This puts a single caller — you — in charge of a measurable variable, pitch, and moves it up in visible steps while everyone tracks it. The contagion rule teaches the second half: energy moves person to person by proximity and return, not by broadcast. The freeze-and-release then demonstrates the mechanism that showcase performers most often miss — that a held pause raises what follows it. All of that lands in the body in five minutes, before anyone has to think about it on a stage. The cue 'serve the show, not your bit' becomes literal here: nobody's individual hiccup is interesting, and the room only works because everyone gives theirs to the person next to them.

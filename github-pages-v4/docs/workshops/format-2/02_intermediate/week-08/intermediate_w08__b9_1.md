# 🏟️ Showcase round — The Scoreline

![The Scoreline](../img/intermediate_w08__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Two teams play the same timed improv game one after the other while a referee whistles fouls and the audience applauds a winner.

`Players 10–20` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 8 is the public showcase, and this is the spine of it. Seven weeks of two-person scene work, offers and heightening have been played to a room of classmates who already forgive everything. The Scoreline puts the same skills in front of strangers with a clock, a whistle and an applause meter. It comes after the warm-up and the crew brief, and it feeds the bow: the moment the group discovers that a show holds together because people hand each other the ball, not because anyone scored.

## What students actually learn

- That an audience rewards clarity and generosity far more reliably than it rewards jokes, because they hear the applause land on the round where someone gave their partner the win.
- That a whistle is information, not punishment — they can be stopped mid-sentence, correct, and keep playing without collapsing.
- That performing to a public room feels survivable. The first laugh from a stranger changes what they think they are allowed to do.
- That the scoreboard is a frame for the evening, not a verdict on them.

## Setup

Two benches of chairs angled towards the audience, left and right, with a clear playing space between them. One spare chair centre stage for One Prop. Audience seating faces the playing space. You need a whistle, a scoreboard the audience can read (flip cards, a small whiteboard, or numbers on paper), and a running order card for the referee listing all six games and their rules. Brief the referee and scorekeeper before doors: the referee runs the room and the clock, the scorekeeper flips numbers and does nothing else. Decide team names in advance and write them on the board. Have water on each bench. If you have front of house or tech, tell them they are named in the bow.

## How to run it — 30 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 3 | Host opens. Three house rules, referee introduced, one whistle blown so the sound is known. Referee names the four fouls and the one-point penalty. Introduce both benches by team name. Move fast — this is the top of a show, not a briefing. |
| 5 | Round one, Open Scene. Referee reads the rule, takes one place from the audience. Team A plays ninety seconds, then Team B plays the same place for ninety seconds. Referee raises a hand over each bench, audience applauds, scorekeeper posts two and one. |
| 5 | Round two, Story One Word. Take a story title from the audience. Each team lines up and tells the story a word at a time, about seventy seconds each. Whistle waffling. Score by applause and post. |
| 5 | Round three, Emotion Dial. Take a relationship or place. Two players from each team, ninety seconds each, referee calls numbers one to ten twice per scene. Team B goes first this round. Score and post. |
| 5 | Round four, One Prop. Set the chair centre. Each team gets seventy-five seconds, players taking turns to make the chair into something else and show it in use. Whistle anything unclear or repeated. Score and post. |
| 4 | Round five, Tag Scene, double points. Take a suggestion. Each team runs eighty seconds of tag-outs, every new scene starting from the last line spoken. Referee announces the doubling before play. Score and post double. |
| 3 | Close. Referee reads the running total and names the winner in one sentence. Both teams walk forward and bow together. Then name referee, scorekeeper, tech and front of house, and let each bow. Host thanks the audience and ends it. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Top of the show, to the audience, while benches settle | *"Loud for both sides. You are half the show."* |
| First scene where a player argues with an offer | *"Whistle. Take the offer. Point the other way."* |
| A player lists options instead of committing | *"Choose. Any choice."* |
| Mid-round, when a scene starts building on itself | *"Keep it. That's the game — play it again."* |
| A player starts performing at the audience rather than to their partner | *"Talk to your partner. We can all hear you."* |
| Tag Scene, when tags dry up | *"Tag it. Last line, new scene."* |
| Before the applause score, every round | *"Hands up for this bench. Now this one."* |
| Final round, before play | *"Double points. Serve the show, not your bit."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Rounds start within ten seconds of the last one ending. The referee's whistle stops play once or twice a round and the players adjust without sulking or apologising. Players on the bench watch their own team and laugh at the other one. Scenes are short, clear and about two people rather than about one person's idea. The audience applauds without being begged. Two or three moments genuinely land, and several do not, and the show keeps moving through both. At the bow, the players look at each other before they look at the scoreboard.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The referee whistles four or five times in a single ninety-second scene and the scene never gets going. | The referee is enforcing the letter of the fouls rather than protecting the show's pace. | Brief the referee beforehand: two whistles a round is plenty, and only whistle what the audience can also see. If it happens live, catch their eye from the wings and hold up two fingers. |
| Players start pushing gags, mugging to the audience and stepping on each other's lines to get the laugh. | The scoreboard has become the point. Competition has overridden the partner. | Whistle talking over a partner immediately and award the point loudly. Between rounds, have the referee restate one line: the applause goes to the team we can follow. |
| Applause is roughly equal every round and the scoring feels arbitrary. | A small or polite audience, or the referee is raising both hands too quickly for the room to respond. | Have the referee hold each hand up for a full four seconds and say the team name. If it is genuinely level, call it level, give two points each and move on. Nobody minds. |
| One or two players never enter a scene across the whole showcase. | Nerves plus a bench where the same three confident players keep tagging in first. | Before the show, assign each bench a running order for the two-hander games so everyone plays at least twice. The referee can call names rather than waiting for volunteers. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Two benches of five to seven. Everyone plays in the group games and everyone gets at least two two-handers. Run all five rounds as timed. Pull the referee and scorekeeper from staff or a guest, not from the players. |
| **14–17** | Two benches of seven to nine. Take the referee and scorekeeper from the class and rotate them at the halfway point so four students get a crew role. Cut Story One Word to sixty seconds a team to protect the clock. |
| **18–20** | Two benches of eight to nine, plus a crew of referee, scorekeeper and a host who does the opening and the close. Drop round four, One Prop, and stretch the remaining four rounds. Post a strict entry order on each bench so no one sits out a whole show. |

## Variations

**No Scoreboard** — Run the same six games and the same whistle, but do not keep score. The referee announces each round and the audience applauds simply as applause. Both teams bow at the end.  
*Easier. Removes the competitive pull entirely for groups where nerves are high or one bench is much more experienced.*

**Referee Rotation** — Change referee every round. The outgoing referee joins their bench, a new player picks up the whistle. Each referee must read the game rule aloud and take the suggestion themselves.  
*Harder. Players have to hold the room, the clock and the fouls while still being ready to play, which is the actual job of running a show.*

**Audience Fouls** — Give the audience one collective foul call. Any time they think a player is playing for themselves rather than the scene, they can call it by raising both hands. The referee decides whether to award the point.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts attention from the referee's judgement to the audience's, which is what players will actually be reading in every show after this one.*

## Debrief

1. **Which round got the biggest response, and what was actually happening in it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They describe a mechanic rather than a punchline — two people agreeing fast, a pattern repeating, someone clearly wanting something. If they only name a funny line, ask what the other player did just before it.

2. **When the whistle went, what did you do next?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says they took the note and carried on within a line or two. Someone else admits they froze or apologised. Both are useful. Move on once the room can name the difference between a correction and a rejection.

3. **Did the score change how you played?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Honest admission that it did — they pushed, or hid, or tried to top their teammate. The useful end point is naming the moment where handing a partner the ball got a better response than keeping it.

4. **What would you want the audience to remember from tonight?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something about the group rather than an individual moment. If every answer is about their own best line, name that out loud and leave it there — it is the honest state of a first showcase.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    Tag Scene involves one player touching another to tag out. Get consent before doors open: agree the tag is a light touch on the upper arm or shoulder, and ask if anyone would rather be tagged by a spoken word instead. Anyone who prefers verbal tagging says so in front of their bench so their teammates know; the referee notes it. No one is required to opt in and nothing changes for them if they do not. Warn the room about the whistle before you first use it, and if anyone is sound-sensitive, swap to a handbell or a spoken "foul". The laughing-at-someone's-expense foul is real — call it, including on audience members who heckle, and the referee has authority to stop the show. Anyone can sit a round out on their bench without explanation; the referee simply calls the next name. Keep the playing space clear of cables and make sure the chair used in One Prop is sturdy enough to stand beside, not on.

## Why it works

Audience-energy management is a feedback skill, not a performance style, and it needs a feedback signal fast enough to learn from. The applause score supplies one every four or five minutes. Players make a choice, the room responds, the response is measured and posted, and they carry that information into the next round — five times in half an hour. The competitive frame is a delivery mechanism, not the point. What it does is remove the option of playing safely for a whole evening, because a bench that hedges gets quieter applause than a bench that commits, and everyone in the room hears the difference. The four fouls then point the learning in one direction: every penalty punishes taking the show for yourself — blocking, waffling, talking over, laughing at someone. So the fastest route to points is serving the other person and the scene. That is the cue made mechanical. By the bow, the group has run a real feedback loop on a real audience, which is the only way that skill installs.

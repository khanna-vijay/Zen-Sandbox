# 😄 Laughter Blast — The Sneeze Choir

![The Sneeze Choir](../img/intermediate_w01__b2_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> The whole room builds one enormous fake sneeze together, over and over, until the breath-holding turns into laughing.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 01 run-sheet](../week-01.md)

## Why this activity, here

First block of the first week back. The room has just re-contracted and is about to spend the session on two-person scenes and finding the game. This is the physical rehearsal of that idea before anyone has to do it in words: a pattern gets set, repeated, then varied. It warms voices and lungs, gets the first laugh out of the way early, and gives you a shared reference — 'we did the build twice, then you knew what the third one was' — to point back to when scenes need their pattern named.

## What students actually learn

- That a laugh can come from the wait, not the punchline — they feel the delay working on them before anyone explains it.
- That two repetitions are enough to make a room predict the third, which is the whole basis of finding a game.
- That making a loud, ugly noise in front of fourteen adults costs nothing, which lowers the price of the next risk they take.

## Setup

No chairs, no props. Whole group standing in one loose circle or a wide arc facing you, arm's length apart — you need to be seen by everyone and you need room to swing an arm. Do not pre-announce the activity. Check nobody is standing directly under a low fitting or in a doorway.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Demonstrate twice, no explanation. Two enormous fake sneezes. Then: 'Everyone. That. Together.' Teach the arm signal — arm up is the breath, arm down is the sneeze. Run it twice. |
| 1 | Three unison rounds, each build longer. Round one short. Round two a touch longer. Round three stretched until the room starts laughing on the wind-up. Only then drop your arm. |
| 1 | Four sneeze types, one build each: tiny mouse sneeze, opera sneeze, dog sneeze, slow-motion sneeze. You go first and loudest on every one. Keep it moving, no notes between. |
| 1 | Sneeze storm. Thirty seconds of everyone sneezing freely, own rhythm, no signal. Then raise your arm. Wait for the room to notice and gather. One final unison sneeze on the drop. |
| 1 | Cut it dead on the drop. Ask one debrief question. Take three or four quick answers. Do not let it become a conversation. Move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Straight after your first two demo sneezes | *"Uglier than that. Nobody's judging the sneeze."* |
| During the second unison round | *"Watch the arm. Breath goes up with it."* |
| Mid-way through the third, stretched build | *"Not yet. Stay in the breath."* |
| When the laughing starts before you drop | *"That's it — that's the wait."* |
| Calling the sneeze types | *"All of you, same type, one breath."* |
| Opening the sneeze storm | *"Own rhythm. Don't wait for anyone."* |
| Raising your arm to close the storm | *"Find the arm. Come back together."* |
| Immediately after the final sneeze | *"Stop. Hands down. One question."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    By the third build the room is laughing before the release, and the release is loud and messy and lands within half a second across twenty people. Eyes come up off the floor. Nobody is checking whether their sneeze is good. When you raise your arm at the end of the storm, the room gathers without being told.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Polite, quiet sneezes. Everyone doing about forty per cent. | You demonstrated at forty per cent, or you explained before you demonstrated so it became a task rather than a copy. | Do one more yourself, absurdly big and physically committed — bend at the waist. Say 'again, mine was bigger'. Commitment is copied, not instructed. |
| The unison release smears out over two seconds; people sneeze whenever. | They cannot see your arm, or you dropped it too fast to be read. | Move to where everyone sees you. Drop the arm hard and wide, like a conductor's cut-off. Run one more round purely to fix the timing. |
| The third build collapses into chatter and self-conscious giggling and the sneeze never really happens. | You held past the point where the tension was funny into where it was just long. | n/a — the release cue. |
| One or two people standing with arms folded, not joining. | Volume in front of strangers, week one. Normal, not defiance. | Do not single them out. Keep going. Most join by the sneeze storm, when nobody can hear any individual. Leave the rest alone. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Everyone in it, including you. Keep all six steps as written; with a small room the unison lands easily, so spend the extra attention on stretching build three longer than feels comfortable. |
| **14–17** | One wide arc facing you rather than a circle — you need every eye on the arm. Cut the sneeze types from four to three (mouse, opera, slow-motion) so the storm still gets its full thirty seconds. |
| **18–20** | Arc facing you, two rows if the room is narrow, taller people behind. Stand on nothing, but step back so the arm is visible from the ends. Cut to three sneeze types. Expect the unison release to be slightly ragged — do not fix it, the mess is fine at this size. |

## Variations

**Yawn Choir** — Same structure, but the group builds and releases an enormous yawn instead of a sneeze. Build is a slow inhale, release is the vocalised yawn.  
*Easier. Less volume, less exposure, and yawns are contagious, so the unison happens by itself. Use it with a very reserved 9am room.*

**Split Choir** — Divide into two halves. Left half breathes while right half holds. Alternate builds, then bring both in for a single shared release on a two-handed drop.  
*Harder. Requires listening across the room and holding your impulse while someone else releases theirs — closer to scene-partner timing.*

**Name the Pattern** — After round three, stop for ten seconds and ask: 'What did we all know was coming?' Take one answer. Then run the sneeze types.  
*Shifts emphasis from warm-up to game identification. Makes the pattern explicit before the two-person scene work, at the cost of some momentum.*

## Debrief

1. **Where did the laugh actually happen?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'Before the sneeze.' 'In the waiting.' Someone naming the third build specifically. If you get that in one word from two people, move on.

2. **How did you know what the third one was going to be?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'You'd done it twice already.' 'We'd all agreed it without saying.' You want the word pattern or the idea of a rule the room had learned. Do not explain it further — you will use it in the next block.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point, so no consent step needed. Say clearly at the start that these are fake sneezes — mouth closed on the release or turn to the side, no spraying at each other. Repeated deep breathing can make people light-headed: tell the room that anyone who feels dizzy should stop, breathe normally and sit down, and that this is expected rather than a problem. Anyone with asthma, a chest condition, vertigo, or who is pregnant can mime the build without the deep inhale and still be fully in it. Volume is high — flag it for anyone noise-sensitive and let them stand near the door. Sitting participants do the whole thing seated; nothing here needs standing.

## Why it works

Game identification (D3.S1) depends on a group noticing a repeated pattern and predicting its next instance. This block installs that experience in the body before it is asked for in scenes. Two identical builds establish a rule. The third build extends the interval, and the laughter that arrives during the wait is the audible evidence that everyone in the room is running the same prediction. That is precisely what the cue — 'if this is true, what else is true?' — asks a player to do in a two-person scene: take the established pattern and follow it forward. The sneeze types then demonstrate variation within a fixed frame, which is heightening. Nobody has to be funny for any of it to work, so the whole mechanism is visible without skill obscuring it.

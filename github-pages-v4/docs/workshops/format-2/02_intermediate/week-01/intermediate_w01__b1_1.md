# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Unreasonably Particular

![Unreasonably Particular](../img/intermediate_w01__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> A fast round of tiny personal fussiness that puts every voice in the room before anyone has to be interesting.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 01 run-sheet](../week-01.md)

## Why this activity, here

First block of the first week. The room has just re-contracted and nobody has performed yet. This puts every voice into the air inside sixty seconds, on material nobody has to invent. It feeds the two-person scene work later: the fussiness people name is a first unusual thing, and lap two is the first act of noticing someone else's pattern and repeating it back.

## What students actually learn

- Their own ordinary specifics are already interesting enough to build on — no invention required.
- Speaking in the circle costs nothing, so the next ask is smaller.
- Listening for one other person's detail is a thing you can do while waiting for your own turn.

## Setup

Chairs pushed back. One standing circle, everyone able to see everyone. No props, no paper. Know your own example before you start and make it small and slightly embarrassing — the size of yours sets the size of theirs.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Form the circle. Give the prompt and your own example. Then have everyone turn left and both talk at once for twenty seconds about their thing. Cut it. |
| 2 | Lap one. One sentence each, five to seven seconds, no commentary. Anyone with a duplicate taps their own chest instead. Keep the pace hard. |
| 1 | Lap two, same direction, three seconds each. Point at someone and say their thing back in three words. Accuracy only. Repeats allowed. |
| 1 | Ask one debrief question. Take two or three answers. Stop and move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Before the overlap turn | *"Both of you talk. Nobody is listening. Go."* |
| Start of lap one | *"One sentence. Small and true."* |
| When someone starts explaining or justifying | *"Just the thing. Next."* |
| Mid lap one, if the pace sags | *"Keep it moving — seven seconds."* |
| Start of lap two | *"Point, three words, accuracy not wit."* |
| When someone hesitates on lap two | *"Anyone. Say what you heard."* |
| On time, ending | *"Hold that. Chairs in."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Lap one moves without gaps and nobody waits to be prompted. Details are small and domestic rather than grand or funny. On lap two people can actually produce someone else's thing, and a couple of chest-taps happen, which tells you the room noticed an overlap. The whole thing ends with people slightly warmer and no one having tried to be clever.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| People give long stories with explanations attached. | Your own example was too big, or you paused after the first person and let it become a share. | Cut in with 'just the thing, next'. Reset the size by naming three tiny examples yourself and restarting the lap from where it stalled. |
| Lap two collapses — people can't recall anyone's thing. | Lap one ran too slowly and became background noise, or people were rehearsing their own line. | Say the requirement out loud again, then allow the person to their left to feed them a name. Move on rather than pausing to fix each one. |
| Long silence when someone's turn comes. | They are searching for something interesting rather than something true. | Say 'tap your chest and we come back'. Return to them at the end of the lap. Never leave them standing in silence. |
| The room turns it into a laugh competition, each answer bigger than the last. | An early answer landed a laugh and the pattern set. | Say 'smaller and duller' and give a deliberately flat example of your own. Keep the pace up so there is no room for performance. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Lap one at a comfortable seven seconds each; you will finish under time, so take three debrief answers instead of two. |
| **14–17** | One circle. Push lap one to five or six seconds each and cut anyone at their first full stop. Lap two stays three seconds. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten, run both simultaneously in opposite corners; you float and call the laps for both at once. Rebrief as one group. |

## Variations

**Pass the chest tap** — Allow anyone to tap their chest at any point in lap one instead of speaking, no duplicate required.  
*easier — gives a visible, non-verbal opt-out for a nervous room while still marking their turn*

**Third lap: the consequence** — Add a forty-second third lap. Each person names someone else's particularity and one thing that must therefore also be true of them.  
*harder — moves the room directly onto the session cue and demands inference, not recall*

**Two things, one lie** — On lap one each person gives two particularities, one invented. On lap two, name which one you think is real.  
*different emphasis — shifts attention from speaking to reading, and shows how invented detail sounds thinner than true detail*

## Debrief

1. **Which one do you still remember, and what made it stick?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They name a concrete detail — the angle of the fork, the specific bus seat — rather than a person they like. That tells you specificity did the work, not charm.

2. **How much did you have to make up?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'None of it.' Then you can say that is the standard for the scenes later — the material is already there.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. The chest tap is on your own body. Anyone may tap instead of speaking at any point and you never ask why. Nothing personal is required — a preference about cutlery is enough, and you should model at that level so nobody feels invited to disclose. If someone is standing-averse, run the circle seated; the exercise is unaffected. In two-circle configurations keep both within your sightline so nobody is left unattended.

## Why it works

Game identification starts with noticing a first unusual thing and treating it as reliable. Here the unusual thing is handed to you by a real person, so nobody is spending attention on invention. Lap one plants twenty specifics. Lap two forces retrieval, which is the same muscle as spotting the odd detail your scene partner just dropped. The three-word limit strips out commentary, so what remains is the pattern itself — the raw material the cue 'if this is true, what else is true?' operates on for the rest of the session. The simultaneous overlap at the start exists only to break voice inhibition before anyone is heard alone.

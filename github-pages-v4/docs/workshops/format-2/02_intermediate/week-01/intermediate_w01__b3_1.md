# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Catch the Glitch

![Catch the Glitch](../img/intermediate_w01__b3_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> The room marches as one, someone breaks the pattern, and everyone copies the break within two seconds.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 01 run-sheet](../week-01.md)

## Why this activity, here

Opens Week 1 of the scene block, before the re-contract and the first two-person scenes. It puts the session cue in the body first: a pattern appears, the room names it and extends it. Later, when they hunt for game in a scene, they already know the feeling of spotting the first odd thing and treating it as the truth of the world.

## What students actually learn

- Copying is not passive. You have to decide what the change actually was before you can join it.
- One deviation is enough to organise a whole group — you don't need a clever idea, just a clear one.
- The knock-on change is usually obvious once you stop hunting for something better.

## Setup

Clear floor space so everyone can stand in a loose circle with arms free. No chairs in the way. No props. Note anyone who has told you about knees, hips or balance before the session starts — you will offer them the seated option in the first thirty seconds.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Everyone up and marching on the spot to your clap. Knees low. Call "baseline" until the tempo locks. Give the rule in one breath: named person changes one thing, everyone copies inside two seconds. |
| 2 | Round one. Call five or six names, roughly fifteen seconds apart. After each copy lands, call "and what else is true?" and take whatever knock-on the room offers. Keep clapping. |
| 1 | Round two. Stop calling names. Anyone glitches. One at a time; if two collide, take the bigger one. Push the tempo slightly faster than comfortable. |
| 1 | Freeze mid-glitch. Take three quick "we are all..." answers, six words max, no commentary. Shake arms and legs out. Settle for the next piece. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| During the opening march, before the rule | *"Find the clap. One tempo, one room."* |
| First named glitch, when copying is slow | *"Copy it now, work it out later."* |
| After each copy has landed in round one | *"And what else is true?"* |
| When someone changes three things at once | *"One change. Make it readable."* |
| Start of round two | *"No names. Anyone. Go."* |
| When two glitches collide | *"Take the bigger one."* |
| When the tempo sags in round two | *"Keep the feet going."* |
| On the freeze | *"Hold it. Three voices: we are all..."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room looks like one slightly deranged machine. Changes spread across the group in under two seconds without anyone looking around for confirmation. The "what else is true" additions come fast and are unglamorous — a matching head tilt, a grunt on the beat. People are breathing hard and grinning, and nobody has tried to be funny.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Half the room is watching the glitcher instead of marching. | They have made copying into a spectator decision rather than a physical reflex. | Call "feet keep going" and clap louder. Tell them a wrong copy is better than a late one. |
| Glitches are elaborate — a whole character, a voice, a spin. | Someone has read the exercise as an invitation to perform. | Freeze, restate: one limb, one angle, one rhythm. Model a dull change yourself and let the room copy it. |
| Round two stalls — nobody glitches, everyone marches politely. | Waiting to be chosen; the group has no permission signal. | Name one person quietly to seed it, then withdraw. Or count down "three, two, one, somebody" once. |
| Two or three glitches land at once and the room fragments. | Nobody is arbitrating and the group splits into copying clusters. | Call "take the bigger one" and point at it. Do it twice and the room self-arbitrates after that. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Call five names in round one; everyone gets a turn or nearly. Round two runs the full minute — small groups need longer to risk the first unnamed glitch. |
| **14–17** | One circle, slightly wider. Call six names in round one, deliberately including the quietest people. Timing unchanged. |
| **18–20** | Two half-circles facing each other across the room so everyone can see a glitch land. Call six names, three from each side. Copies travel across both halves — say so explicitly. |

## Variations

**Named only** — Skip round two. Keep calling names for the full two-and-a-half minutes, going slower, fifteen to twenty seconds between calls.  
*Easier — removes the pressure of self-starting for a nervous or very new group.*

**Sound layer** — From round two, every glitch must include a sound as well as a movement. The room copies both.  
*Harder — voice makes people self-conscious, and the two-second copy window stops them curating it.*

**Name the pattern** — After each copy in round one, instead of "what else is true", ask one person to say in four words what the room is now doing.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from physical joining to verbal labelling, which is closer to the naming work in scene game identification.*

## Debrief

1. **What did you do in the two seconds before you copied?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone admits they guessed, or picked one detail and committed to it rather than getting all of it right. If they say "I waited to see what everyone else did", name that as the thing to stop doing.

2. **When I asked "what else is true", where did your addition come from?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* "It was just the next obvious thing" — the limp meant the arm dropped, the fast beat meant shorter breath. If answers sound clever or invented, point out the obvious ones were the ones the room could join.

3. **How is that different from waiting for a good idea?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something like: the pattern was already there, they just had to follow it out. Move on as soon as one person says the first change made the second one obvious.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact in this activity. Offer a seated version from the start, not as an afterthought — marching becomes hands on thighs, or foot taps, and all glitches can be done from the waist up. Say this before the first march so nobody has to ask. Anyone with knee, hip or balance issues sets their own knee height. Nobody is put on the spot for a "we are all..." answer; take volunteers.

## Why it works

Game identification depends on treating the first deviation as information rather than noise. Here the deviation is physical and unmissable, so nobody can argue about whether it happened. The two-second copy window removes the evaluation step — you cannot judge whether a glitch is good, only join it. Then "what else is true?" forces the same move that builds a scene game: take the established oddity as a premise and derive the next consequence from it, not from your own invention. The body learns the sequence — notice, accept, extend — before the head has to do it in dialogue.

# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Second Shift

![Second Shift](../img/intermediate_w06__b3_2.jpg){ .infographic }

> One repeating action never stops; it just keeps changing owner, handed over on eye contact and copied exactly before the first person lets go.

`Players 8–24` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 06 run-sheet](../week-06.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 6 opens the ensemble half of the term. Week 5 left them competent at building a scene with one partner. This block moves the unit of attention from the pair to the room, and it does it physically before anyone has to talk. It feeds directly into the support and edit work later in the session: an edit only works if the person taking over has actually received what was there. Second Shift makes that receipt visible in the body, so when you say "you left before they had it" in a scene, they already know the feeling.

## What students actually learn

- That an offer isn't given until it's been taken — the giver's job continues after they've started passing.
- How to hold attention on one other person in a room of twenty without narrowing to a private duet.
- That copying exactly is harder, and more useful, than adding something of your own.

## Setup

One circle, standing, everyone able to see everyone. Clear the floor of bags and chairs. No props. If the floor is hard or the room is below a neighbour, choose actions that don't involve heavy stamping. You need nothing but space and a voice loud enough to call over the noise.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Demonstrate. Start a sawing action with its sound. Catch one person's eye, hold it, keep sawing until they match you exactly, then stop. Point out that you carried on until they had it. |
| 1 | Restart the same action and release it into the circle. One action only. Enforce the two rules out loud as it travels: never stops, never changes. |
| 2 | Add a second action from the opposite side of the circle. Two actions circulate independently. Correct anyone holding both — they pass one on immediately. |
| 1 | Call for speed: a handover every three or four seconds. Let it run about forty seconds, then call "last shift" and let both actions finish and stop. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As the first handover begins | *"Hold their eye. Don't leave yet."* |
| A giver drops out early and the action wobbles | *"Copy first. Then rest."* |
| Someone embellishes what they received | *"Exact copy. Not better."* |
| Handovers are only going to neighbours | *"Across the circle. Anyone."* |
| Second action enters | *"Two engines running. Take one."* |
| Somebody ends up doing both | *"Give one away. Now."* |
| Calling the speed round | *"Three seconds. Pass. Pass."* |
| Closing | *"Last shift — finish it and stop."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Two actions moving round the circle with visible overlap: for a second or two, two people are doing the same thing at once, and only then does one of them stop. The sounds stay recognisable ten handovers in. Heads are up and eyes are moving. Nobody is waiting to be chosen in turn order. At "last shift" the room stops within a beat of itself, and there's a small silence before anyone laughs.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The action has become something completely different by the tenth pass. | Givers are dropping out at the moment of eye contact rather than at the moment of accurate copying, so each receiver is copying a guess. | Freeze it. Ask who has it and what it was originally. Restart and side-coach the overlap: "two of you doing it, then one." |
| Actions grow bigger and funnier each time — a stir becomes a whole cocktail routine. | The group is playing for laughs, which is the default when a warm-up feels exposing. | Say "exact copy, not better" and mean it. Praise the plainest, most faithful handover you can see. It resets the norm faster than a rule restated. |
| One or two people never receive anything. | Passes are going to friends and to whoever is easiest to catch eyes with. | Call "across the circle" during the run. If it persists, name it neutrally afterwards: "four of you never got one — go looking next time." |
| When the second action enters, both collapse into one. | People are watching the room rather than the single person offering to them. | Stop, reseed the second action from the far side, and coach: "you only need to see one person." |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Actions come round fast, so introduce the second action after about forty-five seconds rather than waiting the full minute. In the speed round add a third action — the circle is small enough to carry it. |
| **14–17** | One circle, run exactly as written. Two actions is the right load. If it settles early, tighten the speed round to two-second handovers rather than adding a third action. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten in opposite corners. Seed the first action in each yourself, then let each circle add its own second action on your call. You side-coach both from between them; drop the speed round to thirty seconds so you can call "last shift" for both at once. |

## Variations

**Silent shift** — Run the whole thing with movement only, no sound. Handovers rely purely on eye contact and the copied shape.  
*Easier — removes the vocal exposure that stops some adults committing in the first minute.*

**Long-distance** — Ban passing to anyone standing within two people of you. Every handover must cross the circle.  
*Harder — forces genuine scanning of the whole room instead of a comfortable local exchange.*

**One degree of drift** — The receiver may change exactly one thing — speed, size, or sound — at the moment they take it, then holds it steady.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from faithful copying to tracking gradual group-made change, which is closer to how a scene actually evolves.*

## Debrief

1. **What told you it was safe to stop?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something concrete about the other person: their hands matched, the sound came back at me, they weren't looking at me any more because they were busy doing it. Not "I just felt it was time."

2. **When the second action came in, what did you stop noticing?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* An admission that they narrowed — lost track of the other action, or of half the circle — and that the fix was watching one person properly rather than watching everything.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point. Anyone who prefers not to make vocal sound can run the action silently and still give and receive normally — say this before you start, not as a concession afterwards. Offer a chair at the edge of the circle for anyone who needs to sit; seated players pass and receive on the same terms. Keep actions low-impact: no jumping, and swap stamping for a hand slap on the thigh if knees or the floor are a concern. If someone finds sustained eye contact difficult, tell them to aim between the eyebrows — it reads the same from across a circle.

## Why it works

Support work fails when players treat an offer as something you make rather than something that has to arrive. This exercise makes the arrival the only thing that ends your turn. You cannot rest by declaring you've passed; you rest when the other person is demonstrably carrying it. That is "follow the follower" made physical — the leader is whoever currently holds the action, and leadership transfers only through a moment where both people are doing the same thing. The two-action layer then adds the real ensemble condition: more is happening than you can track, so you have to trust that the other half of the room is being held by someone else. Both habits transfer straight into edits and tag-outs later in the session.

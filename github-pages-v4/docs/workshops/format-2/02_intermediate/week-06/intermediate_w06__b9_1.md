# 🏟️ Showcase round — The Scoreline

![The Scoreline](../img/intermediate_w06__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Two teams play the same timed improv game one after the other while a referee whistles fouls and the audience applauds a winner.

`Players 10–20` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 06 run-sheet](../week-06.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 6 has spent the morning on the edit and on making a partner's idea the strongest thing in the room. Those habits hold in a quiet workshop. This block adds a scoreboard, a whistle and an audience — the three things that usually make people start playing for themselves — and asks whether support survives. It closes the session on a shared performance and hands Week 7 an ensemble that already knows what it feels like to hold a form under pressure.

## What students actually learn

- That competing as a team means feeding your teammate rather than out-playing them; the points come from the pair, not the individual.
- That a foul is information, not shame — you hear the whistle, adjust, carry on inside the same scene.
- That an audience response is survivable, and that applause for the other bench does not cost you anything.
- That a form with rules and a clock removes the burden of deciding what to do next.

## Setup

Two rows of chairs facing an empty playing area, five or six chairs per bench. One spare chair centre stage for One Prop. A whistle for you. A flip-pad or whiteboard for the scorekeeper, with both team names written up. A visible timer or a phone you can glance at. Pick and write your five games in order before the class arrives so you are not choosing under pressure. Team names take ten seconds each — do not let them become a bit.

## How to run it — 30 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 4 | Split into two teams and seat the benches. State the three house rules and blow the whistle. Name the four fouls. Give the scorekeeper the pad. Ask for a show of hands from anyone opting out of shoulder taps. |
| 5 | Round one, Open Scene. Name it, read the rule, take a place from the room. Team A plays ninety seconds, then Team B to the same place. Applause vote, score up. |
| 5 | Round two, Story One Word. Read the rule, take a story title. Team B goes first this time, then Team A. Whistle waffling hard here — the game dies on hesitation. Applause vote, score up. |
| 5 | Round three, Emotion Dial. Take a place, call intensity numbers roughly every twenty seconds. Team A first, then Team B, same suggestion and the same sequence of numbers. Applause vote, score up. |
| 5 | Round four, Tag Scene. Read the rule, take a suggestion, remind everyone the tap is a shoulder or a called "switch". Team B first. Applause vote, score up. |
| 6 | Final round, double points — One Prop or Expert. Both teams play. Applause vote, doubled. Read the total, both teams bow together, then name and bow referee, scorekeeper, tech and front of house. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As the first scene of the block begins | *"Follow the follower."* |
| A player steers away from what their partner just built | *"Take what's there."* |
| Someone stalls hunting for a clever answer | *"Choose now. Any choice."* |
| A bench sits silent while their own team plays | *"Bench — back your players."* |
| Straight after any whistle | *"Point the other way. Keep going."* |
| Thirty seconds left in a timed scene | *"Land it where you are."* |
| Tag Scene loses its handover | *"Tag on the line, not the pause."* |
| The room starts playing to win rather than to play | *"Score's mine. Scene's yours."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Rounds start and finish on time without you chasing anyone. Players walk out fast and start before they have a plan. Benches make noise for their own team and applaud the other one. When the whistle goes, the scene absorbs it and continues — no apology, no collapse. Suggestions come only when asked. The score stays close enough that nobody mentions it, and by the bows most people cannot remember who won.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| You whistle four or five times in a single ninety-second scene and it stops dead. | You are refereeing for accuracy rather than for rhythm. The whistle is a nudge, not a verdict. | Cap yourself at two whistles per scene. Let smaller fouls go. If a player is fouling repeatedly, name the pattern in the debrief, not mid-scene. |
| Players start scoring off each other — mocking a teammate's choice for a laugh from the bench. | The competitive frame has leaked from teams onto individuals, which is exactly what the fourth foul exists to catch. | Whistle it immediately, say "foul four", give the point away, restart the scene from the same line. Do it once and the room recalibrates. |
| Applause votes are indistinguishable and the room starts arguing about it. | Both benches are polite and the audience is small, so volume carries no signal. | Say "tie, one each" and move on inside three seconds. Never re-run a vote. State up front that ties are fine and common. |
| The clock slips and you reach minute twenty-five having run three rounds. | Suggestion-taking and rule-reading are eating the round, usually because you are explaining the game twice. | Read each rule in one sentence, take the first usable suggestion you hear, and cut a round rather than shortening the bows. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Two teams of five or six. There is no separate audience — the resting bench is the audience and votes on the other team. Run all five rounds as written. Every player will play in at least three rounds; track it on the scoresheet margin. |
| **14–17** | Two teams of seven or eight. Before you start, write each team's running order on a slip and hand it to the bench, so nobody negotiates who plays while the clock runs. Drop nothing; the rounds hold. |
| **18–20** | Two teams of nine or ten. Cut one round — run four plus the double final — and use three-player versions of Open Scene and Emotion Dial so more people get on. Pre-assign every round on a bench card. Give front of house and tech to two students who then join the final round. |

## Variations

**No whistle** — Run the same six games and the same applause scoring, but drop the fouls entirely. You call the games and time them; nothing is penalised.  
*Easier. Use it with a nervous group or a first showcase — the form still holds the room, without the extra thing to fear.*

**Fouls on the bench** — Extend the four fouls to the benches. Heckling your own team, coaching from the chairs, or a suggestion nobody asked for is a whistle and a point away.  
*Harder. Support stops being something you do onstage and becomes something the whole team is accountable for.*

**Shared score** — One scoreboard, not two. Both teams play every round and you award nought to three points for the round as a whole, judged on how well players backed each other.  
*Different emphasis. Removes the win condition and puts the whole attention on mutual support, at the cost of some of the energy competition brings.*

## Debrief

1. **When you were onstage, what were you actually trying to do — win the round, or make your partner's idea work?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone admits they were doing one at the start and the other by round three, and can name the moment it switched. Move on when two or three people have located that switch.

2. **What happened in your body when the whistle went?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Concrete and physical — froze, apologised, kept talking louder. Someone should notice that recovering took about a second and cost nothing. That is the point; do not labour it.

3. **Whose round was the best one out there, either team?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* People name players from the opposing bench without prompting, and describe a specific offer or handover rather than a joke. If they only name funny moments, ask what made the scene possible.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    The only contact is the shoulder tap in Tag Scene. Before round one, ask openly for a show of hands from anyone who would rather not be touched, and tell the room those players are tagged by calling "switch" instead. Do not ask why. Anyone can sit a round out by telling their bench — the running order absorbs it silently. Say clearly that the fourth foul covers punching down at anyone in the room, including yourself. Keep the whistle at conversational volume; sound it once at the start so nobody is startled by it later. Chairs stay in place during One Prop — the prop chair is handled, not stood on. If a scene reaches material someone plainly does not want to play, whistle it and take the round in a new direction; you never need to give a reason.

## Why it works

Support Work is easy to demonstrate in a quiet room and hard to keep under observation. The scoreboard supplies exactly the pressure that normally breaks it — a reason to be seen doing well individually — and then the scoring rules refuse to reward that. Points come from the round, not the player, so the only route to a point is making the person opposite you look good. "Follow the follower" becomes literal: your partner is reacting to you while you react to them, and neither of you is steering. The four fouls make the failure modes of support audible rather than theoretical. A block, a waffle, a talk-over: each gets a sound, a consequence, and then the scene continues. Players learn the shape of the error at the moment they make it, and learn that the error is survivable, which is what stops the fear of it in the next scene.

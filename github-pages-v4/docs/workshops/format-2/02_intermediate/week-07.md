# Week 07 — Match Play — Short-Form Under Pressure

> *Under a clock and an audience, the skill is hearing what the room already has and adjusting inside the round.*

| Course | Week | Domain | Focus | Stage | Length | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Engine — Scene & Ensemble | 7/8 | D5 | `D5.S1` — Room Reading | Advanced Beginner → Competent | 180 min | 10–20 |

!!! note "Builds on"
    Support and clean edits gave them a group that can run a scene without you on the floor; this week adds a clock, a score and strangers' attention to the same group.

## 🎬 The arc of this session

They arrive keyed up — the format vote is settled, the public date is on the calendar, and the room knows it. Over the session you add the three things they have not had yet: a clock, a score and someone watching who is not on their side, and you keep the teaching narrow — hear what the room already has, and change tack before the round ends. They leave having each felt at least one round turn because they adjusted mid-flight, and with the dress run of their chosen format in their bodies.

!!! tip "Watch for"
    Begging. Nerves push people to fish — mugging to the seats, the raised eyebrow after a line, the pause held open waiting for a laugh that is not coming. Name it the moment it happens, in one plain sentence, then give the round straight back so they can fix it in play rather than in a note.

## 🎒 Before you start

- Set the room as a venue, not a studio: chairs in two banks facing a clear playing area, house lights on the seats so players can see faces. Mark the front edge of the stage with tape.
- Props: a visible countdown timer (phone on a stand is fine, projected is better), a bell or buzzer, a whiteboard for the score, pens. A hat or bowl for suggestions.
- Print two sheets: the running order for the format they voted for (Genre Night or the Match), and a one-page role list — referee/host, timekeeper, scorer, tech, players — with names left blank for Block 9.
- Review last week's homework on clean edits: pick two people who reported a clean edit that saved a scene and be ready to name them out loud in the warm-up cluster.
- Decide your seat-fillers in advance. At 10-13 you are the whole audience plus whoever is off; at 14-17 split into two teams so half are always in the seats; at 18-20 run three teams of six or seven and rotate so no one sits out two rounds running.
- Have the public-date logistics on one sheet — call time, door time, running time — and hand it out at reflection so admin does not leak into the teaching.

## 1. 🧊 Ice-breaker (5 min)

*Standing circle, no bags in hand. We are scoring things today, so let us get used to a number being said out loud about us.*

**Why here:** They walk in nervous about being judged; this puts judgement in the room immediately, low stakes, with everyone laughing at how arbitrary a score is.

### Correctly Rated

> Everyone delivers snap verdicts on ordinary things — overrated, underrated, correctly rated — and the room finds out who hedges and who commits.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Name the three verdicts: overrated, underrated, correctly rated. Nothing else counts.
2. Tell them subjects must be ordinary — a food, a film, a kitchen appliance, a season. Real opinions only, not jokes.
3. Put them in pairs. Everyone talks at once. Thirty seconds each way, three verdicts each. Call the swap out loud.
4. Bring them back to the circle. One verdict each, six words maximum, no reasons offered.
5. Whip round fast. Anyone may shout 'why?' after a verdict. That person gets five seconds to justify, then you move on.
6. Cut any justification at five seconds, mid-sentence if needed. Speed matters more than completeness.
7. Finish with a show of hands on the two verdicts that split the room hardest. Say the split out loud and move on.

## 2. 😄 Laughter Blast (5 min)

*Same circle, wider. Feet apart. We are going to make a lot of noise and it does not have to be clever.*

**Why here:** Nervous bodies are tight bodies; five minutes of shared noise gets breath and volume up before anyone has to be watched.

### Trophy and Tantrum

> The whole room swings between full-body victory celebration and full-body playground strop on your call, faster and faster, for five minutes.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand where everyone can see you. Name the two settings: WON and LOST. Nothing in between, no half-sizes.
2. Demonstrate WON yourself at full size: arms up, running on the spot, roaring. Then LOST: stamping, arms folded, wailing NOT FAIR.
3. Call WE WON. Everyone celebrates together for forty seconds. Roar with them. Nobody watches anybody — everyone is too busy.
4. Call WE LOST. Everyone strops together for forty seconds. Be louder and sulkier than anyone in the room.
5. Alternate the calls at random. Start five seconds apart, squeeze down to one. Bodies flip mid-movement — that is the point.
6. Split the circle down the middle. One half won, one half lost, facing each other, full volume, twenty seconds. Swap. Swap again.
7. Final call: everyone won AND lost at once, ten seconds of both. Shake out arms and legs. Ask one question. Move on.

**Side-coach:** *“Louder than feels sensible — you will need this volume in a room with an audience in it.”*

## 3. 🔥 Warm-up cluster (15 min)

*Three short ones, back to back, no discussion between. Each one is about noticing something outside your own head.*

**Why here:** Three warm-ups that each train one channel of room reading: pulling attention, adjusting by increments, and naming what is actually in the space.

### Pull Them In

> One player at a time works a live crowd that answers only with its feet — step in if it lands, step back if it doesn't.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Form a wide arc, everyone standing, feet on an imaginary start line. Leave a clear space of about two metres in front of the arc for the player in the middle.
2. Explain the deal: the player in the middle has ten seconds to hold the crowd using body only. No words, no props, no asking for a reaction.
3. Explain the crowd's only vocabulary: one step in if the player holds you, one step back if they don't. Feet only. No laughing to be kind, no commentary.
4. Demo it yourself for thirty seconds. Do something flat first, let them step back, change one thing, pull them in. Say out loud what you changed.
5. Run the arc. Ten seconds each, you call 'next' hard on time. Crowd returns to the start line between every player.
6. Run a second pass with the crowd already one step back, arms folded. Six to eight volunteers only, ten seconds each. They must earn the first step in.
7. Close by calling everyone into the tightest huddle they are comfortable with. One shared breath out. Straight into the next warm-up, no discussion.

### Half a Degree

> One partner talks, the other becomes a living warmth meter, and the talker adjusts mid-sentence to keep the needle up.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Split the room into two facing lines, partners opposite. Odd numbers make one trio: two talk, one meters.
2. Tell the listeners they answer with body and wordless sound only. Lean in and hum when held. Drift back and go flat when not.
3. Say the readings must be honest. Never cruel, never fake warmth. No words, no faces pulled for a joke.
4. Start line A talking about something ordinary — a journey, a meal, a queue. Tell them the job is not to be funny.
5. Tell talkers to notice the drift and change something before it lands: volume, detail, subject or pace.
6. Swap after a minute. Line B talks, line A meters, same rule.
7. Shuffle: line A steps one place left, the person off the end walks to the far end. New partners.
8. Run a final round where both partners talk at once, each metering the other with body only. Keep talking, keep reading, keep adjusting.
9. Stop the room on a clap. Ask two people what their partner did that pulled them back in. Move on.

### Name the Room

> The circle goes quiet and players take turns saying out loud, in one sentence, what state the room is actually in.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Circle everyone up standing. Tell them to stand how they honestly are — tired, flat, buzzing. No performing a mood. No fixing your face when someone looks at you.
2. Call "Scan". Ten seconds of silence, everyone looking slowly round the circle. Then call "Read" and tap one player on the shoulder.
3. The tapped player says one sentence describing the room's actual state. Not a wish, not a compliment, not a joke.
4. Take the verdict in silence. Flat palm on the chest means true. Palm turned outward means not quite. No talking, no defending, no follow-up.
5. Run rapid rounds: five-second scan, tap a new reader, one sentence, silent verdict. Cut any reading that flatters or gags, and send that player straight back in.
6. For the last round, have the reader name the room and then give one physical instruction to shift it — drop your shoulders, three seconds of shaking out.
7. Run the instruction. Scan once more. Ask whether it moved. Take a show of palms, not a discussion.
8. Finish with fifteen seconds of stillness. Everyone stands and notices the new state before you move on.

## 4. 🧠 Theory — Room Reading (20 min)

![Room Reading](../../../assets/infographics/D5.S1.jpg){ .infographic }

- **The big idea:** The audience is sending information the whole time, and it is usable inside the round. Laughter, the shape of the silence, the shuffle, the sharp intake — each tells you whether the room is ahead of you, with you, or lost. Room reading is hearing that and changing what you do next, not after the round ends.
- **Where you are on the path:** Competent looks like this: they can already run a scene without you on the floor. What they cannot yet do is stay in the scene and take in the seats at the same time. Expect tunnel vision — two players locked on each other, playing beautifully, while the room got the joke ninety seconds ago.
- **The one cue to coach:** *“Earn the laugh. Never beg for it.”*

**The three beats**

1. **Say it.** Say: the room gives you four things. Laughter that lands and stops — they got it, move on. Laughter that keeps rolling — hold, do not talk over it. Silence that leans in — they are with you, keep going. Silence that goes flat — they are lost or bored, change something. Then say the cue: earn the laugh, never beg for it. Earning is doing the scene well enough that the laugh arrives. Begging is asking for one.
2. **Show it.** Get one volunteer and play a sixty-second scene with them. Play it twice. First time, tunnel-vision it — keep piling on detail after the room has clearly got the premise, and let them watch it sag. Second time, take the same premise and cut on the first solid laugh, hard, to a new beat. Ask the seats which one they wanted to keep watching. Do not explain the answer; let them say it.
3. **Try it.** Everyone in pairs, no audience. Sixty-second scenes, all going at once. Their only job: at some point, one of you changes tack — pace, volume, subject, status — and the other follows. Thirty seconds, swap who changes. Then one question round the room: what did you change, and did your partner notice? Five minutes, no scenes shown.

!!! warning "The misunderstanding that always comes up"
    They will hear "read the room" as "give the room what it wants", which becomes chasing the biggest available laugh and abandoning the scene. Correct it directly: the room does not want a joke, it wants the scene to keep moving. Nine times out of ten the right adjustment is to cut, speed up or commit harder — not to add a gag.

## 5. 🎲 Drill A — isolation (25 min)

*Half of you in the seats, half up. Seats, you have a job — you are not being polite, you are being honest.*

**Why here:** Isolates the adjustment itself: they get one variable to move, and immediate feedback on whether the room registered it.

### More or Less

> Calibrate scene length and narrative momentum by letting the audience vote to continue or cut.

`Players 5–99` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Clear a playing area with the rest of the group seated as audience. Tell them their job is to vote honestly, out loud, with real volume.
2. Send one player forward as Director. They either pitch a one-line premise or take a simple suggestion from the room.
3. Have the Director cast two or three players from the sidelines, place them in an opening picture, and call the start of the scene.
4. Keep the Director at the edge of the playing area, facing half out — watching the scene and the audience's faces at the same time.
5. After sixty to ninety seconds, on a cliffhanger or a rising beat, the Director freezes the scene with a clear call.
6. The Director turns to the audience and asks: 'More or less?' The room shouts one or the other.
7. If 'more' clearly wins, the Director restarts the scene for another beat, then freezes and asks again. If 'less' wins — or the response is thin — the Director ends it immediately, thanks the cast, sits down.
8. Send a new Director forward with a new premise and a new cast. Keep going until time runs out.

## 6. 🎲 Drill B — under load (25 min)

*Clock on. Score on the board. Same skill, but now the room can see you seeing them.*

**Why here:** Adds the load they will actually face next week — the audience acknowledged, the clock running, the score visible.

### The Fourth Wall Accord

> Master the fourth wall by reading audience energy and making deliberate, real-time performance adjustments.

`Players 3–99` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Put one performer on the stage area. Everyone else is the live audience, seated facing them.
2. Tell the audience to react honestly but silently — lean in, still, smiling, or fidget, shift, look away. No sound, no heckling, no phones.
3. Give the performer a low-action solo premise: waiting for a delayed train, fixing a broken watch, queuing at a chemist. Nothing clever required.
4. Tell the performer to play it straight with the fourth wall up — clear physical actions, voice reaching the back wall.
5. Agree one silent cue with the performer beforehand — a slow nod from you. Give it every 45 to 60 seconds.
6. On the cue, the performer takes a micro-beat, reads the room, and picks one of two moves: Reinforce or Break.
7. Reinforce means the room is with them: drop volume slightly, deepen the world, make the audience come forward. Break means address the audience directly — eye contact, a question, a confession.
8. Have the performer say the reason out loud in one sentence — "Breaking; front row is shifting" — then resume immediately in the new dynamic.
9. Run three to four cues per performer, then cut the scene and rotate.

## 7. 🎯 Scene Lab (35 min)

*Longer rounds now. I will stop you mid-scene, and when I do I want the adjustment inside ten seconds, not a conversation about it.*

**Why here:** Longest block, and the one where the mid-round turn gets practised until it is habit rather than luck.

### The Resonance Dial

> Calibrate your stage energy in real time by responding to a dynamic audience engagement dial.

`Players 3–99` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Send two or three players up. Take a one-word suggestion from the seated group. Everyone else is the house — they watch, they don't call out.
2. Tell the players to start a grounded scene. Establish who they are, where they are, what's going on, before anything else happens.
3. Once the platform is set, call a number from one to ten every twenty to forty-five seconds. Add a short read of the room if it helps: "seven, they're leaning in."
4. On one to three, players lock onto each other. Fourth wall solid. Keep voices and actions big enough for the back row anyway.
5. On four to seven, players cheat out, widen stance and voice, and let the house in — still fully in character, no direct address.
6. On eight to ten, players thin or break the wall: direct address, a look out, an aside. Stay in character while doing it.
7. Insist every shift comes from the character's feeling, not a gear change. The number is a weather report, not a command to obey visibly.
8. Keep moving the dial for four to five minutes, then call the scene. Rotate a fresh set of players up.

## 8. 🏟️ Showcase round (30 min)

*Roles on the sheet, names in the boxes. From this point I am in the seats and not on the floor. Host, it is your room.*

**Why here:** Full dress run of the voted format with students in the roles they hold next week, so the first time it goes wrong is tonight and not in front of strangers.

### Five Tellings

> One audience suggestion becomes a plain two-minute story, then the same story is retold four more times in genres the audience picks.

`Players 10–20` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Collect one ordinary object or dull errand from each person on a slip as they arrive. No jobs, no puns. Put every slip in a hat.
2. Welcome the room. Say the shape out loud: one story, told five ways. Draw three slips, read them, let applause pick one.
3. Send three players to run the Plain Telling. No style, no jokes. They establish who they are to each other, where they are, and what one wants.
4. Have the Story Keeper write three anchors on the board: one spoken line, one object, one relationship. These bind every later telling.
5. Have the Genre Caller draw a genre card. A fresh cast retells the same story in that genre, holding all three anchors. Repeat for a second genre.
6. Reread the anchors aloud. Draw two more genres. Cast fresh players each round; anyone who has not yet performed goes on now.
7. Run the Stack: the whole company replays all five tellings in order, sixty seconds each, cut by a bell. Finish on the Plain Telling's last line.
8. Bow in one line — tech and front of house included. Name the three anchors once more, thank the room, bring lights up.

## 9. 💭 Reflection & homework (10 min)

**Deepen your improv**
1. Name the moment tonight when you knew the room had already got it. What did you do in the next ten seconds?
2. Where did you catch yourself begging? Not judging it — just say what the tell was, so you can spot it next week.

**Beyond the stage**
3. Think of a conversation this week where you kept explaining after the other person had understood. What would stopping there have cost you?

??? note "🎓 Facilitator notes"

    **Timing risks**
    - Block 9 overruns every time. Set a hard timer for the dress run and let it break the format mid-round if it must — students seeing that the clock is real is part of the lesson.
    - The theory block swells if you let them debate what counts as begging. Cap the discussion at two contributions, then move to the try-it.
    - At 18-20, Drill B eats time in changeovers. Have the next pair standing at the edge before the buzzer goes.

    **Failure modes this week**
    - Nerves turn to over-caution: everyone plays small and safe, nothing is loud enough to read. Push volume before you push content.
    - Laugh-chasing spreads. One person mugs successfully, three copy it, the whole showcase goes broad. Name it the first time, not the third.
    - The audience half goes soft and laughs at everything to be supportive, which removes all the information. Tell the seats plainly: honest response, silence included.
    - The score becomes the point and someone gets genuinely stung by losing. Keep the referee light, keep points arbitrary and say out loud that the score is a clock, not a verdict.

    **What good looks like by the end:** At least half the room visibly changes tack mid-round because of something that came back from the seats — a cut, a jump in pace, a drop in volume. The dress run finishes inside its stated time with every role filled by a student. Nobody had to be funny on demand to get through the session.

    **Carry into next week:** Names in role boxes are now fixed — send them out with the running order and their role. Note which two or three people are still tunnel-visioning; give them the first slots next week so they get their adjustment in early rather than watching nerves build.

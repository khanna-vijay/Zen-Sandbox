# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Two Millimetres

![Two Millimetres](../img/intermediate_w05__b3_3.jpg){ .infographic }

> Standing still, the room stacks three tiny postural changes and names the mood each one delivers.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 05 run-sheet](../week-05.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 5 turns character work outward — body first, feeling second. This is the first thing they do all session, before any scene, any partner, any word of dialogue. It plants the cue physically so the later blocks have something to refer back to. It feeds straight into whatever full-body character work follows: they have already felt three shifts produce three moods without deciding on any of them.

## What students actually learn

- They notice that a mood turns up uninvited when the body changes, rather than being chosen and then performed.
- They stop needing a big physical commitment to feel different — two millimetres is enough, which lowers the cost of trying something.
- They get a shared vocabulary for the session: the room heard everyone else's word, so they know their read is not the only one.
- They learn to hold stillness without filling it with a joke.

## Setup

Clear floor space, everyone standing, roughly an arm and a half apart, all facing the same wall. No chairs, no props, no music. Say beforehand that this one runs in silence and that you will cue every change and every murmur, so nobody has to guess. Anyone who cannot stand for five minutes does it seated — the same three changes work in a chair. Have your three calls memorised so you are not reading.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Set the neutral and hold it. Feet parallel, knees soft, arms hanging, eyes on a spot. Talk them into it, then go quiet for the last twenty seconds. |
| 1 | Call change one: weight into the heels, two millimetres. Hold in silence for around thirty seconds. Cue the murmur — one word, everyone together, low. |
| 1 | Call change two on top: chin down two millimetres, breath high in the chest. Hold in silence. Cue the murmur. |
| 1 | Call change three on top: one shoulder drifts forward, jaw slackens. Hold in silence. Cue the murmur. |
| 1 | Four slow steps forward carrying the whole stack. Stop. Shake it out, return to neutral, then move straight into the next block. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Setting neutral | *"Soften the knees. Let the arms hang. Eyes on one spot."* |
| Immediately after each change is called | *"Two millimetres. Not a pose."* |
| Mid-hold, when people start adjusting | *"Stop editing. Stay in it."* |
| Just before each murmur | *"One word. Don't choose a clever one."* |
| During the murmur, if it gets loud | *"Under your breath. Nobody performs it."* |
| Layering change two and three | *"Keep the first one. Add, don't swap."* |
| On the four steps | *"Carry all three. Let it walk you."* |
| After the steps | *"Shake it out. Back to nothing."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is genuinely still and genuinely quiet during the holds — no shuffling, no glances across for reassurance. The murmurs come out fast and unpolished, and you hear five or six different words at once rather than one word repeated. By the third layer, faces have changed without anyone acting. The four steps look like a different person walking, and nobody has cracked it with a laugh.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| People adopt a full character posture instead of a two-millimetre shift — hunched shoulders, dropped head, the whole thing. | They think the exercise wants a visible result, so they oversell it. | Call it while it happens: "Halve it. Halve it again." Say out loud that if a neighbour can see the change, it is too big. |
| The murmur turns into laughter or a chorus of one obvious word. | The silence has built tension and someone releases it; the rest follow. | Let one laugh go by without comment. Before the next hold, say "Whatever word turns up first — nobody is marking it." Keep your own delivery flat. |
| Blank faces, no word comes, people say nothing at the cue. | They are waiting to be sure they have felt something, and the shift is subtle enough that certainty never arrives. | Give an out: "If nothing arrived, say 'nothing'." It keeps them in the exercise and usually the second layer lands. |
| Chatter, questions and comparing between rounds. | The silence rule was mentioned once and not enforced. | Do not answer questions mid-exercise. Raise a hand, wait, restart the hold. Two seconds of enforced quiet resets it faster than a speech. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One loose group facing the wall, plenty of space between people. Run all three layers as written. You may get an extra ten seconds of hold per layer — use it, the murmurs get better. |
| **14–17** | One group, two rough lines so nobody stands directly behind someone else. Keep the holds to thirty seconds each so the murmur cues stay crisp. Same three layers. |
| **18–20** | Three lines, staggered so everyone can see the wall spot. Shorten the neutral set-up to forty seconds and each hold to twenty-five, so you still fit three layers plus the walk. Cue the murmurs with a raised hand rather than your voice — twenty people need a visual. |

## Variations

**Two Layers** — Drop the third change. Run neutral, heels, chin-and-breath, then the four steps with longer holds.  
*Easier. Good for a nervous room or a first Saturday — more time in each state, less to remember.*

**Silent Read** — Run all three layers with no murmur at all. After the four steps, everyone writes or says one word for where they ended up.  
*Harder. Removes the group's shared vocabulary mid-exercise, so nobody can borrow a read from a neighbour.*

**Reverse the Stack** — Start with the full three-change stack held in silence, then peel one change off at a time, murmuring after each removal.  
*Different emphasis — they feel a character drain away rather than assemble, which is useful for dropping a character between scenes.*

## Debrief

1. **Did the word arrive before or after you looked for it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says the word was just there, or that they were surprised by it — evidence the body led. If everyone says they thought about it, name that the shifts were too small to register and move on anyway.

2. **Anyone hear a word from the room that was nothing like yours?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Two people report opposite reads of the same posture. That is the point: the body offers a state, it does not dictate one. Don't resolve it.

3. **What changed on the four steps?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They mention pace, breath, where their eyes went, feeling watched. If they only say "it felt weird", ask which part of them felt weird and then move on.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point. Say so at the start. Standing still for five minutes is harder than it sounds — offer a chair openly before you begin, and run the same three changes seated for anyone who takes it. Eyes stay open on a wall spot, so nobody has to close their eyes in a room of strangers. Breath high in the chest can feel like anxiety; tell them in advance that if it does, drop the breath change and keep the posture. Anyone who wants to stand out and watch may, without explaining.

## Why it works

Emotional fluidity stalls when people try to generate a feeling directly and then wait to see if it worked. This block removes the target. You give a physical instruction with no emotional content — weight into the heels — and the state that follows is a by-product, not a task. Because the changes stack rather than replace, the room feels the mood shift three times in four minutes, which is the fluidity itself: not depth of feeling, but speed of change. The collective murmur means no individual is on the hook for having felt the right thing, so nobody performs a feeling to avoid looking empty. And the four steps prove it holds up in motion, which is where scenes happen.

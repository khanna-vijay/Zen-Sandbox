# 😄 Laughter Blast — Half Speed, Full Volume

![Half Speed, Full Volume](../img/intermediate_w05__b2_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> The whole room celebrates an invisible goal in extreme slow motion, roaring at quarter speed, then does the same lap at double speed.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 05 run-sheet](../week-05.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 5 works character from the outside in. This is the first thing they do after the door shuts, and it makes the point physically before anyone has to think about it: alter the tempo of the body and the feeling follows. It warms voices and faces for the character work later in the session, and it gives the room permission to be loud and ridiculous within four minutes of arriving.

## What students actually learn

- That changing tempo alone changes what they feel, without deciding to feel anything.
- That their face is part of their body and moves with it.
- That being loud in a room of adults is survivable, and mostly funny.

## Setup

Push chairs to the walls. You want a clear loop big enough to walk round — a rough circle or oval, whatever the room gives you. Nothing to carry, nothing to hand out. Stand in the middle where everyone can see you. Take off anything you'd worry about jogging in and say so out loud, so others do the same.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Set it up and demonstrate. "Something wonderful just happened. We're watching the replay." Show them yourself: slow arms up, slack jaw, one long low roar. Then run it on the spot, all together, three times. |
| 1 | Add travel. Everyone moves round the room in slow motion — high knees, sound dragging, eyes up, no contact. Keep them going for the full minute. Call the eyeline if people are watching the floor. |
| 1 | Call "slower". Halve it again. One step takes five seconds. The cheer is almost nothing. Hold it well past the point it stops feeling comfortable — the laugh lives here. |
| 1 | Call "double speed". Same lap, same cheer, now high, fast and squeaky. Two laps at a jog. Then call "freeze" — hold pose and face, five seconds — then one final shared slow-motion roar together. |
| 1 | Let the collapse happen. Ask one question: "Which speed felt like a different person?" Take two or three answers, thirty seconds total. Name the cue and move on without comment. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| During the first quarter-speed cheer | *"Face too. Stretch the face."* |
| As the lap starts | *"Eyes up. Nobody looks at the floor."* |
| Mid-lap, if the sound thins out | *"Lower. Drag the note."* |
| On the second halving | *"Slower. Five seconds a step."* |
| When they get restless in the slowest section | *"Stay in it. Don't rescue yourself."* |
| Switching to double speed | *"High and fast. Jog, don't sprint."* |
| On the freeze | *"Hold the face. Hold the face."* |
| After the final roar | *"Let it go."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Noise, and faces that look nothing like the faces that walked in. People overtaking each other in slow motion and finding it funny. At least one person laughing so hard they break the slow motion, which is fine. The slowest section should feel slightly unbearable and then tip into laughter. Everyone moving, nobody standing at the edge deciding whether to join.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet, polite cheering. Arms up but no sound. | You demonstrated at half commitment, so they matched you. | Stop, do one yourself at full volume, obviously too big, then restart. They copy your level, not your instruction. |
| Slow motion that is just walking slowly — arms neutral, face blank. | They're moving their legs slowly and forgetting the rest. | Call "knees up, jaw open" and freeze them mid-stride for two seconds so they feel where their body actually is. |
| People rushing the slowest section, speeding up on their own. | Discomfort. Slow is exposing. | Count out loud — "one step... two..." — and hold the count. Your voice carries the tempo so they don't have to. |
| Collisions or near-misses in the double-speed laps. | Loop too small, or people cutting across the middle. | Stop, widen the loop, say "one direction, outside edge only". Reduce to one lap if the room is tight. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One loop, whole group together. Run three quarter-speed cheers on the spot as written, and two laps at double speed. There's room, so let them travel widely. |
| **14–17** | One loop, whole group, but set a single direction before the lap starts and say it twice. Cut to two on-the-spot cheers to protect time. Double speed stays at two laps. |
| **18–20** | Split into two concentric loops — inner group travels one way, outer the other — or, if the room is small, run the travelling sections in two halves of thirty seconds each with the other half cheering from the wall. Everyone does the freeze and the final roar together. |

## Variations

**Feet planted** — Cut the lap entirely. Everything happens on the spot — cheer, slow down, speed up, freeze.  
*Easier. Use it in a cramped room, or with a group that includes people who'd rather not move about.*

**Three gears** — After double speed, call quarter, double and freeze in unpredictable order, switching every eight seconds or so.  
*Harder. The gear changes stop being anticipated, so the body has to react rather than perform a planned version.*

**Name the win** — Before you start, take one shout-out for what just happened — "the dog passed its exam" — and everyone celebrates that specific thing.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts from pure physical warm-up towards shared imaginary circumstance, useful if the next block needs a common fiction.*

## Debrief

1. **Which speed felt like a different person?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something concrete about the body rather than the mood — "the slow one felt heavy, like someone much older" or "fast made me a kid". If you get "I don't know, it was just silly", take one more answer and move on. Don't dig.

2. **Did you decide to feel triumphant, or did it just show up?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* "It showed up." Someone noticing they were grinning before they meant to. That's the cue landing. One answer is enough.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point — say this before the first lap and again before double speed. Jogging, not sprinting; call it if anyone accelerates. Anyone who'd rather not travel does the whole thing standing, arms and voice only, and is fully in the exercise — say that as an option, not as a concession. Watch for people in unsuitable shoes. The roar is loud and low; anyone with a tired voice can do the movement and mouth the sound. If your floor is slippery, cut the double-speed laps to marching on the spot.

## Why it works

Emotional fluidity is hard to reach by decision. Told to feel triumphant, most adults perform a picture of triumph and stay outside it. Tempo is a lever they can actually pull: the body has a speed, the speed has an associated state, and changing one drags the other along. Quarter speed produces weight and effort; double speed produces lightness and panic. Neither requires an emotional choice, only an obedient body. The extremity matters — at a mild change the old self persists, at four times or a quarter the familiar version stops being available and something else has to fill the gap. Doing it as a whole room removes the question of whether it's acceptable to look ridiculous, which is the actual barrier.

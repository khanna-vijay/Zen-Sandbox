# 🏟️ Showcase round — Five Tellings

![Five Tellings](../img/intermediate_w05__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> One audience suggestion becomes a plain two-minute story, then the same story is retold four more times in genres the audience picks.

`Players 10–20` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 05 run-sheet](../week-05.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 5 has spent the day building character from posture, tempo and breath rather than from ideas. This is where that gets tested in public. The story is fixed, so the only variable left is the body. It feeds Week 6, where players carry a physical choice through a longer scene without a genre to lean on.

## What students actually learn

- That they can change everything about how a scene feels without changing a single event in it.
- That genre lives in the body first — a noir walk arrives before a noir line does.
- That repetition is not boring when the frame moves; they stop fearing the second pass at material.
- That an audience will follow a story they already know if the players commit to a new physical world.

## Setup

Chairs in a shallow arc facing an open playing area. A whiteboard or flipchart visible to players, upstage-left, for the anchors. A hat with folded suggestion slips collected on the door. A deck of twenty genre cards, one word each: noir, horror, nature documentary, telenovela, silent film, courtroom drama, western, musical, heist, children's television, and so on. A bell, a chime or a hand drum for the Stack. Marker pens. Water within reach.

## How to run it — 30 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 2 | Host welcomes the room, explains the shape in plain terms, draws three slips from the hat, reads them out, and takes the loudest applause as the suggestion. |
| 4 | Three players run the Plain Telling. No style. Who they are to each other, where they are, what one of them wants. Story Keeper writes the three anchors on the board while it runs. |
| 5 | Reread the anchors aloud. Genre Caller draws a card. Fresh cast of three retells the same story in that genre. Reset, draw a second card, second fresh cast, second retelling. |
| 5 | Reread the anchors. Two more genres, two more fresh casts. Anyone who has not yet played goes on now, before anyone repeats. |
| 8 | The Stack. Whole company replays all five tellings in order, sixty seconds each, cut hard by the bell. Land on the Plain Telling's final line. |
| 3 | Bows in one line, tech and front of house included. Host names the three anchors once more and thanks the room. |
| 3 | Short debrief on your feet, before the buzz drops. Two questions, no chairs. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| During the Plain Telling, if players start joking | *"Plain. Just tell us who you are."* |
| At the top of each genre retelling | *"Body first. Move before you speak."* |
| When a cast is talking about the genre instead of playing it | *"Show us the world, don't describe it."* |
| When the story starts drifting from the original events | *"Back to the anchors."* |
| When a retelling is stalling for a joke | *"Same story. Different skin."* |
| Thirty seconds before the end of a retelling | *"Get to the want."* |
| During the Stack | *"Sixty seconds. Go now."* |
| On the last bell of the Stack | *"Land the plain line."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The audience recognises the same beats five times and enjoys them more each time. Players walk on already in the genre — a posture, a tempo, a way of breathing — before the first line. The anchors survive all five tellings intact. The Stack feels fast and slightly out of control but still lands the plain line. Nobody has had to be witty to be watchable.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The Plain Telling is already doing bits, so there is no plain version to vary. | Players hear an audience and reach for laughs. | Stop it at thirty seconds if needed and restart with the note: no jokes, just three facts. Tell them the laughs come later and belong to the genres. |
| Genre retellings become unrelated scenes that share only the title. | Players are inventing rather than retelling, usually because they weren't watching the first version. | Reread the anchors immediately before each cast steps out, and make the incoming cast repeat the three anchors back to you aloud. |
| Genre arrives only as accent and vocabulary — noir means saying 'dame'. | Players are working from the neck up. | Call 'body first' before the entrance and hold the cast still for three seconds while they set a posture. Do not let anyone speak until they have moved. |
| The Stack collapses into shouting and the last line is lost. | Sixty seconds is genuinely tight and adrenaline is high. | Have the bell player give a soft warning tap at forty-five seconds. Tell the final cast before the Stack begins that their only job is the plain closing line. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Three players per telling, four retellings, one cast each — that covers twelve. If you have ten, one person plays twice; ask a volunteer. Run the Stack with everyone on stage together, entering and exiting by telling. |
| **14–17** | Use casts of three for the Plain Telling and casts of four for two of the retellings so everyone plays once. Keep the same timings. Story Keeper and Genre Caller are drawn from those who have already played. |
| **18–20** | Cast four players per retelling and add a fifth as a silent environment player in two of them. Trim each retelling to two minutes and keep the reread of anchors to fifteen seconds. Everyone plays once; nobody plays twice. |

## Variations

**Genre on the card, tone from the room** — Instead of a deck, the audience calls the four genres. Host takes the first clear shout each time.  
*easier — the energy in the room carries the choice, and players get audience buy-in before they start*

**No reread** — Read the anchors once after the Plain Telling and never again. Casts work from memory.  
*harder — forces genuine watching and makes recall part of the performance*

**Same cast, five tellings** — One cast of three plays all five versions back to back with fifteen seconds between.  
*different emphasis — puts the whole load on physical transformation in a single body rather than spreading it across the company*

## Debrief

1. **Which telling changed the most, and what actually changed in it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a physical thing — the tempo, the height of the shoulders, how close people stood — rather than saying 'it was funnier'. If they only say the genre name, ask what the genre made them do.

2. **What did you do with your body before you said your first line?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* A concrete answer: dropped the weight into the heels, took a shallower breath, led with the chin. Vague answers like 'I got into character' mean push once more, then move on.

3. **Did knowing the story make it easier or harder?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Honest either way. The useful version is someone noticing that knowing the plot freed them to spend attention on how it was played. Move on once one person says it out loud.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact is required in any telling. If a genre suggests it — romance, wrestling, horror — say aloud before the cast steps out that contact is optional and mimed contact reads the same from the audience. Anyone can pass on going up; give the Story Keeper, Genre Caller and bell roles to those who would rather not perform, and treat them as full company at the bow. Screen the genre deck beforehand and remove anything that pushes towards violence against a person, medical distress or content that a room of strangers cannot consent to. If a suggestion slip names something loaded, quietly do not draw it. Keep the bell soft; loud sudden sound is unpleasant for some people.

## Why it works

Emotional fluidity is hard to practise when players are also inventing plot, because plot eats attention. Fixing the story with three anchors removes the invention problem entirely. What is left to change is the how — posture, tempo, breath, distance between bodies. Repeating the same events four times makes that variable visible to everyone in the room, including the person doing it, because the contrast is direct rather than remembered. The genre card supplies a physical frame without requiring the player to have an idea first, which is the cue in practice: change the body, and the person arrives.

# 😄 Laughter Blast — Slow-Motion Champions

![Slow-Motion Champions](../img/intermediate_w02__b2_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Everyone runs an imaginary race in slow motion at the same time and celebrates winning it like a nine-year-old.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 02 run-sheet](../week-02.md)

## Why this activity, here

It opens the session. The room has just arrived, coats still on, faces still in the week. This burns off the arrival stiffness and puts a body in play before any scene work. It also plants the week's mechanic physically: the slower you go, the more one small detail has to carry. That is the same move they will make in scenes all morning — establish the normal, then find the single thing that isn't and stay with it.

## What students actually learn

- That committing fully to something silly costs nothing when nobody is watching, and the floor for commitment is set by whoever goes biggest first.
- That slowing an action down does not empty it — it makes each part of it bigger and more specific.
- That their body will produce detail their brain would not have chosen: a wobble, a grimace, a hand that gets there late.

## Setup

Clear floor space, roughly a metre per person. No chairs in the way, no props, no music. Everyone stands. You need a countdown voice loud enough to carry over roaring. Check the floor before the knee slide round — if it is concrete, hard tile or gritty, call the slide as mimed standing-up only and say so before round three starts.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Spread them out facing the walls, backs to the centre. State the rule: nobody watches anybody. Announce that you will be loudest and worst. Start round one — slow-motion running while you count down from ten. On zero: tape broken, both arms up, slow. |
| 1 | Round two. Half the speed of round one. Same countdown from ten, same slow arms-up finish, and now a long low roar that lasts the entire celebration. Roar with them, louder than anyone. |
| 1 | Round three. Quarter speed — barely moving. Countdown, break the tape, arms up, roar, then the knee slide and the shirt over the head. Let the celebration run three or four seconds longer than feels comfortable. |
| 1 | Shake it out at full normal speed. Arms, legs, hands, face — call each body part. Then three sharp claps together on your count. Bring them round to face the centre. |
| 1 | Ask: what got bigger when you went slower? Take two or three answers, ten seconds each. Do not summarise. Move straight into the next block. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As round one starts | *"Faces out. Nobody is watching you."* |
| Mid round one, if legs are moving but faces are blank | *"The face runs too."* |
| Setting up round two | *"Half that speed. Half again."* |
| During the round two roar | *"Let it be ugly."* |
| Round three, if they rush the finish | *"Slower. You have all the time in the world."* |
| At the peak of the round three celebration | *"Hold it. Keep going. Still going."* |
| On the shake-out | *"Full speed now. Shake the face."* |
| Just before the claps | *"Three claps together, on me."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Grunting, a bit of laughter leaking out, and at least half the room genuinely slow rather than pretending to be slow. Faces working, not just limbs. The roar in round two lands as a proper sound, not a polite hum. By the knee slide, several people have lost track of how they look. Nobody is standing still with folded arms.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone jogging at normal speed with a slightly exaggerated arm swing. | They have heard "slow motion" as a style rather than an actual speed. Nobody has committed to being the slowest. | Stop and demonstrate for five seconds at a speed that feels absurd. Say: that is round one. Restart. Call "half again" mid-round. |
| The roar comes out as a thin, embarrassed hum from three people. | You did not go loud enough first. The group is calibrating to your volume. | Roar over them, badly, for the full length of the beat. Do not ask them to try again — just be louder on the next round and it corrects itself. |
| People peeking over their shoulders at each other and laughing at someone. | The facing-out instruction has drifted, and the exercise has turned into an audience. | Call "faces to the walls" without stopping the round. Physically walk the room and turn shoulders if needed. Do not name anyone. |
| Someone stands at the edge doing a small version of everything. | Usually self-consciousness, sometimes a knee or back they have not mentioned. | Leave them alone. Do not single them out or encourage them. Most join by round three. Small participation is participation. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One spread across the whole floor, everyone facing out. Run all three rounds as written. You have room for a real knee slide if the floor allows — keep the round three celebration a beat longer. |
| **14–17** | Same single spread but push people right to the walls before starting; the middle will feel crowded otherwise. Shorten the countdown to eight instead of ten in rounds two and three to protect the timing. |
| **18–20** | Split into two facing-out clusters, one at each end of the room, so nobody is running into anyone. Countdown from eight throughout. Cut the knee slide to a mimed drop with no travel — there is not enough floor. |

## Variations

**No roar** — Run all three rounds with the countdown, the tape and the arms-up finish, but drop the sound entirely. Pure silent slow motion.  
*Easier. Groups that freeze at making noise will still commit physically. Use it with a very new or very quiet room, then add the roar the following week.*

**The photo finish** — On zero in round three, everyone freezes completely at the moment of winning and holds for a count of five while you call out details you can see.  
*Harder. Holding a committed shape with no movement to hide behind is more exposing than moving slowly, and it demands one clear specific choice.*

**Losing in slow motion** — Same three rounds, but on zero everyone loses by a hair — the collapse, the hands on the head, the slow-motion disbelief.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts from celebration to specificity of reaction, and gets at how a single unexpected beat plays out when you cannot rush past it.*

## Debrief

1. **What got bigger when you went slower?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something concrete and physical — "my face", "my hands", "I noticed I was gritting my teeth". They name a detail they did not plan. Two or three of those and move on.

2. **Where did you stop thinking about how you looked?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They point to a specific moment, usually the roar or the shirt over the head. If nobody can name one, that is fine — say so and move on rather than fishing.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point; the facing-out setup keeps people apart. Check the floor before promising a knee slide — on hard or gritty surfaces, call it as a mimed standing drop and say so up front. Say clearly at the start that anyone can run this seated or scale it down, and that a smaller version is fully taking part. Roaring can be uncomfortable for anyone with a voice complaint; offer a silent open mouth as an equal option. Watch for knees and lower backs, and do not encourage anyone to go bigger individually.

## Why it works

This is heightening made physical before anyone has to do it in a scene. Slowing an action down does not remove content — it strips away the speed you normally hide inside, so a single detail has to hold the whole moment. The runner discovers a grimace, a late hand, a shoulder that leads. That is exactly the D3.S2 move: the ordinary action is the base reality, and slowing it exposes the one thing that stands out. The facing-out rule and your own oversized commitment remove the two things that stop people going big — being watched, and being the only one who did.

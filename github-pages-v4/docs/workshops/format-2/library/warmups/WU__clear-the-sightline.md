# Clear the Sightline

![Clear the Sightline — infographic](img/WU__clear-the-sightline.jpg){ .infographic }

> In pairs facing an imaginary back row, one person makes a shape and the other moves to make it read.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

## What it warms

Body and eyeline more than voice. It puts two people in physical relationship without contact and makes each one responsible for the other's readability — which is exactly what a 40-minute public set asks for. Feeds D5.S3 by turning clarity into a group job rather than a personal one.

**Role in the cluster:** connection

## Setup

Clear the floor. Everyone stands in pairs, spread out, all facing the same wall. Point at a spot high on that wall and call it the back row.

## How to run it

1. (30s) Pair up and spread out. Everyone faces the back-row spot. No talking from here until I say. You are all sharing one audience.
2. (45s) A makes one frozen shape with a clear intention. B does not copy. B repositions — angle, height, distance — so A's shape reads from the back row. Hold three seconds. Drop.
3. (75s) Keep going, silently, swapping who shapes each time. Roughly six exchanges. The mover decides fast and commits; the adjuster serves the shape, not themselves.
4. (60s) Now the shaper adds one short sentence, spoken to the back row. The adjuster stays silent and gives focus with their body. Four exchanges, swapping.
5. (60s) Join pairs into fours. One person shapes and speaks; the other three arrange around them so the speaker is the clearest thing in the picture. Rotate the speaker.
6. (30s) Stop where you are. Drop the shapes. One breath in, out. Cue: serve the piece.

## Side-coach

- *"Read it from the back row, not from where you stand."*
- *"Do not copy the shape — frame it."*
- *"Commit to the first shape. It does not need to be good."*
- *"Adjuster: give up your best angle."*
- *"Speaker, your sentence goes to the wall, not to your partner."*

## Build it up

- Shorten the decision window: shaper must be frozen within one second of the drop.
- Take the shaper's eyes away — they close them, and trust the adjuster to place them well.
- Add a second speaker to each group of four, so the adjusters must choose who gets focus and commit to it.

## If it stalls

- Pairs often slide into mirroring. Call it out and give one instruction: if you look like your partner, move.
- If shapes go small and polite, ask for shapes readable from thirty metres away — bigger base, wider arms, clearer line.
- If a pair gets stuck negotiating, tell them shaper moves first, always, and the adjuster reacts. No planning.

## Ask afterwards

1. What did you have to give up to make your partner readable?
2. When you were the shape, could you feel whether you were being framed or competed with?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | Five or six pairs. Run the four-person stage in two groups only; if numbers are odd, make one trio with two adjusters. |
| 14–17 | Seven or eight pairs. In step five you get three or four groups of four — plenty. Cut one exchange from step three to keep time. |
| 18–20 | Nine or ten pairs. Space is tight, so run step five as groups of five rather than adding more clusters. Keep everyone facing the same wall or the sightlines break down. |

## Safety & inclusion

No contact at any point — adjusters move around their partner, never onto them. Keep shapes standing or crouching; nobody needs to go to the floor. Anyone with a knee, back or shoulder issue holds shapes at half size and skips low positions.

## Lineage

In the Studio improv stage-picture and focus-giving work tradition. Built from the standard cheating-out and give-focus drills used before a show. Made into a silent pair mechanic with a fixed back-row target so the adjustment is judged by readability, not by taste.

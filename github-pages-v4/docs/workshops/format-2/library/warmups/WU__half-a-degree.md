# Half a Degree

![Half a Degree — infographic](img/WU__half-a-degree.jpg){ .infographic }

> One partner talks, the other becomes a living warmth meter, and the talker adjusts mid-sentence to keep the needle up.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

## What it warms

Attention and eye contact between two people, plus a small vocal and physical range. It feeds Room Reading by making the audience response continuous and visible, so players practise changing course mid-beat instead of finishing a bit that has already died — exactly what a short-form round under a clock demands.

**Role in the cluster:** connection

## Setup

Two lines facing each other, about a metre apart, so everyone has a partner opposite. No chairs, no props. With an odd number, make one trio: two listeners, one speaker.

## How to run it

1. Brief it (40s). Listeners answer only with body and wordless sound: lean in and hum warm when held, drift back and go flat when not. Honest readings, never cruel, never fake.
2. Round one (60s). Line A talks about anything ordinary — a journey, a meal, a queue. Job is not to be funny. Job is to notice the drift and change something before it lands.
3. Swap (60s). Line B speaks, line A meters. Same rule: adjust volume, detail, subject or pace the moment the needle falls.
4. Shuffle (20s). Line A steps one place to their left; the end person walks to the other end. New partners, new reading habits.
5. Round three (80s). Both partners speak at once, each metering the other with body only while talking. Keep talking, keep reading, keep adjusting.
6. Land it (40s). Stop everyone on a clap. Ask two people what their partner did that pulled them back in. Move straight on.

## Side-coach

- *"Honest meter. Don't be polite."*
- *"Half a degree of drift is enough — change something now."*
- *"Speakers: adjust, don't apologise."*
- *"You are reading, not performing."*
- *"Warm doesn't mean laughing. Warm means held."*

## Build it up

- Listeners may swing sharply and without warning — warm to cold in a heartbeat. Speakers must recover without commenting on it.
- Speakers must finish on a warm beat: they cannot stop until the needle is up.
- Give each speaker two listeners who read independently and often disagree. The speaker plays to both.

## If it stalls

- If every listener stays enthusiastically warm, stop and say the polite version is useless — ask for real drift, and remind them the speaker cannot be wrong, only uncalibrated.
- If speakers narrate at the floor, tell them to hold eye contact and treat the listener as the only audience they will ever get.
- If round three collapses into noise, drop it to one speaker each and add a rule: the listener may swing without warning.

## Ask afterwards

1. What did you actually change when you felt them drift?
2. Was there a moment you chased the warmth instead of earning it — what did that feel like from the other side?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | One corridor of pairs. Run all three rounds as written. |
| 14–17 | One corridor still works but stretch it wide; if the line runs longer than the room, split into two shorter corridors and shuffle within each. Odd number makes one trio. |
| 18–20 | Two separate corridors in opposite corners, running in parallel. Cut the shuffle to a single step and shorten round three to 60 seconds so both corridors finish together. |

## Safety & inclusion

No contact. Speakers choose ordinary, low-stakes content — nothing personal or confessional. Anyone can pass on speaking and stay as a listener for the whole exercise; say so before you start.

## Lineage

In the Attention and status work from the British improvisation tradition, where listeners signal interest or boredom in real time. tradition. Rewritten from scratch as a paired, in-place drill. The usual version judges the speaker as boring; here the meter is information, not a verdict, and the speaker's task is to adjust inside the sentence rather than win approval — which is the week's cue about earning rather than begging.

# Here, Take This

![Here, Take This — infographic](img/WU__here-take-this.jpg){ .infographic }

> In pairs, people mime handing over invisible objects and the receiver names whatever comes first, out loud, before they can improve it.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

## What it warms

Hands, voice and eye contact with one other person; it puts attention on a partner's face rather than the floor. It feeds D1.S1 by making the first noun public before the editing brain gets a vote, and it does it in front of exactly one witness, which is the least frightening audience in the room.

**Role in the cluster:** connection

## Setup

Clear enough floor for pairs to stand facing each other about a metre apart. No props, no chairs. Odd number: make one trio.

## How to run it

1. Pair everyone off, facing each other, arm's length plus a bit. Odd number, make a trio. Tell them nothing gets touched — hands stay in their own space. (30s)
2. Demo with one volunteer. Mime holding something and offer it. They say 'Thanks for the kettle,' take it, then hand you something back. You name it. Keep going. (45s)
3. Round one. Both partners alternate giving and receiving, non-stop. Whatever word arrives first is the object. No swapping the word once it's out. (60s)
4. Stop. Everyone finds a new partner from the other side of the room. Wave a hand if you're unpartnered. (25s)
5. Round two, faster. Receiver names the object before their hands close on it. No repeats of anything from round one. (80s)
6. Stop. Ask for a show of hands: who named something dull? Say the cue — be obvious, be average, your first thought is the gift. Move on. (60s)

## Side-coach

- *"Name it before it lands in your hands."*
- *"Boring objects are perfect. A spoon is a gift."*
- *"Eyes on your partner, not the ceiling."*
- *"Don't shop for a better one. Take the first."*
- *"Say thanks and pass something back straight away."*

## Build it up

- Ban any object that would fit in a pocket. Everything must be big, awkward or alive.
- Add an adjective — the receiver names the object and one quality of it, still without pausing.
- Drop the word 'thanks'. Receiver names the object and immediately hands back, so the exchange becomes one continuous rhythm with no gaps.

## If it stalls

- If people freeze hunting for something clever, call out 'kitchen' or 'shed' and tell them everything comes from there for thirty seconds.
- If pairs go quiet and grin at each other, slow the mime down — a heavy object, a wet object — the body gives the mouth something to name.

## Ask afterwards

1. What happened in your body when you tried to upgrade a word before saying it?
2. Did your partner's dullest object give you more to work with, or less?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | Five or six pairs. Run both rounds as written and add a third 40-second round with the same partner from round two. |
| 14–17 | Seven or eight pairs. Run as written. For the swap, ask one line of the pairs to stay put and the other line to shift two places along. |
| 18–20 | Nine or ten pairs. Cut the demo to 30 seconds and round two to 65 seconds so the swap doesn't eat the clock. Do the swap by having one half of the room step forward one partner. |

## Safety & inclusion

Everything is mimed — no contact at all, hands stay inside each person's own space. Anyone who would rather not face a partner directly can stand shoulder to shoulder and hand objects sideways. Anyone can pass on a turn by handing an object straight back.

## Lineage

In the Endowment and gift-giving drills common in improv teaching tradition. The familiar version treats the object as an endowment exercise about accepting what you're given. We stripped out the acceptance framing and put the pressure on speed of naming, so it feeds first-thought spontaneity instead. The 'name it before it lands' instruction is ours.

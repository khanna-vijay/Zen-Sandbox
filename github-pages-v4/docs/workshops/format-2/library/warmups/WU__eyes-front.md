# Eyes Front

![Eyes Front — infographic](img/WU__eyes-front.jpg){ .infographic }

> A scattered room holds a fixed number of raised hands with heads locked forward, so people track each other by edge of vision only.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

## What it warms

Attention and the group pulse, with the body almost still. It trains the exact habit long-form needs: knowing who is already moving without turning to check, and choosing to support or hold back on that information alone. Expect the first round to collapse — everyone raises at once, or nobody does. That collapse is the useful part; it shows the room how much it has been relying on direct looks.

**Role in the cluster:** focus

## Setup

Scatter the room in a loose block facing one wall, everyone at least an arm's length apart, nobody directly behind anyone else. Mark a spot on that wall at head height. Feet stay planted for the whole exercise.

## How to run it

1. (45s) Set the rule: eyes stay on the mark, heads stay still, feet stay planted. Everything you learn about the room comes from the edges of your vision.
2. (60s) Round one. Say the target number out loud. At any moment, exactly that many hands must be raised. No talking, no signals, no head turns. Raise, hold, lower.
3. (75s) Round two, same number. Add the cap: no hand stays up longer than about five seconds. Traffic increases; call out the count each time it goes wrong, then keep going.
4. (60s) Round three. Raise the target by one and switch the signal to a slow half-crouch instead of a hand. Lower body is harder to see sideways, so the room has to widen its attention.
5. (60s) Call stop. Hands down, eyes still front. Ask one debrief question, take two answers, move straight on.

## Side-coach

- *"Eyes on the mark. Let the room come to you."*
- *"Soften the focus. Stop hunting."*
- *"Too many up — someone drop."*
- *"Don't wait to be safe. Commit and hold."*
- *"Breathe out. You can see more than you think."*

## Build it up

- Ban the reset: if the count goes wrong, nobody drops out of guilt — the room must correct without a scramble.
- Add a second signal type, so hands and crouches both count towards the same target and people must read two shapes at once.
- Run a round in near silence with the lights or your voice dropped, so the only feedback is the count you give at the end.

## If it stalls

- If nobody raises, drop the target to two and say the count out loud every few seconds until the room hears its own rhythm.
- If people keep glancing sideways, have them close their eyes for ten seconds, then reopen on the mark with soft focus.
- If the same three people carry every round, name it plainly and ask them to sit one round out as observers.

## Ask afterwards

1. What told you it was your turn?
2. Where in your vision did you notice the others — and did looking harder help or hurt?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | One block. Target is two raised hands in rounds one and two, three in round three. |
| 14–17 | One block, spread wider. Target is three raised hands, four in round three. |
| 18–20 | Two blocks of nine or ten, side by side with a clear gap, each holding its own target of three, then four. Blocks ignore each other. Run both simultaneously and count each aloud. |

## Safety & inclusion

Fixed gaze can strain necks and make some people light-headed. Tell people to blink normally, use soft focus rather than a hard stare, and look down or sit out at any point without explanation. No contact; keep an arm's length of space so nobody is clipped by a raised arm. The half-crouch is optional — a raised heel does the same job for anyone with knee trouble.

## Lineage

In the Ensemble group-count and shared-attention exercises common in improv and physical theatre training. tradition. Group-count games usually let people scan the room freely. Locking the gaze forward and planting the feet removes the scan, so the only available information is peripheral. Written from scratch for a long-form week about holding the whole stage picture.

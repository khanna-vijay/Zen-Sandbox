# The Scoreline

![The Scoreline — infographic](img/SF__the-scoreline.jpg){ .infographic }

> Two teams play the same timed improv game one after the other while a referee whistles fouls and the audience applauds a winner.

`Players 10–20` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 3/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

## The show

Two teams in team colours play the same improvised game one after the other, to the same suggestion shouted from the seats. A referee times every round, whistles fouls, and then asks the audience to applaud a winner while a scoreboard keeps the tally. Six rounds, one interval, a handshake at the end — nobody has to be clever, they have to commit before the clock runs out.

## The night — 65 minutes

| Min | Segment | What happens |
|---:|---|---|
| 10 | **Doors and seating** | Front of house greets guests, hands out the half-page programme listing team names and the six games, and fills the front rows first. |
| 5 | **Host welcome and referee's rules** | Host gives the three house rules and introduces the referee, who names the four fouls, blows the whistle once so the room knows the sound, and brings both teams on. |
| 18 | **First half — three rounds** | Three games, both teams playing each one to the same audience suggestion, applause scored after each. Roughly six minutes a round including the suggestion and the score. |
| 8 | **Interval** | House lights up, water and drinks in the foyer, scoreboard left visible. Bench swaps happen now if the cohort is large. |
| 18 | **Second half — three rounds** | Three more games, same procedure, teams alternating who plays first. The referee tightens the clock so the half runs faster than the first. |
| 4 | **Final scores and bows** | Last round counts double. Referee reads the total, teams shake hands and bow together, then referee, scorekeeper, tech and front of house are named and bow. |
| 2 | **Foyer** | Players come straight out to meet their guests rather than disappearing backstage. Host thanks the room and points to the exits. |

## Jobs

- Referee — keeps the clock, calls fouls, runs the applause scoring
- Host — welcomes the audience, collects and screens suggestions
- Scorekeeper — flips the numbers, announces the running total
- Tech — house lights, one music sting for entrances, one for the bow
- Front of house — door, programmes, seating, interval drinks
- Team captains — line up their bench, decide who plays each round

## Setup

Two benches of chairs angled towards each other with an empty playing area between them, and a scoreboard (flip pad or whiteboard) where the audience can see it. The referee needs a whistle, a stopwatch and a written round order. Teams wear two colours — scarves or shirts, nothing expensive.

## How to run it

1. Host opens (5 min): three house rules — applaud both teams, shout suggestions only when asked, the referee's word is final. Referee introduces the teams and demonstrates the whistle.
2. Referee names the four fouls, one whistle each, one point to the other team: blocking a partner's offer, waffling instead of choosing, talking over a partner, or laughing at someone's expense.
3. Bank game one, Open Scene: two players, ninety seconds, audience gives a place. Game two, Story One Word: the team lines up and tells a titled story one word each.
4. Game three, Emotion Dial: two players, ninety seconds, referee calls a number one to ten and they play that intensity. Game four, One Prop: players take turns turning a chair into other objects.
5. Game five, Tag Scene: a two-hander runs, teammates tap a player out and start a fresh scene from the last line spoken. Game six, Expert: one player answers interview questions on an audience topic.
6. Each round (4 min): referee names the game, reads its rule aloud, takes one suggestion, then both teams play it in turn to the same suggestion. Alternate who goes first.
7. Scoring (1 min): referee raises a hand over each bench, audience applauds, louder side takes two points and the other takes one. Scorekeeper flips the numbers.
8. Close (4 min): final round scores double. Referee reads the total, both teams bow together, then referee, scorekeeper, tech and front of house are named and bow.

## Side-coach

- *"Take the suggestion. Don't argue with it."*
- *"Referee — whistle it, don't lecture it."*
- *"Bench, watch your own team. Faces up."*
- *"Play to your partner, then turn it out to the back row."*
- *"Clock's running. Choose the boring specific thing."*

## How block 9 builds towards it

1. Weeks 1-2 (25 min): play Open Scene and Story One Word with no score and no referee. Everyone plays twice. Afterwards, name out loud what made a round watchable.
2. Week 3 (35 min): introduce the whistle. Run rounds where players deliberately block, waffle and talk over each other, so the sound becomes familiar and nobody fears it.
3. Week 4 (35 min): work through all six bank games, four minutes each, mixed pairs, no teams yet. Agree and rehearse the contact rules for handshakes, high fives and huddles.
4. Week 5 (35 min): fix two standing teams. Run three scored rounds with the rest of the class as audience. Rotate the referee every round so every student refs once.
5. Week 6 (40 min): run a full half end to end with host, scorekeeper and tech. Then vote — the six games that go in the show, and the order they run.
6. Week 7 (40 min): dress run with lights, sting, scoreboard and four invited guests in the seats. Fix entrances, the interval swap and the bow. Week 8 is the show.

## Looking after the audience

Open the doors ten minutes early and have front of house greet people at the door rather than from behind a table. Programmes list both team names and the six games so nobody is confused about what they are watching. Fill the front rows first and keep the house lights at half so players can see faces. The host says plainly that shouting a suggestion is invited but not required, and that applauding both teams is the point. Interval is eight minutes with water available. At the close, teams bow together, the crew are named, and every player walks out to the foyer to meet their guests instead of vanishing backstage.

!!! warning "What makes it fail"
    The scoreboard eats the improv. Once points are visible, players start pitching lines over their partner's head straight at the audience, volume replaces listening, and the quieter half of the cohort quietly stays on the bench for the whole night. The second failure is a referee who wants to be the funniest person in the room and starts editorialising after every whistle. The third is real: a team can lose in front of their own families and feel it. Guard against all three by keeping the losing margin small — one point for the losing side every round, never zero — by rehearsing the bench swap so everyone plays, and by telling the referee their job is timing and safety, not comedy.

## If it stalls

- If a round dies halfway, the referee whistles, calls a draw, one point each, and moves straight on. Never let a dead scene run its full clock.
- If the audience won't shout, the host asks a closed question: 'Name a room in a house.' First clear word wins.
- If one team keeps losing, give them the next game first and pick the one they rehearsed best.

## Ask afterwards

1. Which round did you enjoy playing, whatever the score said?
2. Where did the scoreboard change how you played, and did that make the scene better or louder?
3. Who on the bench helped you most, and what did they actually do?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | Two teams of four or five. One student refs; one student runs scoreboard and music together. Teams cover the door themselves before the audience is seated, then hand the room to the host. Cut to five rounds and a five-minute interval, bringing the night in around fifty-five minutes. |
| 14–17 | Two teams of six, plus separate referee, host, scorekeeper and tech. Two players from each team sit out rounds one and two and come on for rounds three and four, so nobody plays fewer than two rounds. Six rounds as written. |
| 18–20 | Two teams of seven, plus referee, host, scorekeeper, tech and two front of house. Swap the entire bench at the interval so the second half is played by different bodies. The front of house pair join their team's bench once doors close. Cap each game at three players a side so everyone is actually seen. |

## Safety & inclusion

Handshakes, high fives and huddles are agreed in week four; anyone opting out raises a palm and nods instead, and that is never commented on. No lifting, no grabbing, no contact that isn't in the rehearsed set. The referee's whistle stops anything instantly and is never argued with, by players or audience. Fouls are called on the play, never on the person. The host screens audience suggestions and may reject one out loud without giving a reason.

## Lineage

In the Competitive match-play improv from the Theatresports tradition tradition. Source: *Impro for Storytellers (1999)*. Match improv came out of Keith Johnstone's work turning improvisation into a spectator sport with teams, a clock and penalties. We removed the judging panel, which tends to make judges the entertainment, and put the decision in the audience's hands via the referee. We also rewrote the fouls so they protect players — blocking, waffling, talking over, and getting a laugh at a person's expense — rather than punishing dullness, which nervous first-timers hear as a punishment for existing.

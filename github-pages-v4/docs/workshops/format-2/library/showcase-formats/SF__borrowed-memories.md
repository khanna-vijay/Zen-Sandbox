# Borrowed Memories

![Borrowed Memories — infographic](img/SF__borrowed-memories.jpg){ .infographic }

> The audience writes down small true things that happened to them; the cohort turns two of them into scenes and then shoots one as a short film.

`Players 10–20` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

## The show

On the way in, you write down one small true thing that happened to you and drop it in a bowl. Two of those cards get read aloud, word for word, and the company spins scenes out of them — not re-enactments, but whatever the memory is really about. In the last stretch they pick one thread and shoot it as a short film in front of you, with a director calling wide shots, close-ups and cuts until the credits freeze.

## The night — 60 minutes

| Min | Segment | What happens |
|---:|---|---|
| 12 | **Doors and cards** | Greeters hand out cards and pens, explain the brief, seat people close. Facilitator reads and screens cards in the wings. |
| 4 | **Welcome** | Host explains the format, says nobody is coming onstage, and promises the cards go in the bin at the end. |
| 12 | **Reel One** | First Teller reads a card. Three scenes off it, edited by sweep and tap. Lights hold full throughout. |
| 12 | **Reel Two** | Second Teller, second card, three more scenes. Different cast where numbers allow. |
| 14 | **The feature** | Director names a thread and a genre, then shoots it as a film using wide, close-up, cut-to and flashback calls. Ends on CREDITS. |
| 6 | **Credits and close** | Company bow, Host thanks the two card-writers without identifying them, cards visibly binned, performers head to the door. |

## Jobs

- Host — welcomes, briefs the room, closes the night
- Teller — draws and reads a card from the stool, twice per show
- Director — names the thread and genre, calls the camera shots in the feature
- Tech — two lighting states and one fade, plus the CREDITS blackout
- Front of house — cards and pens at the door, seating, collecting the bowl
- Ensemble — scenes, walk-ons, walls, sound, crowd

## Setup

Chairs in a shallow arc facing the audience, six upstage for players who are waiting, one downstage-left stool for the Teller. Index cards and pens on every audience seat, a bowl by the door to drop them in. Two lighting states if you have them: full and a slow fade.

## How to run it

1. 4 min: everyone writes one small true memory on a card — under sixty words, no names, nothing they would not want read aloud. Shuffle the cards into the bowl.
2. 3 min: a Teller draws a card, reads it aloud exactly as written, then adds two sensory details out loud — a smell, a sound. No jokes, no commentary. Sits.
3. 9 min: run one reel. Three scenes, each launched by a player stepping out on an image or a feeling from the card, not the plot. Edit by sweep or tap.
4. 2 min: the Director names one thread from the reel — a character, a place, a want — and names the film's genre out loud. That is the feature.
5. 4 min: teach the calls. WIDE: everyone builds the location and holds. CLOSE-UP: named player freezes the rest and speaks a private thought. CUT TO: instant new location, same people.
6. 10 min: shoot the feature. Director calls from the side; players obey inside two seconds. Two extra calls: FLASHBACK — play an earlier moment; CREDITS — freeze a final picture, lights fade.
7. 3 min: notes. One thing that came from the card and stayed honest; one moment that turned into re-enactment. No general praise.

## Side-coach

- *"Take the feeling, not the facts."*
- *"Start in the middle of it."*
- *"Director — let that one breathe before the next call."*
- *"Freeze clean for the wide shot."*
- *"Play the person, not the joke about the person."*

## How block 9 builds towards it

1. Weeks 1-2 (30 min): students write memory cards for each other. Read one verbatim, then run a single two-minute scene off it. Coach the difference between the feeling and the facts.
2. Week 3 (35 min): full three-scene reels off one card, edited by sweep and tap. Practise stepping out on an image, never on a plot summary.
3. Week 4 (35 min): drill the five camera calls with no card at all. Everyone directs one three-minute film so the vocabulary is in every body, not just the Director's.
4. Week 5 (35 min): reel plus feature back to back, using cards written by friends outside the class so the material is genuinely unfamiliar.
5. Week 6 (40 min): two straight run-throughs, then vote — running order, who Tells, who Directs, who is front of house. Lock it.
6. Week 7 (40 min): dress run in the performance space with six invited guests writing real cards, full tech cues, foyer and bow rehearsed.

## Looking after the audience

Greeters give out a card and pen at the door with one line of brief: something small and true that happened to you, under sixty words, nothing you would mind hearing read out. Say clearly that writing is optional and anonymous. Seat people in the front rows first — this format dies at distance. The Host repeats at the top that nobody will be pulled onstage and that the cards are destroyed afterwards. At the close, tip the bowl into the bin in full view, then have the company stand at the door so people can say which bit was theirs if they want to.

!!! warning "What makes it fail"
    The cards get re-enacted. Players hear a memory about a broken washing machine and dutifully play a scene about a broken washing machine, and the whole night becomes flat literal transcription with the audience watching their own anecdote get worse. The fix is drilled from week one — take the feeling, change everything else — but under performance nerves the cohort will grab the nearest concrete detail. Watch for it in the first reel and reset early. The second failure is a Director who calls a shot every ten seconds and never lets a scene land.

## If it stalls

- The reel is re-enacting the card. Call a reset and make the next scene a different decade, a different species, a different planet — same feeling.
- Nobody steps out. Give the Teller the job of pointing at two players to open scene one for the next three reels.
- The Director is calling shots every ten seconds. Cap them at four calls for the whole feature until they can feel the gaps.

## Ask afterwards

1. Which scene got furthest from the card and still felt like it belonged to it?
2. Where did we start commenting on the memory instead of living in it?
3. As Director, what did you leave alone, and was that the right call?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | One cast for the whole night. Everyone plays both reels and the feature. One Host who also Tells, one Director, one tech operator who joins the feature crowd. Three scenes per reel. |
| 14–17 | Two casts of seven or eight. Cast A plays Reel One, Cast B plays Reel Two. Both casts merge for the feature but only six are onstage at once — the rest stand at the edges as sound, walls and walk-ons. Directors swap between reels. |
| 18–20 | Three casts of six. Cut each reel to eight minutes with two scenes, add a third reel, and trim the feature to twelve. Two students run front of house and cards through Act One, then join the feature as crowd and sound. Every performer is onstage in at least two segments. |

## Safety & inclusion

Read every card before the show and pull anything about bereavement, illness, abuse or a named third party — say at the top that you do this. Tellers read cards verbatim and never mock the writer. Nobody from the audience is brought onstage. Any lifting, holding or contact between players is agreed in rehearsal, demonstrated at half speed, and either player can say 'change it' at any time with no discussion.

## Lineage

In the Monologue-driven long-form and cinematic 'movie' improv formats tradition. The usual version needs a confident guest monologist inventing true stories on the spot. We moved the storytelling to the audience's written cards, so the most nervous student in the cohort can hold the stool and simply read well. The camera calls in the second half give players a structure to obey when invention runs low, and give one student a directing job rather than a performing one.

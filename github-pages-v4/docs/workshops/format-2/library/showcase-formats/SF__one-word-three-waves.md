# One Word, Three Waves

![One Word, Three Waves — infographic](img/SF__one-word-three-waves.jpg){ .infographic }

> A structured long-form set built from a single audience word: three waves of scenes, two group pieces, one shared ending.

`Players 10–20` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 4/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: none`

## The show

One word from the audience, then forty minutes of improvised theatre made from it. Nine short scenes arrive in three waves, characters return changed, and twice the whole cast floods the stage to build a single moving picture. By the end, things said in the first two minutes come back and land, and nobody — including the cast — knew any of it in advance.

## The night — 60 minutes

| Min | Segment | What happens |
|---:|---|---|
| 10 | **Doors and foyer** | Front of house greets arrivals, seats them, hands out nothing. House music plays. Cast is out of sight and warming up. |
| 4 | **Host welcome** | Host explains in plain terms: nothing is written, one word from you, forty minutes, then we all go home. Names the cast as a group, not individually. |
| 14 | **Set one: opening and wave one** | Word taken. Three-minute association opening, then three scenes, then the first group piece. |
| 18 | **Set one: waves two and three** | Three revisit scenes, second group piece, three collision scenes, closing image referencing the word. |
| 4 | **Bows and host close** | Cast line, single bow, host thanks the audience and the person who gave the word, points to the exit and the bar. |
| 10 | **Foyer** | Cast comes out front. Front of house holds the door open, clears chairs, keeps the room warm for ten minutes so nobody's family leaves without seeing them. |

## Jobs

- Host — takes the word, tops and tails the night, handles any awkward suggestion
- Designated editors (two to four) — responsible for cutting scenes on time
- Tech operator — house lights, one lights-up cue at the top, one lights-down on the closing image, music in and out
- Front of house — door, seating, latecomers, counting heads
- Line captain — keeps the back line tidy, quiet and watching; last one off stage
- Stage-managing understudy — knows the running order cold and can host if the host is ill

## Setup

Clear playing area with a back line of chairs or a standing line where the cast waits in view. Two side entrances kept clear. One microphone or a marked spot downstage for the host to take the audience word.

## How to run it

1. Cast stands in a back line, visible. Host greets the audience, asks for one ordinary word, repeats it twice, and steps off. Cast takes three seconds of silence before moving.
2. Run the opening: three minutes of unscripted association off the word — spoken images, short physical tableaux, overheard lines. No scenes yet. The opening ends when someone steps out and starts a scene.
3. Wave one: three two-handed scenes, unrelated to each other, each drawn from something the opening threw up. Edit each scene by sweeping across the stage or tagging a player out.
4. Group piece one: the whole cast makes a three-minute moving picture of one place, idea or ritual that has already appeared. Anyone may start it; everyone joins within ten seconds.
5. Wave two: revisit the same three scenes later in time. Same characters, changed situation. Then group piece two, run like the first but built from a different thread.
6. Wave three: three scenes that collide the earlier threads — characters from different scenes meet, or one scene's rule invades another. Aim for shorter, faster beats.
7. Close: the cast finds one final image or line that references the opening word. Host calls the cast forward. Hold the line, bow together, exit.

## Side-coach

- *"Edit on the laugh, not after it."*
- *"If you are not in the scene, watch the scene."*
- *"Bring back something we have already heard."*
- *"Walk on and give them a reason to stay."*
- *"Shorter now. Third wave runs hot."*

## How block 9 builds towards it

1. Weeks 1-2: openings only. Take a word, run three-minute associations, stop. Then in pairs, pull one scene out of each opening. No structure beyond that.
2. Weeks 3-4: waves one and two. Three scenes, then the same three scenes later in time. Drill edits separately — sweep and tag-out, twenty reps, nothing else.
3. Week 5: group pieces. Whole cast builds moving pictures off a thread from a wave-one scene. Practise entering within ten seconds and leaving cleanly.
4. Week 6: two full runs of all three waves, unlit, no audience. Then vote on the format and cast the roles at the end of the session.
5. Week 7: dress run in performance conditions — host, tech cues, bows, real word taken from a visiting colleague. Debrief on timing only.
6. Week 8: thirty-minute call. Warm-up, one three-minute opening, silent run of the running order, then the show.

## Looking after the audience

Front of house greets every arrival by the door and seats them from the front row back, so the cast never plays to an empty first row. Host states up front that no one will be brought on stage and no one will be picked on. Take the word from a raised hand, not a shout, and repeat it back so the whole room hears it. If the word is crude or aimed at getting a reaction, thank them, take a second word and use that one. Hold the lights up for the bow so families can see faces. Keep the room open ten minutes afterwards.

!!! warning "What makes it fail"
    The structure gets performed instead of the scenes. Casts under pressure start announcing the shape — hitting the group piece because it is time, revisiting a scene with no reason to revisit it — and the audience watches people executing a diagram. The other common failure is edits arriving thirty seconds late in every wave, so a forty-minute set bloats to fifty-five and the last wave plays to a tired room. Name both in week 3, drill edits harder than anything else, and tell the cast that dropping a group piece is always allowed.

## If it stalls

- If the opening dries up, call a hard stop at ninety seconds and start scene one anyway — the openings exist to feed scenes, not to be admired.
- If nobody edits, appoint two designated editors per wave in rehearsal and keep them for the show.
- If wave three tangles into noise, drop it to two scenes and go straight to the closing image.

## Ask afterwards

1. Which thread did the whole cast hear, and which one did only two of you hear?
2. Where did an edit come too late, and what would you have lost by cutting earlier?
3. What came back at the end, and did it feel earned or bolted on?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | Ten to thirteen: run three scenes per wave with two players each, all three waves. Two designated editors. Everyone plays in at least two scenes and both group pieces. |
| 14–17 | Fourteen to seventeen: run three scenes per wave but split the cast into two standing lines, stage left and right, alternating which line supplies each scene. Four editors, one per side per wave. |
| 18–20 | Eighteen to twenty: cast the show in two teams of nine or ten. Each team runs a full set of roughly twenty-two minutes off its own word, with an interval between. Off-team players run front of house and tech for the other set. |

## Safety & inclusion

Group pieces can crowd the floor. Agree a walking pace, no running, and no lifting. Any contact — hands on shoulders, being led, leaning — is negotiated in rehearsal and rehearsed the same way every time; anyone may decline without explanation and stand in as an object or voice instead. Keep one clear route off stage on each side.

## Lineage

In the Chicago long-form improvisation of the 1970s onward, in the widely taught three-beat structure. tradition. The classic three-beat structure is public teaching property; the wording, timings and rehearsal ladder here are written from scratch. We loosened the traditional group-game slots into simply 'make a moving picture of one place or idea', because adult beginners freeze when asked to invent a game format live. We also fixed the number of scenes per wave at three so nervous performers know exactly how many times they are on.

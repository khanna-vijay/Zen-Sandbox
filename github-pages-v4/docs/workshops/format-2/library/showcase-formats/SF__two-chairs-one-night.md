# Two Chairs, One Night

![Two Chairs, One Night — infographic](img/SF__two-chairs-one-night.jpg){ .infographic }

> Every pair gets two chairs, a named introduction and one honest scene in front of a real audience.

`Players 10–20` · `~35 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: required`

## The show

Eight or nine pairs, two chairs, one scene each. A host introduces both performers by name, the audience calls out a single word, and the pair play a three-minute scene between two people who already know each other. It is short, warm and unpretentious — closer to a series of small portraits than a comedy gig.

## The night — 60 minutes

| Min | Segment | What happens |
|---:|---|---|
| 10 | **Doors and foyer** | Front of house welcomes people, hands out a printed running order with every performer's name on it, seats them from the front row back. |
| 5 | **Host welcome** | Host explains the shape of the night: pairs, two chairs, one word from the audience each time. Sets the rule that shouted suggestions should be one word and printable. Demonstrates once. |
| 16 | **Act one** | Four scenes. Host names both performers, takes a suggestion, scenes run three to four minutes, bell to end, bow. |
| 8 | **Interval** | House lights up, music on, front of house sells drinks or hands out water. Backstage the host confirms act two order. |
| 16 | **Act two** | Four or five scenes, same structure. Host varies the suggestion prompt slightly — a word, then an object in the room, then a colour. |
| 5 | **Company bow and close** | All performers on stage, one bow, host thanks the audience and names the venue and the class. House lights up. |

## Jobs

- Host — introduces pairs by name, takes suggestions, cuts scenes
- Sound and lights — one music cue in, one out, bell or blackout for scene ends
- Front of house — door, running orders, seating, interval drinks
- Stage manager — keeps the running order backstage, sends each pair on
- Photographer or programme maker — one job for anyone not performing that night

## Setup

Two chairs centre stage, angled slightly out towards the audience, with a clear entrance either side. Audience in rows or a shallow arc, no closer than two metres. One host stool or music stand at the side, plus a small table for the suggestion cards.

## How to run it

1. Pair the cohort. Each pair keeps the same partner for all eight weeks. Odd numbers make one trio who play the same slot with a third chair.
2. Run a consent step before any scene: each pair names aloud what contact is fine (handshake, hand on shoulder, none) and agrees either can say 'switch' to drop contact instantly, no explanation.
3. Each pair prepares a relationship and a location, nothing else. No plot, no lines. They write both on a card the host reads out.
4. Take one audience suggestion per scene: a single word or a small everyday object. The pair use it inside the relationship they already own.
5. Scenes run three to four minutes. The host cuts with a light or a bell. Nobody has to end cleverly.
6. Run scenes in a fixed order set in week 7. Host names both performers before every scene, and both bow after.
7. Close with all pairs on stage for a single company bow, then house lights up and the host thanks the room by name.

## Side-coach

- *"Say the relationship out loud in the first thirty seconds."*
- *"Talk to your partner, not to the joke."*
- *"Let the pause sit. The back row can hear silence."*
- *"Take the suggestion literally. Nobody is checking your cleverness."*
- *"Stand where the audience can see both faces."*

## How block 9 builds towards it

1. Week 1 (30 min): assign fixed pairs. Each pair runs three two-minute scenes on relationships they invent on the spot. No audience, all pairs at once.
2. Week 2 (35 min): consent conversation in pairs, then each pair picks two relationships they like and runs each twice. Half the room watches, half plays.
3. Week 3 (35 min): add the two chairs and the entrance. Practise walking on, sitting, starting from the middle of a conversation. Every pair goes once in front of everyone.
4. Week 4 (35 min): add the audience suggestion. Cohort shouts one word per scene. Practise using it small.
5. Week 5 (35 min): host rehearsal. Three volunteers practise naming performers and cutting scenes. Every pair performs once with a full introduction and bow.
6. Week 6 (35 min): each pair performs their best relationship; cohort votes on the running order and on who hosts. Week 7 is a full dress run with tech, timed to the minute.

## Looking after the audience

Greet people at the door and hand them a running order with every performer's name printed on it — that page is half the reason families came. Seat from the front so nobody plays to a gap. In the welcome, tell the room exactly what will happen and what a good suggestion sounds like, so nobody feels tested. At the close, the host names the class and invites the audience to stay ten minutes; performers come off stage and into the room rather than vanishing backstage.

!!! warning "What makes it fail"
    It goes flat when pairs treat the prepared relationship as a script and try to remember a scene instead of having one — the audience watches two people recalling rather than listening. The other failure is a host who apologises. If the host is nervous and hedges every introduction, the room learns to expect little and the warmth drains out by scene three. Rehearse the host as hard as the scenes.

## If it stalls

- If a pair freezes, the host calls the suggestion out again from the side. That restart is legal and audiences read it as part of the night.
- If scenes are running thin, cut prepared relationships from two per pair to one and rehearse that one until it is boring, then perform it.
- If the room is over-polite in rehearsal, put four chairs out and run two scenes at once so nobody is being watched too hard.

## Ask afterwards

1. What did your partner give you that you nearly missed?
2. Which moment felt honest rather than funny, and what did the room do?
3. What do you want your introduction to say about you next week?

## Scale

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| 10–13 | Five or six pairs. Two acts of three scenes, one short interval. Everyone performs once and takes one backstage role. |
| 14–17 | Seven or eight pairs. Two acts of four scenes. Two hosts split the acts; one runs sound while the other hosts. |
| 18–20 | Nine or ten pairs. Three acts of three or four scenes with two intervals, or cut scene length to three minutes flat. Rotate three hosts so hosting is a real job, not a consolation. |

## Safety & inclusion

Contact is negotiated pair by pair before every rehearsal and again on show night; the default is none and any performer can decline without giving a reason. Chairs stay on the floor — nobody stands on them. Anyone who wants to leave a scene walks off; their partner covers with a line and the host cuts.

## Lineage

In the Two-person scene work as taught in most beginner improv classrooms since the 1960s, with the named-introduction convention borrowed from cabaret and variety billing. tradition. We fixed the pairs for eight weeks and let each pair own a relationship in advance, so nervous beginners walk on stage already knowing who they are. The audience suggestion is deliberately small — one word — so it decorates the scene rather than deciding it.

# 🏟️ Showcase round — Two Chairs, One Night

![Two Chairs, One Night](../img/beginner_w06__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Every pair gets two chairs, a named introduction and one honest scene in front of a real audience.

`Players 10–20` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: required`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 06 run-sheet](../week-06.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 6 has spent the session on the seesaw: raising and lowering status by degrees, on purpose, inside a scene. This is where that gets tested in front of watchers rather than in a safe circle. It also runs the showcase format cold, so week 7 sets the order and week 8 performs without anyone meeting the shape for the first time on the night.

## What students actually learn

- They find out that status still reads when a real audience is watching — and that they can adjust it mid-scene without the scene collapsing.
- They stop preparing content. Owning a relationship and a location turns out to be enough to start on.
- They get one clean experience of being named, watched, and applauded for showing up rather than for being funny.
- They learn that a partnership carries the nerves better than an individual does.

## Setup

Two chairs centre, identical, facing slightly out. Clear a playing area roughly three metres by three. Seat the rest of the group as audience in a single arc, close enough that performers can hear breathing. One bell, or a light you can dim, at the host position. Index cards and pens, one card per pair. A visible running order on flipchart or wall. You host tonight; hand hosting to a student in week 7. Have water and a chair at the side for anyone who needs to sit out.

## How to run it — 25 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 3 | Pairs sit knee to knee. Run the consent step out loud — contact allowed, contact not allowed, 'switch' agreed. Then agree relationship and location, write both on a card, hand it in. |
| 2 | Seat the audience. Read the running order. Say the rules once: two chairs, one suggestion, bell ends it, both bow. Say the cue: play the gap, not the person. |
| 6 | Scenes one and two. Host names both performers, reads the card, takes one word or object from the room. Bell at two and a half minutes. Bow. Reset chairs. |
| 6 | Scenes three and four. Same shape. Keep the gap between scenes under thirty seconds — momentum matters more than tidiness. No notes between scenes. |
| 6 | Scenes five and six, or the remaining pairs. If you are running short, drop to two minutes a scene rather than cutting a pair from the bill. |
| 2 | All pairs on stage. One company bow together. House lights up. Thank each person by name. End there — debrief after a break, not on the applause. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Just before the first scene, to the whole room | *"Play the gap, not the person."* |
| As two performers take the chairs | *"You already own the relationship. Start from it."* |
| When a scene opens with both playing equal and flat | *"Someone sit lower. Someone sit taller."* |
| When a scene turns into rapid joke exchange | *"Slow it. Let the status land."* |
| When a performer starts narrating instead of playing | *"Talk to them, not about it."* |
| Around the two-minute mark of a scene that has found something | *"Shift it. Give the gap away."* |
| After the bell, if the pair hesitate | *"Bow. Both of you."* |
| Before the company bow | *"Line up. Look out. Take it."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Scenes start inside a relationship rather than establishing one. Within thirty seconds you can tell who is higher without anyone announcing it, and at some point in most scenes that gap moves — a chair scrapes back, a voice drops, someone stops apologising. Endings are ordinary. The bell arrives mid-thought and nobody scrambles for a punchline. Performers bow without shrinking, and the audience applauds each pair the same amount.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pairs whispering plot in the wings and arriving with a plan. | Nerves. A plan feels safer than a relationship. | Take the card before they can add to it. Say out loud: relationship and location only, and I will read the card, so anything extra stays unsaid. |
| Every scene runs at the same flat, polite energy with no status difference at all. | Both performers are protecting each other and neither will take the higher position in public. | Between scenes, remind the room that low status is the more generous choice. Then side-coach the next pair: someone sit lower. |
| Scenes overrun and the block eats the debrief. | You are waiting for a good ending before ringing the bell. | Ring on time regardless. Say once, before scene one, that the bell is not a judgement. Then be ruthless with it. |
| Contact happens that was not on the agreed list. | Momentum in the scene overrode the consent step, usually with the more confident performer. | Stop the scene yourself. Restate the pair's list. Restart from the chairs. Do not treat it as a discipline moment — treat it as a reset. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Five to six pairs. Every pair performs. Scenes at two and a half minutes, bell strict. Keep the six-minute blocks as written; the sixth pair fits in the third block. |
| **14–17** | Seven to eight pairs. Cut scenes to two minutes and shave the setup block to two minutes by writing cards during the previous break. Two pairs per six-minute block, remaining pairs go in the final block back to back. |
| **18–20** | Nine to ten pairs. Split the bill: five pairs perform tonight, the rest go first next week and the order is posted before the group leaves. Non-performing pairs are the audience and hold the bell. Say this at the start so nobody feels cut. |

## Variations

**Chairs only** — Drop the audience suggestion. Pairs get relationship and location and ninety seconds. Host reads the card and starts them.  
*Easier — removes the one unknown element and halves the exposure time for a nervous cohort.*

**One turn** — Tell each pair the gap must move at least once. The one who starts higher must finish lower, or the reverse. Host notes whether it happened.  
*Harder — forces deliberate modulation rather than settling into a comfortable fixed position.*

**Thirty seconds silent** — The first thirty seconds of each scene are wordless. Performers establish who is higher using the chairs, distance and eye contact only. Then speech opens.  
*Different emphasis — moves status from dialogue into body, which is where an audience reads it first.*

## Debrief

1. **Watching, not performing — when could you first tell who was higher?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They point at something physical and specific: he stood when she sat, she kept checking his face, nobody looked at the door. Not 'he was more confident'.

2. **In your own scene, did the gap move? Who moved it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Honest either way. 'It moved when I stopped defending myself' is good. So is 'it never moved and I could feel us both holding it.' Move on once someone names a moment rather than a feeling.

3. **What was different about doing this with people watching?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Concrete reports — talked faster, forgot the location, gripped the chair. Not reassurance. If the room only says 'it was fine', ask one person what their hands were doing.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    Contact is opt-in, named aloud, and agreed by both before the scene. 'Switch' drops contact immediately with no explanation and no follow-up question — say this yourself at the top so it does not need courage to use. Anyone who does not want to perform tonight can host, ring the bell or run the lights and still counts as present; offer this privately, not in front of the room. Chairs stay available for the whole scene — nobody has to stand to play high status. Keep the playing area clear of bags and cables. If a scene touches something raw, ring the bell early and move to the next pair without comment.

## Why it works

Status modulation only becomes a skill when there is a witness, because the gap between two people is something an audience reads before the performers feel it. In the safety of an exercise circle, players can fake a status choice with a line and nobody checks. Under lights, with a bell and a bow, the body does the reporting: the audience sees who leans, who waits, who fills the silence. Fixing the pair for eight weeks removes the negotiation of who to work with, so the only variable left is the gap between them. Giving them relationship and location but no plot removes content as a hiding place. What remains to play is the seesaw — which is the point of the cue.

# 🏟️ Showcase round — The Request Board

![The Request Board](../img/beginner_w07__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Eight short games chalked on a board, chosen by the audience, run to a bell, with a rota so nobody has to volunteer.

`Players 10–20` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact incidental` · `Props: required`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 07 run-sheet](../week-07.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 7 has spent the session putting emotion in the body and the breath before the line. This is where that gets tested in front of watching eyes. The board format means the group does not choose the pressure — the audience does — so nobody can retreat to the game they already feel safe in. It feeds Week 8, where emotional shifts have to survive a longer scene without a host calling them.

## What students actually learn

- That an emotion arrives faster through the breath than through a sentence, and that the audience reads it either way.
- That being watched is survivable, because the bell always comes and the applause is unconditional.
- That a called change — a number, a genre, a freeze — can be obeyed without explaining it, and the scene still holds.
- That fumbling in front of people costs nothing when the room has agreed in advance to applaud it.

## Setup

A board or flip chart the audience can read, chalk or marker, and the eight game names written up before anyone arrives. A bell, a chair for the host (you), and a printed rota sheet with names against each round so no one has to volunteer. Chairs in a shallow arc facing the playing space. Leave a clear upstage line where the waiting cast stands. One household object for Prop Parade if you use it — a mug, a scarf, a length of pipe.

## How to run it — 25 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 3 | Show the board. Read the eight names aloud with one line of rules each. Point at the rota and say nobody volunteers, everyone plays, the bell ends it. Seat the audience half. |
| 4 | Round one. Take an audience pick, one suggestion, call the rota names. Bell at three. Applause, players sit, next names up. Keep your patter short. |
| 4 | Round two. Same shape. If Emotional Dial has not come up yet, offer it as the choice — the session skill needs one airing. |
| 4 | Round three. Same shape. Push the side-coaching harder now: call breath before words, call the number, call the change. |
| 4 | Round four. Same shape. Choose a game with the most bodies on stage so the back of the rota gets time in front of the audience. |
| 3 | Swap audience and cast halves for one fast all-play round — Prop Parade or Photo Album. Whole cast on stage, bow, thanks. |
| 3 | Sit everyone down. Run the debrief questions. Finish on the cue line and let them go. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As a pair begins any scene | *"Breath first."* |
| A player narrates their feeling instead of showing it | *"Body, not commentary."* |
| Calling Emotional Dial | *"Grief. Four."* |
| The dial has settled and gone flat | *"Same chore. Take it to nine."* |
| A freeze is called and the replacing player hesitates | *"Exact shape. Then speak."* |
| A Baton Story teller dries up | *"Bow. Pass it on."* |
| Double Talk translation is lagging behind the gibberish | *"Translate the feeling, not the syllables."* |
| Thirty seconds before the bell in the last round | *"Land it. Bell coming."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Rounds start within ten seconds of the pick. Players walk on without being coaxed because their name is on a sheet. When you call an emotion and a number, the change lands in the shoulders and the breath before the next line — you can see it from the back. The bell cuts things that were still going well, and nobody argues. The watching half is loud and generous, and laughs at fumbles rather than through them. The host talks less than the players.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The audience half stares at the board and takes forty seconds to choose. | Eight options and no time pressure. | Point at three games and say pick one of these. Cover the rest of the board with your hand if you have to. |
| Emotional Dial players announce 'I'm so angry' and carry on scrubbing the same way. | Emotion is being played as vocabulary, not as a physical state. | Freeze them. Say breathe the anger for five seconds, then move the brush. Restart from the breath. |
| The same three confident players end up in every round. | The rota was written loosely or ignored under pressure. | Read the names off the sheet every time, out loud, before you take the suggestion. Never take a volunteer. |
| Freeze and Swap turns into two people jostling for the same position. | No agreement about touching or space. | Stop the round. Restate: call freeze from where you stand, wait for the player to step out, then take the shape. No hands. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Split five or six as audience, the rest as cast, and swap at the all-play round. Four rounds of three minutes gets everyone on twice. Write the rota so each person's second slot is a different game. |
| **14–17** | Split seven or eight each way. Four rounds of three minutes gets everyone on once. Choose at least two multi-player games — Photo Album, Baton Story, Prop Parade — so the rota clears faster. |
| **18–20** | Split ten each way. Drop the bell to two and a half minutes and run five rounds instead of four, taking the extra ninety seconds from your opening patter. Favour Prop Parade and Photo Album, which put six or more on at once. |

## Variations

**Two games only** — Chalk just Emotional Dial and Photo Album. Run the whole block alternating them, three minutes each.  
*Easier — removes the choice overhead and lets nervous groups get familiar with one shape rather than eight.*

**Audience calls the dial** — In Emotional Dial, hand the emotion and the number to the watching half instead of calling them yourself.  
*Harder — the calls get stranger and faster, and players lose the safety of a facilitator moderating the pressure.*

**Silent board** — Run every round without the host taking a spoken suggestion. The audience writes the suggestion on a slip and you hold it up.  
*Different emphasis — shifts the work off host patter and onto how quickly players read and physicalise a cold prompt.*

## Debrief

1. **When the emotion changed on you mid-scene, what moved first?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something bodily and specific — breath, jaw, shoulders, the speed of their hands — rather than 'I thought about what an angry person would say'.

2. **What did being on the rota do that volunteering wouldn't have?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They name the relief of not having to decide, or admit they'd have sat out every round given the choice, and got on anyway.

3. **What happened in the room when someone fumbled?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They noticed the applause landed the same either way, and that the fumble cost them less than the fear of it predicted.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    Freeze and Swap involves stepping close to another player and copying their position. No touching is needed and none is permitted — call freeze from where you stand and wait for the player to step clear. Say this out loud before that game runs, every time. Anyone who does not want to be replaced mid-scene says so at the top; take them off that round's rota without comment. The rota removes the pressure to volunteer, but keep an unmarked pass: anyone may point to the sheet and say pass, and you move to the next name without asking why. Watching is a real role here, not sitting out. Check that the playing space is clear of bags and chair legs before the first freeze.

## Why it works

Emotional fluidity is blocked by two things: needing a reason to feel something, and needing to be ready before being seen. The board format removes both. The host calls an emotion and a number, which is an instruction rather than a justification, so the player has nothing to build and can only do. The rota puts them in front of an audience before they have decided they are ready, which is the only condition under which most beginners find out they can be. The three-minute bell caps the exposure, so the cost of a bad round is fixed and known in advance. What remains is the mechanism the cue points at — the body registers a change faster than language does, and when the change enters through the breath the audience believes it before a word is spoken.

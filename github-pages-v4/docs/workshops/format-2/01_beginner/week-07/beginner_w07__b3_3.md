# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Breath Tell

![Breath Tell](../img/beginner_w07__b3_3.jpg){ .infographic }

> A silent circle switches emotion in the breath alone while one person in the middle tries to read it.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 07 run-sheet](../week-07.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 7 puts emotion in the voice. Before anyone speaks an emotion, they locate it lower down. This warm-up strips out face, gesture and sound so only the breath carries the state, and proves the breath carries enough. Everything later in the session — emotional scene work, voice that shifts with feeling — leans on that. It also seeds the session cue directly: breath first, words after.

## What students actually learn

- That an emotion is legible in breath alone, before any face or voice gets involved.
- That they can drop one state and pick up another in ten seconds without winding up to it.
- That the pull to indicate with the face is a habit, not a necessity.

## Setup

Open floor, circle of chairs pushed back. No props. Have four to six emotion words picked before you start so you are not choosing on the spot: dread, relief, boredom, quiet fury, delight, suspicion. Standing works better than sitting — the ribs move more freely.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Give the rule: only the breath changes, no sound, no gesture, no face. Demonstrate one breath yourself for three seconds. Pick your first reader and place them centre, eyes closed, hands over ears, humming. |
| 1 | Round one. Say the emotion to the circle. Ten seconds of breathing it. Click. Reader opens, turns, names it. Swap in the next reader while the circle resets. |
| 1 | Round two. New emotion, further from the first — if you opened with dread, go to delight. Same ten seconds, same click, same read. |
| 1 | Round three. Choose a low-energy word this time: boredom or relief. Coach the circle to keep it in the ribs, not the shoulders. |
| 1 | Round four. Last reader, last emotion. After the guess, everyone drops it and takes three plain breaths together. Hold the silence for a few seconds, then release. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As the reader covers their ears and you name the emotion | *"Breath only. Nothing above the neck."* |
| Three seconds into the ten | *"Lower. Put it in the ribs."* |
| When faces start doing the work | *"Faces off. Let the breath carry it."* |
| When the circle goes stiff and holds still | *"Keep breathing. Don't freeze — change the breath."* |
| Just before the click | *"Hold it. Don't perform it."* |
| As the reader turns and scans | *"First instinct. Say what you see."* |
| Between rounds, resetting the circle | *"Drop it. Neutral. New one coming."* |
| On the closing three breaths | *"Ordinary breaths. Nothing added."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The circle is genuinely quiet and the room's breathing has visibly changed shape — you can see shorter, higher breaths on dread and slow low ones on relief. Readers guess in the right family more often than not: they say "something nervous" for suspicion, "heavy" for boredom. Nobody is mugging. Switching between emotions takes seconds, not a run-up.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone is pulling faces and hunching shoulders; the reader guesses easily. | Default habit — students indicate emotion with the face because that is what they think acting is. | Stop the round. Say "faces off" and run the same emotion again with hands loosely at their sides. Point out that the read still works. |
| Giggling breaks out and the silence collapses. | Standing still in silence being looked at is exposing; laughter releases it. | Name it once, lightly: the silence is the exercise. Shorten the hold from ten seconds to six for the next round and keep the pace brisk. |
| The reader guesses wrong every time and looks deflated. | The reader thinks it is a test they can fail. | Say aloud that wrong guesses are fine and the circle is the one working. Take the guess, thank them, move straight on without discussion. |
| Breathing gets so exaggerated it becomes audible panting. | Students equate "only breath" with "bigger breath". | Call "quieter, not bigger". Ask for the smallest change that still feels like the emotion. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle, four rounds, four readers as written. Everyone can see everyone, so the read is easy — use subtler emotions like suspicion and boredom to keep it honest. |
| **14–17** | One circle, still four rounds. Tighten the ring so the reader can see the far side without walking. Cut the hold to eight seconds so all four rounds fit. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten, each with its own reader, running the same emotion at the same time on your call. You handle the calls and clicks for both. Three rounds only, six readers total. |

## Variations

**Named list** — Write the four emotions on the wall before you start. The reader knows the options and picks from them.  
*Easier — removes the blank-page problem for the reader and lets the circle focus purely on the breathing.*

**Split circle** — Give half the circle one emotion and half the opposite, without telling the reader. They must name both and point to the dividing line.  
*Harder — the circle has to hold their state next to a contradicting one, and the reader has to discriminate rather than sample.*

**Breath then line** — After the reader guesses, the person opposite them speaks one neutral sentence — "the bus was late" — keeping the breath they built.  
*Different emphasis — carries the breath into voice, which is where the session is heading.*

## Debrief

1. **What actually changed in your body when I called the word?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something located and physical — "my breath got shallow and high", "I stopped breathing out fully" — rather than "I thought about being scared".

2. **Readers: what were you reading?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They name breath and posture, not face — the speed, where the chest moved, whether the circle looked held or loose. If they say "their expression" the rule slipped; note it and move on.

3. **How long did it take to swap from one emotion to the next?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Surprise at how quick it was. "Almost instant once I stopped trying to feel it first." That is the fluidity point — it goes straight into the next block.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point. Anyone uncomfortable covering their ears or closing their eyes can face outward from the circle instead — same effect, no sensory pressure. Anyone who does not want a turn in the centre passes with a headshake; do not push. Standing is not required — a chair in the circle works fine. Emotion words here are ordinary, not traumatic; avoid grief, shame or terror in a Week 7 warm-up. Breath-holding is not part of this: if anyone feels light-headed, they breathe normally and sit out a round.

## Why it works

Emotional Fluidity depends on being able to enter and leave a state quickly, and that is hard while the face and voice are doing the work — those channels are performative, self-monitored and slow. Removing them leaves the breath, which shifts faster and is harder to fake. The reader supplies the evidence: when a stranger correctly names your state from breathing alone, you stop believing you need to show emotion and start trusting that you need only change it. The ten-second hold and immediate reset train the switch itself, so by the time voice comes back in later blocks, the breath is already leading it — which is exactly what the cue asks for.

# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Three Quick Truths

![Three Quick Truths](../img/beginner_w01__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Three thirty-second pairings with three different strangers, then one lap of the circle so everyone hears every name.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 01 run-sheet](../week-01.md)

## Why this activity, here

First activity of the first session. It comes before any character, scene or physical work and feeds everything after it. Names get into the room, voices get used once each, and the pass rule gets its first live test — so when the harder blocks arrive later this morning, "I'll give you a different one" is already an established, unremarkable move rather than a confession of failure.

## What students actually learn

- They hear their own voice in the room within the first five minutes, before any pressure to be interesting.
- They discover that declining a prompt costs nothing — because they watch someone else do it and the room keeps moving.
- They arrive with a handful of real names attached to real faces, not a wall of strangers.
- They learn that true and ordinary is enough material to speak with.

## Setup

Standing circle, chairs pushed back, enough clear floor for people to cross to a new partner in three seconds. No props, no paper. Know your own answer to each prompt in case you need to model one fast. Have a watch or phone timer visible to you but not announced — this block runs on your calls, not on a countdown the room can see.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Explain the shape: three quick pair chats, then one lap. Everything true. State the pass rule and give an example of using it. Keep this under a minute — do not elaborate. |
| 1 | Call "find someone you have not spoken to today". Both partners say their name and what they ate this morning. Odd number, make one three. Call time firmly at forty seconds. |
| 1 | Call "new partner". Both say their name again and describe the last photo on their phone out loud. No screens shown. Call time and keep them moving. |
| 1 | Call "last partner". Both say their name and one small reliable irritation. Side-coach "smaller" if anyone goes big. Call time and bring the circle back in. |
| 1 | One lap clockwise: name, one true word for how you arrived. Three seconds each. Then say what you noticed, ask one debrief question, take two answers, move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As you finish explaining, before the first call | *"True is enough. It does not have to be interesting."* |
| Immediately after each "find a partner" call, while people are still hesitating | *"Nearest stranger. Do not audition partners."* |
| During the first pairing, if only one person is talking | *"Both of you. Name first."* |
| During the irritation round, when someone starts a real grievance | *"Smaller. Mugs, not managers."* |
| As the circle reforms | *"One word. Three seconds. No sentence."* |
| If someone starts explaining their word | *"Just the word. Next."* |
| If anyone visibly stalls in the lap | *"Pass is a full answer. Keep going."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is loud during the pairings and cuts off cleanly when you call time. People move to new partners without scanning for a friend. At least one person uses the pass rule and nobody reacts to it. The final lap moves fast — names land, single words land, and nobody explains themselves. You come out of it knowing more names than you did, and so do they.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| People pair with the person they arrived with, all three rounds. | The call was too soft and nobody enforced "never spoken to". | Between rounds, physically break clusters. Point: "You, cross the circle. You, come here." Assign two or three pairings yourself and let the rest follow. |
| The pairings overrun and the circle lap gets squeezed to nothing. | You waited for conversations to finish rather than cutting them. | Cut mid-sentence. Say "hold that, new partner" out loud. The lap is the part that gets names into the whole room — protect it over the chats. |
| The one-word lap turns into sentences and stories. | The first two or three people set a length and everyone copies it. | Model it yourself first with a single flat word. Interrupt the second person who over-explains with "just the word" — after that it self-corrects. |
| Somebody offers something heavy in the irritation round and the room goes quiet. | "Annoys you" was heard as an invitation to disclose. | Acknowledge briefly, do not probe, redirect: "Small stuff for now — we're saving the big stuff for never." Move to the next call within five seconds. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Runs as written. One circle. Three pairings at forty seconds each. The lap takes roughly forty seconds, so you have slack — use it to let the last two people take a full breath rather than adding content. |
| **14–17** | One circle still. Cut pairings to thirty-five seconds each so the lap has room. Say "three seconds, and I will move you on" before the lap starts, and do move them on. |
| **18–20** | Drop the third pairing entirely — run two chats, not three. The lap is the priority at this size and needs a clear seventy seconds. Split the lap into two half-circles going simultaneously only if you are prepared to lose the whole-room hearing of every name; otherwise keep it single and brisk. |

## Variations

**Same prompt, three times** — Use the breakfast prompt for all three pairings instead of changing it. Only the partner changes.  
*Easier — removes the load of processing a new question while meeting a new face. Good for a very nervous or very tired group.*

**Recall lap** — For the closing lap, each person says their own name and then the name of one person they spoke with.  
*Harder — adds retrieval pressure and makes the listening in the pairings matter. Only use it if the group arrived warm.*

**Object instead of fact** — Replace the phone-photo prompt with: describe one object currently in your pocket or bag, without taking it out.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from disclosure to plain description, which lowers the personal stakes and starts the specificity muscle for later scene work.*

## Debrief

1. **Did anyone use the pass? What happened when they did?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says they passed and it was fine, or that nothing happened at all. Somebody else says they did not notice. Move on quickly — the point is that it was unremarkable, not that it was brave.

2. **Which was easier to say — the fact, or the one word at the end?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Split answers, with reasons attached to exposure rather than skill: "the fact, because it wasn't about me" or "the word, because it was over faster". You are listening for people noticing their own stakes, not for a consensus.

3. **How many names have you got right now?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Honest low numbers — two, four, none. Nobody claiming all twenty. Say the cue: you can always say cut, nothing happens without a yes — and go straight into the next block.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No physical contact anywhere in this activity — pairs stand and talk, nobody touches, nobody shows a phone screen. If someone prefers not to move, let them stay put and bring partners to them; announce this once at the start so it does not read as a special case. The pass rule covers every prompt including their own name-and-word in the lap. Anyone who arrives late joins the circle at the next call without explanation. If someone cannot stand for five minutes, run the whole thing with chairs and have the mobile people rotate.

## Why it works

Boundary navigation only becomes real when a boundary is exercised in public and the room does not react. This activity manufactures three cheap opportunities to decline within four minutes, at a moment when the stakes are near zero — nobody is committed to a scene, nobody is being watched by more than one person. The pass therefore gets tested before it is needed. The prompts also descend in exposure order: what you ate is nearly impersonal, the photo is a chosen edit, the irritation is a small confession, the single word is unguarded. By the time the harder ask arrives, the person has already answered three times and has evidence that speaking cost them nothing. The closing lap serves a second function: it puts every voice into the whole-room space once, so the first time anyone speaks to twenty people is with a name they cannot get wrong.

# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Small Hill

![Small Hill](../img/beginner_w02__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Everyone names one trivial thing they'd genuinely argue about, in five words or fewer, and the room votes with thumbs.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 02 run-sheet](../week-02.md)

## Why this activity, here

First activity of week two, straight after the welcome. Week one got them speaking in the room; this gets them stating a position without checking whether it's clever first. It feeds every scene-start exercise later in the session, where the offer has to arrive before the edit does. It also gives you a live read on which parts of the circle are still hedging.

## What students actually learn

- That a plain, unremarkable opinion said out loud gets a reaction — they don't have to manufacture one.
- That the room responds to commitment, not quality; the thumbs come regardless.
- That five words is enough, and hunting for the better phrasing costs more than it buys.

## Setup

Chairs pushed back. Standing circle, shoulder to shoulder, everyone able to see everyone. No props, no paper. Know your own two examples before you start — pick genuinely small ones. Have a watch or phone timer visible to you.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Frame it standing. Everyone has a small hill — a trivial thing they'd actually argue about. Give yours and two more. State the ban: no politics, faith or bodies. Then send them into pairs. |
| 1 | Pairs, both talking at the same time. Each tells the other their small hill and why. Don't let them take turns. Call time hard and pull them back into one circle. |
| 2 | Twenty seconds to boil it to five words. Then round the circle, one at a time, five words each. Room votes instantly with thumbs up or down after every one. Keep it moving. |
| 1 | Name the two hills that split the room hardest. Ten seconds each for one extra sentence, no reply. Then one sentence from you on the room's flavour today. Move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Framing, if the examples are drifting big | *"Smaller. Something nobody would write down."* |
| During the pairs, when one person goes quiet | *"Both of you, at once. Don't wait."* |
| During the five-word edit | *"Take the first version. Say it as it is."* |
| Round the circle, when someone stalls | *"Five words. Now."* |
| When thumbs are slow or half-raised | *"Commit the thumb. Up or down."* |
| When someone starts explaining instead of stating | *"Just the hill. Defence comes later."* |
| During the two ten-second defences | *"One sentence. Land it."* |
| Closing | *"That's the room today. Straight on."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The circle moves at roughly one hill every five seconds. Thumbs go up and down fast and unevenly. There's laughter at the small stuff — toast, the dishwasher, which end of the sofa. Nobody is workshopping their phrasing. When you name the two split hills, the room already knows which two they were.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| People giving long, careful explanations instead of five words. | They're treating it as a chance to be interesting rather than a chance to be plain. | Cut in mid-sentence with "Five words." Do it to the first offender and the rest self-correct. Don't apologise for the interruption. |
| Hills that aren't trivial — a real grievance about work, a health thing, something political in disguise. | The framing examples weren't small enough, or someone genuinely wants to talk about it. | Say "Too big — give me a food one" and move on within two seconds. Don't discuss it in front of the room. |
| The whole room thumbs up on everything. | They're being polite and reading each other rather than voting honestly. | Call "Honest thumbs — disagreeing is the point." Deliberately thumb down the next one yourself. |
| Two or three people fall silent when their turn comes round. | Nothing arrived in the pair stage, usually because they listened rather than talked. | Say "Pass, come back at the end." Give them the last two slots. Almost always something arrives by then. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Everyone speaks. You'll have spare seconds in the circle round — use them on three split hills instead of two. |
| **14–17** | One circle. Everyone speaks. Enforce five words strictly or you'll overrun; count on your fingers if you have to. Two split hills only. |
| **18–20** | One circle, but drop the pairs to 45 seconds and start the round-the-circle immediately. Cap defences at one person, not two. If you're still over, take the last four hills as a quick-fire chain with no vote between them. |

## Variations

**Show of hands first** — Before anyone speaks, ask for hands from anyone who has a hill about food. Then transport. Then household objects. Let them pick a category before they pick a hill.  
*Easier — gives hesitant people a shelf to take something off instead of a blank page.*

**Three words** — Cut the limit from five words to three. Run the circle twice: everyone gives one hill, then round again with a second, different one.  
*Harder — kills any chance of composing the line, and the second lap forces them past their pre-prepared answer.*

**Defend the opposite** — Run it the same, but after the vote each person gets five seconds to argue the side they don't hold. No preparation.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from committing to your own first thought to accepting a position handed to you.*

## Debrief

1. **Whose hill got the strongest reaction — and was it the cleverest one?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a completely mundane hill, and the room agrees it landed hardest. Ideally the person who said it admits they nearly said something else instead.

2. **What happened in the gap between thinking of your hill and saying it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* "I changed it." Or "I was worried mine was boring." Naming the edit out loud is the whole point. Move on as soon as two people have said it.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact in this activity. Shoulder to shoulder means near, not touching — say so, and let anyone stand a step back. The topic ban on politics, faith and bodies is not optional; state it before the pairs and enforce it instantly rather than negotiating. Anyone can pass and come back at the end; make that explicit before the circle round starts. If someone is hard of hearing, the simultaneous-talking pair stage is difficult — let them do it as a normal back-and-forth. Thumbs are a visible vote; anyone who prefers not to vote can keep hands down without comment.

## Why it works

Unfiltered spontaneity fails because the filter is fast — most people replace their first thought before it reaches their mouth. This activity attacks the filter on three fronts at once. The five-word cap removes the time to compose. The trivial-topics rule removes the stakes that make composing feel necessary. And the instant thumb vote proves, within a second, that the plain answer gets a response anyway. Nobody is asked to be funny; the laughter comes from recognition, which is exactly the mechanism the cue describes. Be obvious, be average, and the room still meets you.

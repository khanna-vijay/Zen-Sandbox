# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Lighthouse Sweep

![Lighthouse Sweep](../img/beginner_w08__b3_3.jpg){ .infographic }

> Standing still in a circle, each person sweeps eye contact across three faces, says their own name to the back wall, and holds one beat of silence before passing on.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

It is the first thing in the room on showcase week. Before anyone plays a scene in front of guests, they practise being looked at while doing nothing clever. It feeds every two-hander later — the ability to stand still, be heard, and let a pause exist without filling it.

## What students actually learn

- That being watched while still is survivable, and takes about eight seconds.
- That their own voice reaches the back of the room when they aim it there.
- That a beat of silence after speaking reads as confidence, not as a mistake.

## Setup

Clear the floor. One circle, everyone standing, arm's length gaps. No chairs in the ring. Know where your 'back wall' is and point at it before you start — the far wall, not the nearest one. No materials needed.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Explain the turn and demonstrate it once: three faces, two seconds each, own name to the back wall, one silent beat. Point out the back wall. |
| 1 | Set posture together — feet flat, knees soft, hands still, three slow breaths. State the rules: no fidgeting, no shuffling, no laughing it off. Let the stillness sit a moment. |
| 2 | Go round the circle in order, roughly eight seconds each. Say 'next' if anyone freezes. No comments, no applause between turns. Keep the pace steady. |
| 1 | Unison beat: all still, eyes up, say 'here' together at back-row volume. Two seconds of silence. Release, and move straight into the next block. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As the group sets posture | *"Feet flat. Hands still."* |
| First turn begins | *"Three faces. Two seconds each."* |
| Someone rushes the name | *"Slower. Send it to the back wall."* |
| Someone smiles or shrugs after their name | *"Hold the beat. Don't apologise for it."* |
| Someone freezes for more than three seconds | *"Next."* |
| Fidgeting spreads around the circle | *"Still hands. Nothing needs doing."* |
| Just before the unison beat | *"Eyes up. Together."* |
| After the unison 'here' | *"Two seconds. Then release."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The circle goes quiet quickly. Names land audibly from the far side of the room. Nobody laughs after their own turn. The silent beat is genuinely silent — you can hear the room. By the last third, people are turning their heads deliberately rather than glancing.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Giggling ripples round after each name. | The stillness feels exposing and laughter is the release valve. | Stop the circle. Do three slow breaths together in silence. Restart from the person after the break. Do not explain why — just reset the temperature. |
| Names are mumbled and swallowed at the end. | People are aiming the name at the circle, not the room. | Point physically at the back wall and say 'that wall, not us'. Have the last two people repeat their turn. |
| The beat gets skipped — the next person starts immediately. | Group discomfort with silence; the circle rushes to fill it. | Count the beat aloud yourself for three consecutive turns, then drop out and let them hold it. |
| Someone locks up and can't say their name. | Genuine nerves on showcase week. | Say 'next' immediately and keep going. Offer them the turn again at the end, no pressure, and do not comment on it. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Full turn round takes about ninety seconds, so run a second lap with names only — no head sweep — in the spare time. |
| **14–17** | One circle. One lap fills the two minutes almost exactly. Keep turns tight at eight seconds and don't let the pace sag in the middle. |
| **18–20** | One circle still, but shorten to two faces instead of three and six seconds a turn. Alternatively split into two circles of ten and run them simultaneously, joining for the unison 'here'. |

## Variations

**Name and one word** — After the name, add a single word — a colour, a food, anything. Then hold the beat.  
*easier — the extra word gives nervous people something to aim for and softens the exposure of a bare name*

**Full stop** — Extend the silent beat to three seconds and have the whole circle hold eye contact with the speaker throughout.  
*harder — sustained mutual gaze plus a longer pause is where people crack; only use with a group that has settled*

**Line, not circle** — Stand everyone in one line facing the empty end of the room. Each person steps forward, sweeps three faces along the line, says their name to the far wall, steps back.  
*different emphasis — shifts from being watched by a ring to addressing an audience front-on, which is closer to showcase conditions*

## Debrief

1. **What did you do with your hands?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone admits they had no idea, or that they were gripping something. Someone else notices stillness felt fine once they stopped monitoring it. Move on once two people have named a habit.

2. **What happened in the silent beat?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'It felt much longer than it was.' 'I wanted to add something.' Good enough — you want them to recognise the urge to fill, not to have beaten it.

3. **Which was harder, being looked at or looking?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* A genuine split in the room. Some found the sweep intrusive, some found being the focus worse. Don't resolve it — name that both are part of taking the stage.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact in this activity. Anyone can step out of the circle at any point and rejoin without explanation — say so before you start. Chairs available at the edge for anyone who can't stand for five minutes; the exercise works seated with the same head sweep. If eye contact is difficult, offer aiming at foreheads or shoulders instead. Never comment on a freeze, in the moment or after.

## Why it works

Stage presence and clarity are usually taught as things you add — more energy, more projection. This removes instead. With no scene, no partner and no content to hide behind, the only variables left are stillness, volume and timing. Eight seconds is short enough that nobody has to endure it, long enough that habits surface. The cue applies directly: you settle your own feet, breath and voice before you turn your attention outward to three faces. Doing it in strict rotation with no applause takes away the social reward, so people stop performing composure and start practising it.

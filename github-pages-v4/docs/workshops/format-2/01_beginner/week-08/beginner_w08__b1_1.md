# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Lucky Socks

![Lucky Socks](../img/beginner_w08__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> In pairs, everyone swaps one true pre-nerves ritual, then reports their partner's back to the circle in five words.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

Showcase day. Nerves are already in the room and pretending otherwise costs more than naming it. This runs before the two-chairs and games-night material, so the first thing people do together is admit they get scared and hear that everyone else does too. It feeds the session cue directly: you look after your own state first, then you can be useful to a scene partner. It also sets the reporting habit — brief, accurate, about someone else — which is the listening the showcase needs.

## What students actually learn

- Nerves are common equipment, not a personal defect, and saying so out loud costs nothing.
- Their attention can hold someone else's small detail accurately for a minute without commentary.
- Being reported on kindly by a partner is survivable, which is most of what a showcase feels like.

## Setup

Chairs or standing in a loose circle. No props, no writing. Know your headcount before you start so you can set the report cap. Have a timer visible or in your hand. If the room is echoey, tighten the circle now rather than mid-report.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Pair people with whoever is beside them. Brief the task: one true thing you do before something that makes you nervous. Small, specific, not a boast. Nominate A and B. |
| 1 | A speaks, B listens only. No advice, no swapping stories. Call forty seconds and cut cleanly. |
| 1 | Swap. B speaks, A listens. Remind them you will ask for the repeat-back, so listen for the detail. |
| 2 | Circle up. Round the ring: partner's name, ritual in five words or fewer, one word on whether it works. Eight seconds each, cut on time. Finish with hands up for the ritual nobody admitted to, then move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As pairs start talking | *"Small and specific. Not the impressive version."* |
| Ten seconds into the first turn | *"Listeners — nothing back. Just take it in."* |
| At forty seconds, first turn | *"Cut there. Swap. B, you're up."* |
| Just before the circle report | *"Name first, then five words. Not six."* |
| When someone starts explaining | *"Five words. Land it and pass."* |
| If a report drifts into a joke about the partner | *"Straight report. Their words, not yours."* |
| On the final hands-up | *"Hands up if you kept one back. Good. Nobody's odd."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Pairs lean in and talk quietly rather than performing to the room. The listener does not interrupt. Reports come fast and land in the right shape — a name, a concrete image, a verdict — and the room laughs at recognition rather than at anyone. By the last few reports people are pre-loading their five words and the round moves without you pushing it. The hands go up at the end without hesitation.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pairs swap advice and coping tips instead of one detail each. | The listening constraint was briefed as a suggestion, not a rule. | Interrupt once, loudly: listeners say nothing. Restate that A gets the full forty seconds alone. It only needs saying once. |
| Reports run to fifteen or twenty seconds and the block overruns. | Nobody chose their five words during the listening turn. | Cut mid-sentence on the third overrun and say the cap out loud again. Drop to four words for the remaining reports if you are behind. |
| Someone says they have no rituals and nothing to report. | They read 'ritual' as something mystical or embarrassing rather than habitual. | Offer the mundane frame: what they do with their phone, their hands, their coffee, the route they take. Take a plain habit and move on. |
| Reports get mocking — partners playing the ritual for a laugh. | Showcase-day energy tipping into deflection. | Name it neutrally: straight report, no impressions. Model one yourself in a flat voice, then continue. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Eight seconds per report is comfortable; you will finish the round with time for a second show of hands. Take one extra report from any threesome. |
| **14–17** | One circle, cap reports at six seconds and cut without apology. Start the report round the moment the second forty seconds ends — no regrouping chat. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles for the report round with a confident student anchoring the second. Cap at five seconds, five words. Do the hands-up together as one room at the end. |

## Variations

**Three words** — Cut the report from five words to three. Keep everything else identical.  
*Harder — forces real selection during the listening turn rather than paraphrase afterwards.*

**Own it** — Skip the report-back. Each person says their own ritual in five words to the circle.  
*Easier — no memory load, no responsibility for someone else's words. Use with a very new or very anxious group.*

**Show, don't say** — Instead of speaking the report, each person performs their partner's ritual as a three-second silent action; the circle guesses the words.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from listening accuracy to physical clarity, which suits a showcase built on games.*

## Debrief

1. **What was harder — talking for forty seconds or listening for forty?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone admits listening was harder because they were assembling their own story instead of taking in the other person's. Move on as soon as one person says it plainly.

2. **How many rituals in that circle were actually about calming down, and how many were just habit?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* People notice most were habit — a route, an order of doing things — and that the calm comes from repetition rather than content. That is the whole point; do not extend it.

3. **Your partner heard your nerves and reported them back. How did that land?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something like 'less exposed than I expected' or 'it sounded smaller out loud'. One or two answers is enough — then say the cue and go.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No touch and no eye-contact requirement. Anyone can pass on the ritual and say a plain fact about themselves instead — nobody is asked to disclose anything about health, medication or history, and say so in the brief. If a partner shares something heavier than intended, the reporter chooses a lighter true detail from what they heard; tell them that rule before the report round. Seated participation works throughout. Deaf or hard-of-hearing participants should be paired facing each other, away from the noisiest part of the room, and reports should be given standing still rather than while walking the circle.

## Why it works

Stage presence and clarity fail on showcase day mostly because people are managing hidden nerves while trying to look composed. This puts the nerves on the table in the first five minutes, at a size nobody can be embarrassed by, so the energy that would go into hiding goes into the work instead. The two-turn listening structure enforces the cue mechanically: you get your own forty seconds with no obligation to respond, then you take responsibility for your partner's words. Self first, partner second, in that order. The five-word cap trains the same compression the showcase needs — choose the clear detail, deliver it, get off — and the eight-second cut teaches that a short offer landed on time is enough.

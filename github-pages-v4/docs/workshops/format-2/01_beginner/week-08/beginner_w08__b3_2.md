# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Across the Gap

![Across the Gap](../img/beginner_w08__b3_2.jpg){ .infographic }

> Two facing lines send one plain true sentence across a widening gap, and every receiver says out loud that they got it.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

It opens the showcase week. Seven weeks of scene work happened at conversational range; tonight there is an audience and a back wall. This warm-up moves the body's default volume and eyeline outward before anyone plays a scene, so the two-chairs work later lands without anyone being told to 'project'.

## What students actually learn

- That being heard at the back is a matter of breath and stance, not of pushing harder with the throat.
- That the receiver's confirmation is information — you can tell whether you landed, rather than guessing.
- That you can be plain and still be watchable; nothing here rewards being clever.

## Setup

Clear a strip of floor long enough for two lines to stand three metres apart and then step back another metre each. Chairs pushed to the walls. No props. Know your room's back wall — that is the target for the widest round.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Form two facing lines, three metres apart, everyone opposite a partner. Each person picks one plain true sentence about today. Say the example out loud. Stop anyone workshopping a funny one. |
| 1 | All at once on your call: settle feet, one breath, find the eyes, send. Receivers answer 'Heard you.' Two exchanges each way. Expect noise — that is fine. |
| 1 | Slide one line right by one; end person walks round. New partner, new sentence. Same order: settle, breathe, look, send. Two exchanges. |
| 1 | Both lines step back one big pace. Slide again. Repeat across the wider gap. Call for belly, not throat. Two exchanges. |
| 1 | Silent round: slide once more, send with eyes and stance only, receiver nods when they have it. Then call the lines in, one word each on what changed, and move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As the first round starts | *"Feet first. Breathe before you speak."* |
| When people rush the send | *"Find the eyes, then send. Not before."* |
| When receivers mumble the confirmation | *"'Heard you' — mean it, or don't say it."* |
| After the step back | *"Belly, not throat. Let the floor push it out."* |
| When someone starts performing the sentence | *"Plain is enough. Just tell them."* |
| During the silent round | *"Nod only when you've actually got it."* |
| Last ten seconds | *"Hold the look one beat past comfortable."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    By the third round the room is loud without being strained, and people are standing squarer to their partners. Sentences stay dull and delivery stops apologising. In the silent round, most nods come late — after a real look, not politely on cue.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone shouting from the throat, voices rasping by round three. | They are treating volume as effort rather than support, and the gap widened faster than their breath did. | Stop the room for five seconds. One hand on the belly, one breath in, then send on the way out. Restart the round. |
| People turning the sentence into a bit and waiting for a laugh. | Showcase week nerves. Being funny feels safer than being seen saying something plain. | Call it: 'Boring sentence, real look.' Give them a boring one to use — 'I had toast.' Nobody is allowed to improve it. |
| The confirmation becomes an automatic reflex, said before the sentence finishes. | The line has become a rhythm exercise instead of a receiving one. | Add a beat: receivers must wait until the sender's mouth closes, then breathe, then answer. |
| The slide collapses into confusion, people opposite nobody. | Both lines moved, or the end person did not walk round. | Point at the line that moves and say 'only this line'. Walk the end person round yourself the first time. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One pair of lines. Run all four partner changes as written. Take a place in the line yourself if numbers are odd. |
| **14–17** | One pair of lines still works, but the strip gets long — drop to three partner changes and give the extra time to the widest gap. Keep the silent round. |
| **18–20** | Two pairs of lines at opposite ends of the room, facing away from each other so they do not compete. Two partner changes each. Call the step-back and the silent round for both simultaneously. |

## Variations

**Half the gap** — Start the lines at arm's length and only widen once, by a single pace. Keep everything else identical.  
*Easier — for a nervous or vocally tired group; keeps the breath work without the volume demand.*

**Back wall** — For the final spoken round, senders stand against one wall and receivers against the opposite one, as far apart as the room allows.  
*Harder — forces genuine support and stops anyone getting away with conversational volume.*

**Same sentence, new person** — Everyone keeps their original sentence for all rounds instead of choosing a new one each time.  
*Different emphasis — shifts attention off invention and onto how the same words change with distance and partner.*

## Debrief

1. **What changed in your body when the gap widened?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something physical and specific — feet planted wider, breath dropped, chin lifted. Not 'I got louder'. If you get 'louder', ask 'louder from where?'

2. **Did you know when you had landed it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They point at the confirmation or the nod as evidence — 'I could see her get it before she said anything.' That means they were watching the receiver, not monitoring themselves.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact in this activity. Voice work: tell people up front that anyone with a sore throat, a cold, or who is recovering their voice can mouth the sentence and let their partner nod instead — no explanation needed. Anyone who cannot stand for five minutes takes a chair in the line; partners adjust their eyeline down. Eye contact is part of the exercise, but say that looking at the bridge of the nose counts, and mean it. Sentences stay light — if someone offers something heavy, receive it and move the line on.

## Why it works

Stage presence and clarity fail when performers calibrate to the person in front of them and never re-calibrate to the room. Widening the gap in stages forces recalibration four times in five minutes, and each time the body has to find support rather than strain. The receiver's spoken confirmation makes landing measurable, so nobody guesses. That is the cue in practice: settle your own feet and breath first — take care of yourself — and only then send it across to your partner. Reverse the order and you push, and pushing reads as tension from the back row.

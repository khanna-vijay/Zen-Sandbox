# 🏟️ Showcase round — Two Chairs, One Night

![Two Chairs, One Night](../img/beginner_w08__b9_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Every pair gets two chairs, a named introduction and one honest scene in front of a real audience.

`Players 10–20` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: required`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 08 run-sheet](../week-08.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week eight is the last one. Seven weeks of two-person work in a closed room now happens once in front of people who did not take the course. Everything before this — yes-and, listening, staying in the relationship, the two-chair form itself — has been rehearsed with no stakes. This block supplies the only ingredient that could not be simulated: a real audience, real light, real silence before the first line. It feeds nothing further in the syllabus. It is the thing the syllabus was for, and it sends people out with proof that they stood up.

## What students actually learn

- That nerves do not remove the ability to hear a partner. They will remember being frightened and still catching a line.
- That an audience is not an examiner. Laughter comes and goes and the scene keeps working either way.
- That the bell ends the scene, not their cleverness. Endings stop being something to fear.
- That looking after your own footing — breath, volume, where you are standing — is what makes you available to your partner.

## Setup

Chairs for the audience facing a clear playing space. Two matching chairs on stage, plus a spare within reach for the trio slot. One bell, or a light cue you can hit cleanly. The running order from week seven printed large and taped where you can see it without hunting. The pairs' cards collected before the doors open, stacked in running order. Water at the side. A seat in the front row for anyone who steps out. If you have guests, seat them mixed with the class rather than in their own block — a solid wall of strangers reads harder from the stage.

## How to run it — 25 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 3 | House lights half. Welcome the room in thirty seconds. Explain the form: two chairs, a relationship, a location, one suggestion, a few minutes. Read the running order aloud. Take the first pair's suggestion. |
| 6 | Scenes one and two. Name both performers, read the card, take the suggestion, go. Bell at three to four minutes. Both bow. Reset chairs yourself between scenes so the next pair walks onto a clean stage. |
| 6 | Scenes three and four. Same pattern. Keep your patter between scenes to two sentences — a name and a card. Momentum matters more than warmth here; the room stays warm if it keeps moving. |
| 6 | Scenes five and six. If the room has gone quiet, do not comment on it. Name the next pair and carry on. Watch your clock against the running order and start trimming scenes towards three minutes if you are behind. |
| 2 | Buffer. Use it for a slot that overran, a pair who need a moment, or an extra scene if you are ahead. If you do not need it, take a short pause, refill water, and let the room settle. |
| 2 | Call all pairs to the stage. One company bow together. House lights up. Thank the audience, then thank each performer by name. Do not give notes and do not review the show. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| To a pair waiting in the wings, quietly, before they walk on | *"Contact rules out loud. Now."* |
| To the pair as they step on, under your breath | *"Two chairs. Take your time getting there."* |
| First thirty seconds, if a pair are gabbling | *"Slow. Let them hear you."* |
| When a pair stand frozen apart, addressing the room lightly so it lands as staging, not rescue | *"Sit down together."* |
| When someone abandons the relationship to chase the suggestion | *"Back to the person in front of you."* |
| When a pair start visibly hunting for an ending | *"The bell ends it. Keep going."* |
| Immediately after the bell, to both performers | *"Bow. Both of you."* |
| To the whole company before the final bow | *"Line up. Look out at them."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Scenes start slowly and get louder. Performers face out enough to be heard and still talk to each other. The suggestion appears once or twice and then stops mattering. Some scenes are funny and some are quiet, and the room accepts both. The bell lands mid-conversation and nobody apologises for it. Every pair bows. At the company bow you can see people looking at the audience rather than the floor.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| A scene collapses at forty seconds — both performers laugh, break, and look at you. | Nerves. They have decided the scene has already failed and are trying to leave the stage politely. | Do not cut. Call "sit down together" and let them restart from the chairs. Ten seconds of silence on stage costs nothing. If it happens twice, ring the bell early, get the bow, and move on without comment. |
| A pair perform something clearly written — clean plot beats, punchlines that land on cue, no listening. | They rehearsed in the corridor because a real audience frightened them into planning. | Let it run; correcting it mid-show helps nobody. Before the next scene, remind the whole room the card holds a relationship and a location only. Note it for the debrief, not the stage. |
| Scenes run long and you are four minutes behind by the third pair. | You are hesitating on the bell because the scene is going well. | Ring it at three minutes flat for the rest of the show. A good scene cut early is remembered better than a good scene allowed to sag. Use the buffer block only once. |
| Classmates in the audience heckle affectionately or over-laugh at every line. | Support tipping into noise. The performers stop hearing each other. | Between scenes, say plainly that the best thing the room can give is attention. Do not scold. It usually settles within one scene. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Five or six pairs, possibly one trio. Every pair plays. Scenes get the full three to four minutes and you will probably use the buffer for an overrun. Keep your between-scene patter to a name and a card. |
| **14–17** | Seven or eight pairs. Cap scenes at three minutes and ring the bell on the number. Drop the buffer block into the running order as one extra scene slot. Reset chairs while announcing the next pair to save thirty seconds a time. |
| **18–20** | Nine or ten pairs will not fit honestly in twenty-five minutes. Split the showcase: half the pairs in this block, half in a second block later in the session, with a games-night segment between. Do not compress scenes below two and a half minutes — under that nobody gets a relationship going. One company bow at the very end, everyone on stage. |

## Variations

**Warm house** — No outside guests. Classmates only, seated close, lights unchanged from the rehearsal room. Scenes run two to three minutes.  
*Easier. Removes the strangers and the lighting change, keeps the running order, the naming and the bows. Use it when the cohort is visibly frightened in week seven.*

**Card swap** — Pairs write their card as usual, then hand it to the pair playing after them. Each pair performs a relationship and location they did not choose.  
*Harder. Strips away the one thing they had prepared, so the whole scene rests on the first minute of listening.*

**Games night mix** — Alternate two-chair scenes with short whole-group games the audience can call into. Three scenes, two games, three scenes.  
*Different emphasis. Shifts weight from sustained two-person scene work towards audience contact, playing status in a crowd, and recovering quickly in public.*

## Debrief

1. **What did you notice about your own body in the first thirty seconds out there?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something specific and physical — shallow breath, fast talking, gripping the chair, not knowing where to look. Not "it was fine" and not self-criticism. When two or three people name a concrete sensation and others nod, move on.

2. **When did you stop performing and start talking to your partner?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They can point to a moment — a line their partner gave them, sitting down, a laugh from the room. The useful version names something the partner did, not something they decided internally.

3. **What did the audience give you that the rehearsal room could not?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Honest, including the uncomfortable answers: silence was information, laughter was distracting, being watched made them slower. Anything other than "it was great". Do not fish for gratitude.

4. **What would you keep from tonight if you carry on playing?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* One portable thing — take the chair, look at your partner, the ending will arrive on its own. Concrete and small. Stop the debrief once everyone who wants to speak has spoken; do not go round the circle.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    Consent is a spoken step in the wings before every scene, not a blanket agreement from week one. Each pair names permitted contact aloud each time. Either performer can say "switch" and all contact stops immediately; no explanation is asked for, then or later. Say to the whole room at the top of the session that "switch" exists so the audience hears it as normal. Anyone who does not want to perform tonight has a real job: run the bell, hold the running order, manage the door, greet guests. Offer this privately in week seven, not from the stage. Check the playing space is reachable without steps for anyone who needs that, and place a chair in the front row for anyone who steps out mid-show. Do not photograph or record unless every performer has said yes on the night. Nobody is asked to explain a scene afterwards.

## Why it works

Stage presence and clarity are not decisions a performer can make under pressure by wanting to. They come from having removed everything else that needs deciding. The card supplies the relationship and the location before the lights go up, so the two things a beginner would otherwise burn their attention on are already settled. The bell owns the ending, so the last thirty seconds are not spent negotiating an exit. The consent step settles physical proximity before adrenaline arrives, when judgement is still reliable. What is left for the performer is breath, volume, where they stand and who they are looking at — which is exactly what the cue points at. Take care of yourself first: get your feet under you, get loud enough to be heard. Only a performer who is not managing their own panic has any attention spare for a partner. The order in the cue is the mechanism, not a sentiment.

# Week 08 — The Public Showcase — Two Chairs or Games Night

> *You can walk out in front of strangers, say something plain, and stay in the scene when it does not land.*

| Course | Week | Domain | Focus | Stage | Length | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foundations — The Brave Beginner | 8/8 | D5 | `D5.S3` — Stage Presence & Clarity | Novice → Advanced Beginner | 180 min | 10–20 |

!!! note "Builds on"
    Week 7's dress run exposed the rough edges; this week does the same show for people who did not watch it being made.

## 🎬 The arc of this session

They arrive early, wired, and wanting one more rehearsal they do not need. You spend the day keeping their hands busy, their feet grounded and their material untouched, so the energy goes into presence rather than fiddling. They leave having stood in front of strangers, said something plain, and stayed in the scene when it wobbled.

!!! tip "Watch for"
    The urge to rehearse. Every request to "just run ours once more" is nerves asking for a job; give them a physical warm-up or a task instead, and protect the material from being sanded down to nothing an hour before the show.

## 🎒 Before you start

- Set the room in performance shape before anyone arrives: two chairs centre, a clear entrance side, chairs or benches for the audience, and a marked backstage line people can wait behind.
- Test the lights and any sound you are using, and run the exact cue sequence once alone so you never troubleshoot in front of the cast.
- Print the running order in large type and pin it backstage; print a second copy for yourself with names, not just pair numbers.
- Water on a table at the side, plus a bin. Tell people where it is rather than expecting them to find it.
- Review Week 7's dress run notes and pick at most two technical fixes — entrances and running order only. Bring nothing about content.
- Decide now who greets the audience, who counts heads and what time you start regardless of latecomers.

## 1. 🧊 Ice-breaker (5 min)

*"Bags down, shoes on, come into a circle. We're not touching the material today — first thing we do is something completely useless."*

**Why here:** Gives the early arrivals something to do with their hands and their nerves that is not rehearsing.

### Lucky Socks

> In pairs, everyone swaps one true pre-nerves ritual, then reports their partner's back to the circle in five words.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Pair everyone with the person next to them. Odd number, make one three and warn them their reports run back-to-back.
2. Brief it: tell your partner one true thing you do before something that makes you nervous. Exams, dates, dentists. Small and specific, not impressive.
3. Partner A speaks, B listens only. No advice, no matching stories. Call time at forty seconds.
4. Swap. B speaks, A listens. Tell them both you will ask them to repeat it back.
5. Reform the circle. Each person says their partner's name, then that ritual in five words or fewer, then one word on whether it works.
6. Cap each report at eight seconds. Cut politely and move on; do not let anyone elaborate.
7. Finish with a show of hands: who has a ritual they did not admit to. Take the laugh and move straight on.

## 2. 😄 Laughter Blast (5 min)

*"Everyone here is going to wobble tonight. Let's do it now, on purpose, all together."*

**Why here:** Names the wobble out loud and makes it physical and funny before it can become private and frightening.

### Wobble and Bow

> Everyone shakes their whole body while letting their voice wobble, then takes three enormous synchronised bows at nobody.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Bring everyone into one circle, arms' length apart. Shake your right hand hard and let a long wobbling 'aaaah' come out. Go sillier than you expect anyone else to. Then wave them in.
2. Count down together as a whole circle: eight shakes of each limb with the wobbling 'aaaah', then four, then two, then one. The voice wobbles every single time.
3. Keep the shaking going and swap the sound. Call motorboat lip-flaps, then a swooping siren, then a low cartoon growl. Everyone changes on your call, together.
4. Shake all four limbs at once and add deliberate 'ha-ha-ha' on every wobble. Push through the awkward stretch. It usually turns into real laughing after about thirty seconds.
5. Call a dead stop. Silence, still bodies. Then lead three enormous slow bows to the empty middle of the circle, arm sweeping wide, on your count.
6. After each bow, let the room roar and applaud itself. Loud. Then ask one question, take two or three quick answers, and move straight into the next block.

## 3. 🔥 Warm-up cluster (15 min)

*"Three quick things. Body, then voice, then eyes. Then we talk for twenty minutes and then we leave your material alone."*

**Why here:** Puts voice, focus and shared attention into performance shape without touching any scene content.

### Charge and Plant

> Groups jog to a downstage line, stop dead, and send one plain sentence to the back wall without shrinking.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Mark a downstage line with tape or two chairs. Send everyone to the back wall. Have them bounce on the balls of the feet, knees loose, breathing out audibly.
2. Explain the shape: charge to the line, stop dead, hold, then walk back along the side wall. Nobody crosses the line.
3. Run waves of five. Jog to the line, stop, plant feet shoulder-width, drop the shoulders, eyes on the far wall. Hold three slow counts.
4. Repeat the waves. On the plant, each person says their own name at full volume to the far wall, one at a time down the line.
5. Repeat again. Instead of the name, each says one plain true sentence about the room — the floor is cold, the lights are on. No jokes, no cleverness.
6. Run a final pass with watchers deliberately silent and blank. Speakers finish the sentence anyway, hold two more seconds, then walk off without hurrying.

### Across the Gap

> Two facing lines send one plain true sentence across a widening gap, and every receiver says out loud that they got it.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Split the room into two equal lines facing each other, about three metres apart. Everyone stands opposite one partner. If numbers are odd, take a line yourself.
2. Tell each person to choose one plain, true sentence about their day. 'I nearly missed the train.' No jokes required. Nobody has to be interesting.
3. On your call, everyone works at once: feet settled, one breath, find your partner's eyes, send the sentence. The receiver answers 'Heard you.' Two exchanges each.
4. Slide one line one place to the right. The person off the end walks round to the far end. New partner, new sentence, same order: settle, breathe, look, send.
5. Both lines take one big step back. Slide again. Repeat across the wider gap. Remind them volume comes from the belly, not from squeezing the throat.
6. Run a silent round. Slide once more. Send the sentence with eyes and stance only. The receiver nods only when they have genuinely received it.
7. Call the lines in. Take one word each on what changed as the gap widened. Do not discuss. Move straight to the next block.

### Lighthouse Sweep

> Standing still in a circle, each person sweeps eye contact across three faces, says their own name to the back wall, and holds one beat of silence before passing on.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand everyone in one circle, arm's length apart. No props, no chairs. Tell them this is a standing exercise and they can sit out at any point without explanation.
2. Demonstrate one turn yourself: turn your head to three faces across the circle, roughly two seconds on each, then say your own name at back-wall volume.
3. After the name, hold one silent beat. No smile, no shrug, no sitting down into it. Then the turn passes to the next person clockwise.
4. Set the group posture first: feet flat, knees soft, hands still at sides, three slow breaths together. Name the rule aloud — no fidgeting, no shuffling, no laughing it off.
5. Go round the circle in order. Roughly eight seconds a turn. Say 'next' if someone freezes, and move on without comment. No applause, no reactions between turns.
6. Finish in unison. Everyone stands still, eyes up, and says 'here' together at back-row volume. Hold two seconds of silence, then release the circle.

## 4. 🧠 Theory — Stage Presence & Clarity (20 min)

![Stage Presence & Clarity](../../../assets/infographics/D5.S3.jpg){ .infographic }

- **The big idea:** Stage presence is not a personality. It is a set of physical choices — where you stand, how you breathe, whether your voice reaches the back row — and it is the thing you take care of first, before you can take care of your partner.
- **Where you are on the path:** Advanced Beginner. They can listen and accept offers reliably in a workshop room. In front of strangers, that competence drops by about a third, and they compensate by talking faster and getting smaller. Today is the first time most of them have felt that gap.
- **The one cue to coach:** *“Take care of yourself first, then your partner.”*

**The three beats**

1. **Say it.** Say: "Today you have one job before any other job. Feet still, weight even, breathe out before you speak, and let your voice reach the person furthest away. That's it. Take care of yourself first, then your partner. If you're braced and quiet, you cannot hear anything your partner gives you." Then say the harder part plainly: "Some things will not land. That is normal and it is not a signal to stop. Stay in the scene. The audience follows whoever looks like they intend to be there."
2. **Show it.** Take the stage yourself. Do sixty seconds of one plain line — "I've brought the ladder back" — three ways. First: shifting weight, half-volume, eyes down. Second: same line, still feet, voice out, eyes on a real person at the back. Third: say it, get nothing back from the room, and stay standing in it for four full seconds without apologising or explaining. Do not comment on which was better. Ask the room what they saw.
3. **Try it.** Everyone stands in a loose line facing the audience seating. One at a time, walk to centre, plant, breathe out, say your own name and one true short sentence to the back wall, hold for two seconds, walk off. Whole room, no repeats, no coaching mid-line. If someone rushes, call "again, slower" once and let them go.

!!! warning "The misunderstanding that always comes up"
    They hear "take care of yourself first" as permission to be selfish or to steamroll. Correct it directly: taking care of yourself means being audible, still and breathing, so that you are actually available to your partner. It is a precondition for generosity, not an alternative to it.

## 5. 🎲 Drill A — isolation (30 min)

*"Nothing clever in this one. Walk on, be seen, walk off. That's the whole task."*

**Why here:** Isolates presence with no scene demands attached, so they can feel stillness and volume as choices rather than accidents.

### The Open Frame

> Master physical sightlines and body angles to share every silent choice with the entire room.

`Players 3–99` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Set two chairs and a small prop table downstage. Mark the back row of seats. Tell everyone that row is the audience you are playing to.
2. Call two or three players up. Give them a short physical scenario with a prop: weighing a stolen coin, reading a letter, checking a passport.
3. Tell them to play the scenario honestly and pursue what their character wants. Add one rule: keep faces, bodies and props turned so the back row sees everything.
4. Seat the rest of the group as audience. Ask them to watch sightlines only — whose face vanished, which prop disappeared, who got blocked.
5. Call 'Freeze!' the moment a back is fully turned, a partner is masked, a prop is hidden in a palm, or a reaction is lost to the floor.
6. Name what was lost in plain words: 'Your face went behind your shoulder.' 'The letter is flat to the floor.' Do not comment on the acting.
7. Have them adjust slowly in the frozen shape — open the hips, lift the prop, cheat the angle — keeping the same feeling. Then call 'Resume!'
8. Rotate a new group in every three to four minutes so everyone stands on stage at least once and feels the adjustment in their own body.

## ☕ Break — 10 minutes

Water, notes, informal talk. Non-negotiable at three hours — do not let it collapse into a working discussion.

## 6. 🎲 Drill B — under load (30 min)

*"Same job as before, but now there's someone else out there and you have to actually hear them."*

**Why here:** Adds load: a partner and a listening demand, so presence has to survive alongside something else.

### The Echo Line

> Slow down and discover the game of your scene by repeating the first unusual offer.

`Players 2–99` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Ask the room for one mundane suggestion — a household object or a small errand. Take the first thing offered. Do not shop for a funnier one.
2. Send two players up. Give them roughly a minute of ordinary scene: who they are, where they are, how they know each other.
3. Tell both players to listen for the first unusual thing — a line that is odd, too honest, oddly warm, or unexpectedly sharp. Not a joke.
4. Either player repeats that line back, word for word, with the same commitment or more. Nobody negotiates. Whoever hears it first takes it.
5. Volley the identical line back and forth at least five times. Change tone, tempo, body and intention every pass. Never change the words.
6. Call "back to the scene." Players resume, now treating the attitude they uncovered as the centre of the interaction.
7. Cap each scene at three minutes. Cut with a clear "thank you," reset the chairs, next pair up.

## 7. 🎯 Scene Lab (30 min)

*"Running order on the wall. We're doing a technical pass — entrances, exits, who follows who. Play it properly but don't defend it."*

**Why here:** Runs the shape of the show under low stakes so the entrances, handovers and endings are in their bodies before an audience arrives.

### Instant Game Engine

> Spontaneously invent and play absurd, unwritten games by committing to the unknown together.

`Players 3–99` · `~30 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy high` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

**How to play**

1. Stand the group in a loose circle with clear floor space. Tell them everyone plays every round; nobody sits out unless they choose to.
2. Explain the trigger: one person steps in and shouts "Let's play [made-up game name]!" The name should be nonsense. No planning, no explaining.
3. Drill the response. On any offer, the whole group shouts "Yeah!" at full volume and starts moving immediately. Rehearse this once before the first real round.
4. Tell them the rules get invented by watching. Whatever the first bodies and voices do becomes the law of the game. Copy it, then add to it.
5. Let each round run thirty to sixty seconds. Call "Freeze!" while the energy is still up, not after it sags.
6. After each freeze, ask two or three players to state a rule of the game they just played. Take their answers as correct. Do not correct them.
7. Reset the circle and take a new initiator. Rotate so different people start. Keep going until time is up.

**Side-coach:** *“"Enter on your own feet. Don't wait to be pushed."”*

## 8. 🏟️ Showcase round (25 min)

*"Backstage line, water in hand, phones off. When I bring the audience in, we start. Take care of yourself first, then your partner."*

**Why here:** The showcase itself: the same material, performed for people who did not watch it being built.

### Two Chairs, One Night

> Every pair gets two chairs, a named introduction and one honest scene in front of a real audience.

`Players 10–20` · `~25 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact negotiated` · `Props: required`

**How to play**

1. Keep the pairs fixed from week one. With an odd number, make one trio who play a single slot with a third chair added.
2. Run the consent step in the wings before every scene. Each pair says aloud what contact is fine — handshake, hand on shoulder, none.
3. Confirm out loud that either performer can say "switch" and all contact stops instantly, with no explanation asked for or given.
4. Collect one card per pair carrying a relationship and a location. Nothing else. No plot, no lines, no agreed first beat.
5. Set two chairs. Read the card aloud, name both performers, then take one audience suggestion: a single word or a small everyday object.
6. Tell the pair to use the suggestion inside the relationship they already own, not to build a new scene around it.
7. Run each scene three to four minutes. Cut it with a bell or a light. Say plainly that nobody has to end cleverly.
8. Follow the running order fixed in week seven. Both performers bow after every scene. Finish with all pairs on stage, one company bow, house lights up, and thank the room by name.

## 9. 💭 Reflection & homework (10 min)

**Deepen your improv**
1. Where in your body did the nerves live tonight, and what did you do with your feet and your breath when you noticed?
2. Name one moment that did not land. What did you do in the four seconds after it, and what would you do next time?

**Beyond the stage**
3. Where else in your week do you get smaller and faster when you are being watched — and what would planting your feet and finishing the sentence look like there?

??? note "🎓 Facilitator notes"

    **Timing risks**
    - The technical pass in Scene Lab will overrun if you let anyone re-work content. Hold it to entrances, order and exits, and cut anything that becomes a note session.
    - Audience arrival is the real deadline. Build in the door opening ten minutes before the showcase block and start on time even if a pair is still pacing backstage.
    - Reflection gets squeezed by post-show adrenaline and visitors in the room. Clear the audience out first, then sit down for the full ten minutes.

    **Failure modes this week**
    - Someone freezes on entrance and apologises to the audience. Have a pre-agreed rescue: you call "and again" from the side, they re-enter, the show continues. Tell them this before the show so it is a plan, not a failure.
    - A pair decides at the last minute to change what they are doing. Say no, plainly and warmly. Nerves disguise themselves as good ideas.
    - Over-performing: volume up, pace up, listening gone. Side-coach the whole cast on stillness during the technical pass, not individuals in the show.
    - One person goes quiet and drifts to the edge of the room all afternoon. Find them at the break, give them a job — water table, running order, door — and check they still intend to go on.

    **What good looks like by the end:** Every person walks out and performs. Nobody apologises on stage. At least two visible wobbles get played through rather than abandoned. The cast comes off louder and looser than they went on, and the reflection has more specifics in it than praise.

    **Carry into next week:** They now know what their nerves do to their body and voice. Next block of work starts from that: presence as a repeatable set of physical choices, and the beginning of listening under real pressure.

# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Petty Grievances

![Petty Grievances](../img/beginner_w05__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Pairs swap one tiny thing that annoyed them this week, then report each other's back to the circle.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 05 run-sheet](../week-05.md)

## Why this activity, here

First thing in the room on the week the body takes over. Voices are still cold and nobody has committed to anything physical yet. This gets everyone speaking once, in a low-stakes register, and trains the ear for concrete detail — the bin, the third stair, the kettle. That habit of specificity is what you will hold them to all session when the cue is "show me where you are". It feeds directly into the space-work blocks, where a vague mime and a vague grievance fail for the same reason.

## What students actually learn

- They notice that a detail lands and a summary does not — and they carry that into their space work an hour later.
- They hear their own words come back from someone else's mouth, which is the first low-cost taste of being watched.
- They learn the room's names, and that they are expected to have listened.
- They find out their small private irritation is shared, which lowers the cost of the next thing they say out loud.

## Setup

No props. One circle of chairs or standing space, clear enough to break into pairs and re-form fast. Have your own grievance ready before the door opens — a real one, small and dull, with a place attached. If numbers are odd, plan one trio and shorten its speaking turns.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Give your own petty grievance, specific and boring. State the rule: petty only, nothing that actually hurts. Send them into pairs — nearest person, no negotiating. |
| 1 | Both speak. Call "swap" at forty seconds. Side-coach the listeners to hold the detail, not the summary. |
| 2 | Circle. Each person reports their partner's grievance in one sentence, naming them. Partner nods or corrects in a single word. Keep it moving; cut riffs. |
| 1 | Show of hands on the most common annoyance. Count the biggest overlap out loud. Thank them and go straight into the warm-up. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As pairs start talking | *"Petty. Smaller than that."* |
| Twenty seconds into the first speaker | *"Where were you standing when it happened?"* |
| At forty seconds | *"Swap. Second speaker, go."* |
| As the circle reports begin | *"Their name, then the thing. One sentence."* |
| When a report starts turning into a story | *"Land it. Next."* |
| When a partner starts explaining their correction | *"One word only."* |
| During the show of hands | *"Hands up, count them, keep them up."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    Reports come fast and sound like overheard sentences — a name, an object, a place. People are grinning at how small the complaints are. At least two grievances get a wave of hands. The whole thing ends before anyone gets comfortable enough to start performing, and the room is warm and talking as you move into the warm-up.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Someone offers a genuine hardship — a bereavement, a diagnosis, a break-up — and the pair goes quiet. | The word "annoyance" is broad, and some people arrive carrying something and take the first invitation to put it down. | Restate the rule to the whole room, not the pair: "Petty only — bin-lid level." Give a second dull example of your own. Let them switch to a smaller one with no comment. |
| Reports balloon into two-minute anecdotes with impressions, and you overrun. | The first reporter got a laugh and the rest of the circle now sees it as a slot to be funny in. | Interrupt on the next one. Say the model sentence again, out loud, and hold the group to the shape. Take the next three reports yourself as rapid-fire prompts: "Name. Thing. Next." |
| Two or three people say they can't think of anything. | Trivial irritations are forgettable by design, and being asked cold in front of strangers empties the head. | Give categories instead of asking again: kitchen, commute, phone, neighbour, weather. Say them as a list. Almost everyone lands on one within seconds. |
| Reporters say "she was annoyed about her neighbour" — summary, no picture. | They listened for the point rather than the detail, which is the default adult listening habit. | Ask one question back: "Parked where?" Let them supply the detail, then move on. Doing this twice publicly retrains the rest of the circle. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Everyone reports. Two minutes covers thirteen reports at roughly nine seconds each — comfortable. Keep the hands-up count to a single question. |
| **14–17** | One circle, everyone reports, but cut hard. Say up front: "One sentence, no preamble." Take the reports in circle order rather than volunteers so nobody hesitates. Skip the model sentence on the second half. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of ten for the reporting phase and run them at the same time; stand between so you can hear both. Bring the room back together for the show of hands only, asking each circle for its most common grievance. |

## Variations

**Report your own** — Skip the pairs. Each person gives their own petty grievance straight to the circle in one sentence. Two minutes total.  
*Easier — removes the memory load and the pressure of representing someone else. Use it if the group is very new or very quiet.*

**Place first** — Reporters must name the location before the complaint: "On the third stair, Dev's cat waits to trip him." No location, no report.  
*Harder — forces the detail to be spatial, which sets up the week's cue directly. Some people will stall on the first attempt.*

**Show, don't say** — Reporters give the grievance without words: three seconds of physical action, then the circle guesses. Partner confirms with a nod.  
*Different emphasis — moves the block from listening to embodiment. Costs an extra minute and needs a group already willing to stand up.*

## Debrief

1. **Which report gave you a picture in your head, and what was in it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* They name a concrete object or place — the bin, the kettle, the seat by the door — rather than saying "the funny one". Once you have one answer like that, move on.

2. **What was harder, telling yours or holding someone else's?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone says holding it, because they had to keep the exact words. That is the point of the block. Take two answers maximum and go into the warm-up.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No physical contact at any point. Anyone may pass on reporting; their partner reports instead, or they simply say "pass" and the circle moves on with no comment from you. State the petty-only rule before pairs form, not after — it is the whole safety mechanism. If someone shares something heavy in the pair, do not surface it in the circle; the reporter gives a substitute or passes. People with hearing difficulties should pair with someone facing them and near you; keep the pair phase quiet enough that voices don't compete. Standing is not required — run the circle seated if anyone prefers.

## Why it works

The session skill is physicality and space work, and the failure mode all week is the vague gesture that could be any object anywhere. This block attacks that in language first, where the cost of trying is low. Being asked to repeat someone else's grievance forces retention of a specific noun in a specific place, because the summary version is visibly duller when spoken aloud and the partner's one-word correction exposes it. Students hear the difference between "her neighbour annoys her" and "her neighbour parks across her bin" in the same two minutes. When you later say "show me where you are, don't tell me", they already have a working example of what specific beats general. The petty-only rule keeps stakes low so nobody spends attention managing exposure, and the hands-up count converts a room of strangers into a room with something in common before the first physical exercise.

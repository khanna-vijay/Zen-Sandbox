# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Three Beats, Then One Thing

![Three Beats, Then One Thing](../img/beginner_w03__b3_3.jpg){ .infographic }

> Seated and quiet, the room builds one place together — each speaker repeats the line they were handed word for word, waits three beats, then adds exactly one detail.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 03 run-sheet](../week-03.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 3 opens the door on Yes, And. This is the first block of the session, so it sets the temperature before anything scenic happens. It puts the accepting half of Yes, And under a microscope by making people say the offer aloud before they build on it. Everything later in the session — two-person scenes, gift-giving, the first Yes, And scenes on their feet — assumes the offer has actually landed. This is where that lands.

## What students actually learn

- They hear how much of an offer they normally miss — repeating it word for word exposes the gap.
- Three beats of silence stops feeling like failure and starts feeling like room to think.
- One detail is enough. They stop believing a contribution has to be big to count.

## Setup

Chairs in one circle, all facing in. No table. Nothing in hands. Have two or three place suggestions in your pocket in case the room offers nothing on the reset. Room lighting normal, no need for anything else. If chairs are fixed, standing in a loose ring works, but keep it still.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Seat everyone facing in. Give the place: a bus station at 5am. State the rules — anyone speaks, one at a time, no going round the circle. Take no questions yet. |
| 1 | Demo with one volunteer. Give them a line about the place. They repeat it exactly, hold three silent beats, then add one sentence. Point out the pause out loud so the room hears it. |
| 1 | Run round one. Let it build offer by offer. Cut it at about ten offers or when the place feels furnished, whichever comes first. |
| 1 | Reset. Ask for a new place in three words or fewer, take the first offer. Restate the rule. Start round two — no second turns until everyone who wants one has spoken. |
| 1 | Finish round two. Stop while it is still working. One sentence of closure from you, then move straight into the next block. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| First speaker rushes past the repeat | *"Say their line back first. Exactly."* |
| Someone skips the pause | *"Three beats. Count them."* |
| An offer arrives with two or three details stacked | *"One thing. Keep the rest."* |
| The room goes quiet and looks anxious | *"The silence is part of it. Somebody will go."* |
| Offers start getting clever or jokey | *"Small and true. Not funny."* |
| Someone alters the line while repeating it | *"Word for word. Their words, not yours."* |
| Round two, the same three people keep speaking | *"Wait for a new voice."* |
| Last thirty seconds of round two | *"Two more offers, then we stop."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is quiet between offers and nobody is scrambling to fill it. Lines come back accurately — you can hear people listening rather than composing. Details are small and concrete: a vending machine, a wet floor sign, one person asleep. The place accumulates. Nobody has tried to be funny and nobody looks like they need to be.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| People repeat the gist instead of the words — 'so there's like a bench' becomes 'there's a bench thing'. | They stopped listening halfway through and started planning their own addition. | Stop the round. Say the line yourself and ask the room to say it back in unison. Restart. It resets the ear in ten seconds. |
| The three beats vanish and the round speeds up into a list. | Group anxiety about silence. Nobody wants the gap to be their fault. | Call 'three beats, count them' and then hold the next pause yourself, visibly, before anyone speaks. Model it once and it usually holds. |
| Two or three confident people carry the whole first round. | No turn structure in round one, which is intentional but tips easily. | Let it be. Round two's one-turn-each rule fixes it. If it is severe, apply the rule in round one too and lose thirty seconds elsewhere. |
| Additions get grand — an explosion, a celebrity, a twist. | People think a good offer means an interesting offer. | Call 'small and true'. Give an example of the size you want: a flickering strip light. Praise the next dull detail out loud. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Round one runs to about eight offers. Round two, everyone gets a turn comfortably inside ninety seconds — hold them to it and expect the circle to complete. |
| **14–17** | One circle still. Round one, cap at ten offers. In round two you will not get all the way round; say so in advance — 'as many as we fit' — so nobody feels skipped. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles at opposite ends of the room after the demo. Give each circle the same place for round one, then let each take its own place for round two. Run both at once, indoor volume. You float between them. Skip the whole-room reset — call the timings from the middle of the room. |

## Variations

**Repeat only** — Drop the addition entirely for the first round. Someone says a line, the next person repeats it word for word, and that is the whole turn. Add the one detail back in round two.  
*easier — strips out the pressure to contribute and puts everything on accurate listening*

**Repeat the last two** — Each speaker repeats the previous two offers in order before adding their own.  
*harder — loads memory and forces attention across a longer span, so nobody can tune out for a turn*

**Add a person, not a thing** — Same shape, but every addition must be somebody who is in the place, described in one sentence. No objects, no weather.  
*different emphasis — moves from set dressing towards character, which feeds the scene work later in the session*

## Debrief

1. **What happened in you during the three beats?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Somebody names the discomfort honestly — 'I wanted to fill it' — and someone else says the pause gave them time to hear the line properly. Move on once both sides are voiced.

2. **How accurate were the repeats, really?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* People admit they paraphrased without meaning to, and connect it to having already decided what to say. If you get one honest 'I wasn't listening, I was writing', that is the point made.

3. **Did the one-detail limit feel restrictive or restful?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* At least one person says it took the pressure off. Do not push for consensus — someone finding it frustrating is fine and useful. Twenty seconds is enough here.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact, no standing, no performing. Everyone stays seated and speaks from their chair, so mobility and stamina are not factors. Speaking is voluntary in both rounds — round two's rule is 'everyone who wants a turn', not 'everyone'. Say that phrase out loud. If someone is uncomfortable speaking, listening and tracking the place is a full participation. For anyone hard of hearing, seat them where they can see faces and ask speakers to keep their heads up rather than talking into their laps.

## Why it works

Offer reception fails because listening and composing use the same attention, and most beginners spend an offer composing. Mandatory verbatim repetition makes that impossible — you cannot say the line back correctly unless you actually took it in. The three beats then separate reception from response in time, so the two tasks stop competing. Capping the addition at one detail removes the incentive to be impressive, which is the other thing that pulls attention off the offer. Together the three constraints enact the cue directly: accept the reality, then add exactly one thing.

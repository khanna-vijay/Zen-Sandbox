# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — One Thing Back

![One Thing Back](../img/beginner_w03__b3_2.jpg){ .infographic }

> Two facing lines hand each other a small fiction and get back exactly one added detail, then shuffle along for a new partner.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 03 run-sheet](../week-03.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 3 opens the door on Yes, And. This is the first thing they do — before any scene, before any longer form. It puts the mechanic in the mouth as a physical drill: take the thing, add one. Everything later in the session (fuller Yes, And exchanges, then two-person scenes) assumes they can already receive an offer without arguing or over-topping. This block builds that reflex in five minutes and hands it over warm.

## What students actually learn

- They stop hearing an offer as a question they have to answer well, and start hearing it as ground they now stand on.
- They feel the difference between adding one thing and dumping four — and notice the second one leaves their partner nothing.
- They discover they can be endowed with something they didn't choose and be fine.
- Their default first word becomes agreement, not negotiation.

## Setup

Clear floor space long enough for two facing lines. No chairs, no props, no paper. Know your numbers before you start: if the count is odd, you are in the line. Have one worked example ready in your head besides the soaking-wet one, in case the demo partner freezes.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Form the two lines. Give the rule in one sentence: hand them a fiction, they take it as true and add one thing. Ban real bodies and real lives. Demo with the person opposite you and stop hard after the one addition. |
| 1 | Round one. Line A endows, Line B accepts and adds. Call "swap" at thirty seconds so Line B endows and Line A adds. Say "just one" over the noise if you hear people running on. |
| 1 | Shuffle Line B one place right, end person to the far end. New partner. Both directions again with a "swap" call halfway. |
| 1 | Shuffle again. Both directions again. Pick the pace up — you want the exchange finished before they've had time to think of something good. |
| 1 | Final shuffle, both directions, then clap the room to a stop. Name the cue out loud — accept the reality, then add exactly one thing — and move straight on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Straight after the demo, as the first round starts | *"Take it as true. First word is yes."* |
| Thirty seconds into any round | *"Swap. Other line endows."* |
| When someone adds three details in one breath | *"One thing. Then stop."* |
| When you hear hesitation before answers | *"Don't pick the good one. Pick the first one."* |
| At each shuffle | *"Step right. End of the line, run round."* |
| When a pair starts playing a scene | *"Not a scene. One exchange, then move."* |
| When endowments get personal | *"Fiction about them, not facts about them."* |
| Final ten seconds | *"Last one. Then hold."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room is loud and overlapping. Exchanges take four or five seconds. People are answering before they've decided what to say and looking slightly surprised at what came out. Shuffles happen fast, with someone jogging to the far end each time. Nobody is standing silent, and no pair has drifted into a two-minute scene. By the fourth shuffle the word "yes" is landing before the endowment has finished.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Long pauses before the reply, faces doing visible work. | They are auditioning their addition for quality rather than just adding. | Call "first thing, not best thing" and cut the round short by five seconds. Speed removes the editing time. |
| Pairs still talking when you call the shuffle. | The exchange has become a scene because nobody defined an end point. | Re-demo in three seconds flat. Endow, add, stop. Say "the silence after is the point" and restart. |
| Endowments about someone's real appearance, accent or job, followed by thin laughter. | People reach for the observable because inventing feels riskier. | Stop the room. Restate: nothing true, nothing about the body. Give two invented examples — "you've just sold the farm", "you've been up since four" — and go again. |
| One line is confidently endowing and the other line is barely audible. | The adding role feels like the exposed one, so quieter people shrink into it. | Swap the lines' roles more often — call "swap" twice inside a round — so nobody sits in the receiving seat long enough to dread it. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One pair of lines, five to six shuffles. You will be in the line if the count is odd. Rounds can run the full time because exchanges cycle fast. |
| **14–17** | One pair of lines still works. Cut to four shuffles — the line is long enough that your "swap" call takes a second to reach the ends, so leave a beat before it. |
| **18–20** | Split into two separate facing-line pairs at opposite ends of the room. Run three shuffles each. Call swaps and shuffles for both at once from the middle; volume goes up, so use a clap instead of your voice for shuffles. |

## Variations

**Given first words** — Everyone must begin their reply with the literal words "Yes, and" before adding. No exceptions, including when it sounds clunky.  
*Easier — removes the choice of how to agree, so all attention goes on the addition.*

**Add and hand back** — After the addition, the original endower adds one more thing on top, then stop. Two additions, still no scene.  
*Harder — they must build on someone else's build, not just their own idea.*

**Silent endowment** — The endower gives the fiction physically only — a mimed action or a look. Partner names what they think it was and adds one thing.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from listening to words to reading physical offers, which feeds later scene work.*

## Debrief

1. **What happened in your body when someone told you something about yourself that wasn't true?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Someone names a specific reflex — wanting to correct it, laughing it off, going blank — and then says that saying yes was easier than expected. You are looking for the noticing, not for a positive report.

2. **Which was harder, handing out the fiction or taking it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Split answers, with reasons attached. Someone says taking it was easier because they didn't have to invent the world. Someone else says the opposite. Move on once two different reasons are in the room.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact. Lines stand at arm's length and stay there. Endowments are invented circumstance only — never about a person's body, accent, background, age, job or anything they've shared with the group. Say that out loud before the demo, not after. Anyone who wants out can stand at the end of a line and shuffle with everyone else without speaking; they still get the rhythm. If someone is endowed with something that lands badly, they can say "pass" and the pair moves to the next shuffle. Say that pass exists before you start. Standing is not required — a chair at the end of the line is fine.

## Why it works

Offer reception fails because the receiver treats the offer as a proposal to be evaluated. The drill removes the evaluation window. Four or five seconds per exchange is not enough time to decide whether you like the fiction, so the only available move is to take it. The cap of exactly one addition does the second half of the job: it stops the receiver from converting agreement into a takeover, which is the commonest disguised block. Constant shuffling means no partner relationship builds up, so nobody can coast on a rapport they've already got — they meet the cue fresh each time. By the fourth partner the sequence accept-then-add has been run twelve times and stops being a decision.

# 🧊 Ice-breaker — Small Hills to Die On

![Small Hills to Die On](../img/beginner_w03__b1_1.jpg){ .infographic }

> Everyone declares one trivial strong opinion and the room votes with thumbs, so personalities land before anyone has to perform.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 1/5` · `Energy medium` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 03 run-sheet](../week-03.md)

## Why this activity, here

First thing in the Yes, And week. Week 2 got them speaking in the room; this gets them stating something and having it received without argument. The thumb vote is the crudest possible acceptance signal — a whole room registering an offer in one second. That physical habit of receiving before responding feeds directly into the Yes, And work that follows.

## What students actually learn

- They find out their own ordinary opinions are interesting to a room, without having to be clever about it.
- They feel what it is like to be received rather than debated — the vote registers, nobody argues.
- They start recognising each other by content rather than by name badge, which lowers the cost of the first scene.

## Setup

No props. Chairs pushed back. Enough floor for a circle of up to twenty with elbow room. Have three or four of your own small hills ready so you never stall. Know the room's names, or have a seating order you can point along.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Circle up. Explain the small hill in two sentences, give two of your own, and pair everyone with the person on their left. Odd number, make one three. |
| 1 | Say go. Simultaneous talking, both partners at once, swapping hills. Call the swap at thirty seconds. Do not lower the volume. |
| 2 | Back in the circle. Clockwise, one hill each, one sentence, roughly seven seconds. Keep the pace hard; cut anyone who starts explaining. |
| 1 | Keep the vote running after every hill — thumbs up or down, instant. Then name three hills you heard and who said them, and move on. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| Setting up, before the first pair talks | *"Trivial. If it could start a real argument, pick something smaller."* |
| Thirty seconds into the pair talk | *"Swap. Other person's turn."* |
| First person in the circle hesitates | *"One sentence. Say it like it's settled."* |
| Someone starts justifying their opinion | *"No defending. Next."* |
| The room is slow to vote | *"Thumbs. Now. Don't think about it."* |
| A big laugh threatens to stop the ring | *"Keep going. Next hill."* |
| Someone says they can't think of one | *"Something you noticed this week. Anything. Take the first one."* |
| Closing | *"Three names, three hills. Remember them for later."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The ring moves without gaps. Hills are small and specific — the state of the office kettle, the correct number of pillows. Thumbs go up and down fast, sometimes split down the middle, and nobody explains their vote. There is noise, but it is the noise of a room recognising each other, and it stops the moment the next person speaks. You finish inside five minutes with the circle still standing.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Someone offers a genuine political or moral position and the room goes quiet. | The word 'opinion' landed without the word 'trivial' sticking to it. | Say clearly: too big, give me a smaller one. Take another from them immediately and keep the ring going. Do not discuss it. |
| People preface with 'this is stupid, but…' or 'I don't really mind, but…'. | Hedging, because they think an opinion needs defending. | Cut the preface out loud: 'Start at the opinion.' Have them say it again without the wrapper. |
| The circle stalls repeatedly while people think. | Your own examples were too clever, so they think theirs must be good. | Give three deliberately dull examples yourself. Tell them the boring ones vote best. Skip anyone stuck and come back at the end. |
| A pair keeps arguing after you call the circle back. | The simultaneous talking turned into a debate. | Say the phase is over and start the ring anyway. Do not negotiate. The clock is five minutes. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | One circle. Give the pair talk the full thirty seconds each way. Circle round takes roughly ninety seconds; use the slack to take a second hill from two or three volunteers. |
| **14–17** | One circle still. Trim the pair talk to twenty seconds each way. Hold the ring to six seconds a person and cut explaining hard, or you will overrun the block. |
| **18–20** | Split into two circles of nine or ten for the ring, each with a nominated starter, running at the same time. Vote within your own circle. You call the swap and the finish for both. Take the closing recall from one circle only. |

## Variations

**Written hills** — Hand out slips before the circle. Everyone writes one hill, then reads it out when their turn comes.  
*Easier — removes the on-the-spot pressure for groups that freeze, at the cost of some energy.*

**Defend in one breath** — After the thumb vote, anyone who got a majority thumbs-down gets one breath, no more, to justify the hill.  
*Harder — adds pressure and a second offer under a clock.*

**Yes, and the hill** — After each hill, the person to the left adds one sentence that accepts it and extends it further.  
*Shifts emphasis from declaring to receiving — a direct rehearsal of the session cue inside the ice-breaker.*

## Debrief

1. **Whose hill do you still remember, and what was it?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Two or three specific hills named with the person attached, offered quickly and without being asked twice. That means faces have attached to content.

2. **What happened in your body when the thumbs went down?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something honest and small — a flinch, then nothing, because nobody argued. If they say it did not matter much, that is the point landing.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact in this activity. Anyone who does not want to speak in the ring can pass with a raised hand and rejoin on the next lap — say this before you start. If a hill lands on something genuinely contested, name it as too big and move on within two seconds; do not let the room debate it. Voting with thumbs is optional: hands stay down if someone would rather not judge. For anyone who cannot stand for five minutes, keep chairs in the circle from the start.

## Why it works

Offer Reception starts with a receiver who does nothing but register. The thumb vote strips response down to one bit of information — received, and here is my reaction — with no route to negotiation or correction. Because the sender cannot defend and the receiver cannot argue, both sides get repetitions of an offer landing intact. That is the mechanical core of the session cue: accept the reality first. The trivial content keeps the stakes low enough that the habit forms before anyone has anything to protect.

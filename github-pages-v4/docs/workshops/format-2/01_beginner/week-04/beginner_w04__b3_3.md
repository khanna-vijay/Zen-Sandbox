# 🔥 Warm-up cluster — Sound Compass

![Sound Compass](../img/beginner_w04__b3_3.jpg){ .infographic }

> With eyes closed, the room tracks moving sounds by pointing at them, so listening becomes a whole-body act rather than a polite one.

`Players 10–20` · `~5 min` · `Complexity 2/5` · `Energy low` · `Contact none` · `Props: none`

⬅️ [Back to the Week 04 run-sheet](../week-04.md)

## Why this activity, here

Week 4 opens the body as a listening instrument. This is the first thing in the room, before anyone has spoken a scene line. It strips listening down to location and volume — no content to be clever about — and hands the rest of the session a group already turned outward. It feeds directly into the partner-listening work that follows, where the same torso-turn becomes physical attention to a scene partner.

## What students actually learn

- That listening has a direction, and their body already knows how to show it.
- That they were guessing where sounds came from, and guessing is not the same as hearing.
- That matching a partner's volume is information, not politeness.
- That the second they start deciding what to do, they stop taking anything in.

## Setup

Clear floor space so people can stand at arm's length from each other with gaps to walk through. No chairs in the middle. Nothing on the floor to trip on. Have the group spread out before you explain anything. You need no props. Know your three source names before you start talking — pick people who look steady on their feet and pick different corners of the room.

## How to run it — 5 minutes

| Min | What you do |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Brief the rules standing. Give the closed-eyes opt-out explicitly. Name three sources aloud, send them to the edges, and tell everyone else to close their eyes. |
| 1 | Round one. Sources make one small repeating sound every few seconds and take two slow steps between sounds. Listeners point their torso at each sound. Call 'open' at the end and let them see where they landed. Reset. |
| 2 | Round two. Same three sources, but each says a single ordinary word instead of a sound. Listeners point and repeat the word back aloud at the volume they heard it. |
| 1 | Call 'open' and stop everything. Say the cue. Do not debrief here — move straight into the next block while the room is still quiet. |

## Side-coaching script

| When | Say |
|---|---|
| As eyes close, before the first sound | *"Feet planted. Only the chest turns."* |
| First few sounds of round one | *"Point where you heard it, not where you think they went."* |
| If sources are rushing | *"Sources — two steps. Then wait."* |
| Mid round one, if the room is stiff | *"Whole torso. Face it like you'd face a person."* |
| Start of round two | *"Same volume back. Don't improve it."* |
| If listeners are echoing loudly over a whisper | *"If it was small, give it back small."* |
| Last ten seconds of round two | *"Last word coming. Take it."* |
| Immediately after 'open' | *"Hold your point. Look. That's the gap."* |

!!! success "What good looks like"
    The room goes quiet without you asking twice. Torsos swing in loose unison, half a beat behind each sound, and split when two sources fire at once. In round two the echoes come back fast and soft, not shouted. When you call 'open' people are pointing at something, and a few laugh at how far off they are. Nobody has taken a step.

## When it goes wrong

| You see | What's causing it | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Listeners drifting forward or sidestepping with their eyes shut. | They are tracking with their feet, which is faster and less accurate than tracking with attention — and it is a collision risk. | Stop the round. Say 'feet stay, chest turns' and restart. Two people who keep drifting can hold a wall with one hand. |
| Sources making sounds constantly, or loud theatrical noises. | They think their job is to be interesting. It is not. | Call over the top: 'Smaller. And wait longer between.' A source who cannot go small becomes a listener next round. |
| In round two, everyone repeating the word at the same flat volume. | They are reproducing the word, not the sound of it. | Say 'match the volume, not the word'. Do one yourself — whisper a word and have the room whisper it back. |
| Nervous laughter and peeking, especially in the first thirty seconds. | Standing with eyes closed among strangers is exposing, and this is week four not week ten. | Name it once, briefly, then keep going. The laughter dies by the second round. Do not stop to reassure — that makes it bigger. |

## Scaling

| Class size | What changes |
|---|---|
| **10–13** | Three sources, one round of sound and one of words, exactly as written. The room is small enough that everyone can hear all three clearly. |
| **14–17** | Four sources instead of three. Spread them to four corners before you start so the sounds arrive from genuinely different directions. Timing unchanged. |
| **18–20** | Five sources, and split the floor: sources work only in the outer ring, listeners stand in the inner cluster. Shorten round one by fifteen seconds so round two still gets its full two minutes. |

## Variations

**One source** — Use a single source for both rounds. Everyone else listens and points.  
*Easier — no competing signals, so beginners get the win of pointing accurately before you make it crowded.*

**Sit down** — Sources sit or crouch as often as they stand, and vary their height between sounds.  
*Harder — vertical position stops being predictable, so listeners have to hear rather than assume.*

**Name and echo** — In round two, sources say a listener's name. That listener alone repeats it back; everyone else points in silence.  
*Different emphasis — shifts from group tracking to being individually addressed and having to answer.*

## Debrief

1. **What happened in your head just before you got one wrong?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* Something like 'I'd already decided where she was going, so I pointed there.' Anyone naming a prediction rather than a mistake has the point. Move on.

2. **Was matching the volume harder than getting the word?**
   > *A good answer sounds like:* 'I heard the word straight away but I gave it back louder.' You want them noticing that they took the content and dropped everything else about how it arrived.

!!! warning "Safety & inclusion"
    No contact at any point — say this to the sources as a rule, not a preference, and repeat it if anyone gets close. Offer the eyes-open alternative before you name the sources, not after, so nobody has to opt out in front of the room: dropping the gaze to the floor works fine. Anyone with balance issues, vertigo or a hearing difference in one ear can stand with a hand on a wall or chair back, or take a source role instead — offer that quietly rather than announcing it. Check the floor before you begin. If someone chooses to sit throughout, they can still point.

## Why it works

Active listening fails in beginners because they treat it as an inward, silent activity while their attention is actually busy composing a reply. This puts the evidence of attention outside the body — you can see whether someone is pointing at the sound. There is nothing to prepare, because a click from an unknown direction cannot be anticipated. The moment a listener predicts where a source is walking, they point wrong and see it when they open their eyes. Round two adds volume-matching, which is the first step towards taking in *how* something was said rather than only what. The cue lands at the end rather than the start because by then they have felt it happen instead of being told.
